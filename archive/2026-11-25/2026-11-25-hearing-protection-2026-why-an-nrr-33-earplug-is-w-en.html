Hearing Protection: Four Readings of One NRR Number

earplughearing-protectionNRR

Everyone who buys a first pair of hearing protectors hits the same question: can the number on the box be subtracted straight from the noise level in the room.

No, and for more than one reason. The same foam plug labelled NRR 33 comes out as 26 under OSHA's arithmetic, 13 after NIOSH derating, and as a curve running from 34.8 to 43.7 dB in the manufacturer's own nine-row attenuation table. All three numbers sit in official documents. None of them is invented.

I pulled the official datasheets for six earplugs and earmuffs sold on Amazon, took the labels apart down to the octave bands, and sorted the results into six branches. Start with the number you actually need

CDC and NIOSH are blunt about this. Most workplace exposures sit below 95 dBA, "most workers need 10 dB or less sound reduction to bring their exposure down to a safe level," and "Almost any hearing protector, when fit correctly, can reduce noise by 10 dB." NIOSH then gets specific: aim to bring protected exposure to 75–85 dBA, and it uses the phrase "Avoid overprotection." Cut too much and you stop hearing your name being called or a machine changing pitch, so you take the protector off, and protection drops to zero.

That inverts the whole buying decision. If the target is "just enough," then the 2 dB between NRR 33 and NRR 31 is not a decision input at a desk. Three things decide it instead: what the room actually measures, whether you will keep the thing on, and which scale the label is even using.

Three thresholds worth memorising:

85 dBA — NIOSH's recommended exposure limit, and OSHA's action level; cross it and a hearing conservation programme is required.

90 dBA — OSHA's permissible exposure limit, as an 8-hour time-weighted average.

100 dBA — NIOSH says double protection, earmuffs over earplugs, at this level or with impulse noise.

One more line that gets skipped: NIOSH states on the same page that active noise cancellation "should not be considered hearing protection unless labeled with an NRR." ANC headphones fight low-frequency hum; that is not the same as an ANSI-tested attenuation rating, and without an NRR on the box they are not protectors in the regulatory sense.

One label, three numbers

Moldex's Pura-Fit datasheet is unusually complete: tested to ANSI S3.19-1974, NRR 33, CSA Class AL, followed by mean attenuation and standard deviation for nine bands from 125 Hz to 8 kHz. That table is what makes it possible to open up the number 33.

Start with OSHA. 29 CFR 1910.95 Appendix B gives two routes depending on your instrument:

  • With a dosimeter that measures C-weighting: subtract the NRR from the C-weighted TWA, with no 7 dB adjustment.
  • With a dosimeter that cannot do C-weighting, or with an A-weighted sound level meter: "Subtract 7 dB from the NRR" first, then subtract the remainder from the A-weighted TWA.

In the same appendix the 7 dB appears only on the A-weighted route. It is not a discount, it is the C-to-A conversion. Going from 33 to 26 is not a 20 percent haircut, it is a change of weighting network.

Then NIOSH. The 1998 criteria derate again by protector type: 25 percent for earmuffs, 50 percent for formable foam plugs, 70 percent for everything else. The 33 dB foam plug lands at (33−7)×0.5 = 13 dB.

The last row matters. 13 dB sits far below every band floor, which tells you 13 is not "the attenuation you get at some frequency" — it is a scalar built for compliance decisions. Equally, 34.8 to 43.7 is not "what you are guaranteed"; it is the lab figure pushed down by one standard deviation per band. Each number answers a different question, and mixing them produces the popular but wrong conclusion that a 33 dB plug is "really only worth 13." OSHA never claimed 13 dB was what happens inside your ear canal.

NRR and SNR are not the same ruler

3M spells the difference out on its own Chinese product page: "NRR: tested per ANSI 3.19-1974. EN SNR: tested per EN 352; octave-band and HML results are in the test report or manual."

The same X5A earmuff carries three different numbers on three official pages: NRR 31 dB on the US product page; EN SNR 37 dB and GB SNR 36 dB on the Chinese page; and "Attenuation Rating (SNR) 31 Decibel" in the spec table of the Hong Kong page.

All three were written by the same company. This is not necessarily a transcription error — it is the SNR field being populated from different test regimes on different regional sites, while the buyer sees one three-letter abbreviation. For anyone writing a buying guide it is the easiest place to crash: copy the NRR from site A and the SNR from site B, put both in one table, and start subtracting.

The practical consequence: there is no fixed conversion between NRR and SNR. On the X5A, NRR 31 against EN SNR 37 is a 6 dB gap, so the folk rule "SNR minus 3 equals NRR" fails on this product. Loop publishes SNR only — Switch 2 is 20, 23 and 26 dB across its three settings — so subtracting 26 from Moldex's 33 is like subtracting Celsius from Fahrenheit.

The low point is 2 kHz at 34.8; the high point is 4 kHz at 43.7. That is an 8.9 dB spread.

This is not measurement noise, it is the physics of the device: low frequencies are blocked by mass and seal, high frequencies by material absorption, and the middle is where every plug is merely adequate. 2 kHz happens to sit in the region that carries a lot of speech intelligibility, so a plug rated NRR 33 is nearly 9 dB weaker at blocking voices than it is at 4 kHz.

A single number necessarily throws that away. The point of reading the label is to know what was thrown away, not to expect the label to contain everything.

The decision tree

Branch 1: you have not measured anything. Measure. CDC points to NIOSH's sound level meter app for a first pass, and you want the stretch of time you actually spend there, not a second in the corridor. Do not skip this.

Branch 2: below 85 dBA (open office, commute, home). You need about 10 dB, not 26. Loop Switch 2 on Engage (20 dB SNR) already sits at the top of that range; the Quiet setting is for cabins, subway cars and the renovation next door. The classic mistake in this branch is buying NRR 33 foam for an office, then pulling it out because you cannot hear a colleague.

Branch 3: 85–100 dBA (vacuum, cutting, a long print run, a shop floor). You need 15–25 dB. Two equivalent routes: disposable foam at NRR 33 that you forget about, or earmuffs around NRR 30 that come off in a second. Pick by how often you take them off — frequent removal favours muffs, two-hour stretches favour plugs.

Branch 4: 100 dBA and up, or impulse noise (air hammer, hammering, gunshots). NIOSH says double protection, muffs over plugs. The counterintuitive part: the two layers do not add. Training material commonly estimates the pair as the higher protector's NRR plus 5 dB, not 30 plus 33. Double protection buys a ceiling, not a doubled number.

Branch 5: intermittent noise, and you need to hear speech. Electronic muffs that clamp the impulse and pass voices, or a plug with a mechanical dial. The precondition is an NRR on the box; NIOSH explicitly does not count ANC headphones without one.

Branch 6: glasses, all-day wear, or side sleeping. Plugs first. CDC is direct about this: eyewear, even ordinary glasses, can break the seal of an earmuff and let sound leak in. Custom moulded plugs (Decibullz) beat a higher-NRR muff here because they sidestep the seal being pried open.

Fallback: you cannot judge any of it. Buy a headband muff rated around NRR 30. Fastest to put on, no insertion technique required, cheapest mistake — then switch form factor once you have measured.

How to check the fit afterwards

Labels and arithmetic are ceilings; the real attenuation happens in your ear canal. CDC offers a free check: slowly cup and uncup your hands over your ears while counting aloud — if your own voice sounds about the same either way, the plug is sealed.

Where it is available, 3M's E-A-Rfit dual-ear system measures a personal attenuation rating; both the X5A and Optime 105 pages list compatibility. That matches NIOSH's current position too: the 1998 derating factors are described as a previous recommendation, and individual fit testing is now the better check.

One last warning comes from the warning block of the Moldex datasheet, item 3: overprotection is dangerous, and the wearer must be able to hear warning signals. OSHA's appendix closes with the same caveat from the other direction — calculated attenuation reflects reality only to the extent the protector is properly fitted and worn.

FAQ

Is NRR 33 worth paying more for than NRR 31?

Not below 85 dBA, where both are far past what you need. Not above 100 dBA either, where the answer is double protection and 2 dB will not save you. What is worth comparing is form factor: plugs or muffs, and whether you will keep them on.

Can I convert an SNR rating into NRR?

There is no general conversion. The same X5A carries NRR 31 and EN SNR 37 from its own maker, a 6 dB gap, so the "subtract 3" rule fails there. Treat an SNR-only product as belonging to another system and do not put it in the same subtraction.

Do noise-cancelling headphones count as hearing protection?

NIOSH is explicit: ANC reduces noise, but unless the product is labelled with an NRR it should not be considered hearing protection. ANC targets steady low-frequency noise and does almost nothing to an impulse.

Do earplugs plus earmuffs add up?

No. NIOSH recommends double protection at 100 dBA and above, but the combination is far below the sum; training material typically estimates it as the higher NRR plus 5 dB. It buys a ceiling, not a doubled figure.

What goes wrong if I wear NRR 33 foam in an office?

NIOSH warns about overprotection directly: cut too much and you stop hearing colleagues and equipment, you remove the plug, and real-world protection falls to zero. At office levels, about 10 dB is the target.

Last

I did not run sound pressure measurements or a personal fit test for this piece — those need a person and hardware, and no amount of datasheet reading replaces them. What I can stand behind: the label has four readings, NRR and SNR have no fixed conversion, the same manufacturer publishes different numbers for the same product on different regional pages, and no NRR is high enough to compensate for a protector you will not wear.

So the order is reversed from how most people shop: measure the room, decide how many dB you need, and only then read the label. Buying the wrong kind of protection costs more than buying the wrong model.
