KVM Switch Buying Guide: 8K Claims, EDID, USB Power

kvm switchdesktop setupbuying guide

Two machines, one keyboard, one mouse, two monitors. Most people end up with that arrangement because of a single cable: the work laptop has to reach the 4K screen at home, the jump host has to sit next to the local terminal, the bench machine has to be swapped with the test box a dozen times a day.

A KVM switch is the box that swallows the cables. They start around sixty dollars and go past two hundred and sixty, and roughly half of that spread buys a line of text that is not in the headline.

This article does one thing: lays out what five dual-monitor KVM listings actually say, splits the claims into three channels — video, USB, EDID — and then walks each unit to the point where it breaks. Four rows, and that is the whole spec sheet

After reading five listings, the fields you can actually compare come down to four. Everything else is marketing copy.

  • Headline resolution: 4K@60Hz, 8K@60Hz, 4K@120Hz, printed in the title.
  • Port generation: HDMI 2.0, HDMI 2.1, DisplayPort 1.4, buried in a bullet or a comparison table.
  • The EDID row: supported, not supported, or not mentioned at all.
  • USB ports and power: how many, what speed, and whether the box needs its own adapter.

A fifth field is the switching method — hotkey, button, remote, desktop controller. It carries no bandwidth, but it decides how many times a day you reach across the desk.

Of those four rows, the first is the one that misleads. Because it keeps contradicting the second, often on the same page.

The shared premise: every computer runs its own cable pair into the KVM (dual HDMI, or DP plus HDMI). None of these is a single-cable USB-C dock. That wiring requirement repeats in every product section below.

Channel one: the 8K label next to its own HDMI 2.0

Do the arithmetic first, then decide who is being vague.

An 8K frame at 4:4:4 and 8 bit is 7680 × 4320 = 33,177,600 pixels. At 60 Hz that is 1.99 billion pixels per second, times 24 bits, which comes to 47.8 Gbps of payload. HDMI.org, on its current specification page, puts the Ultra High Speed cable class at 48 Gbps and notes the 16b/18b coding — 47.78 × 18 ÷ 16 ≈ 53.8 Gbps of line rate, past the 48 Gbps ceiling.

Put plainly: 8K@60Hz at 4:4:4 and 8 bit does not fit even in the 48 Gbps tier. HDMI.org says the same thing from the other direction: uncompressed 8K@60/4:4:4 at 10 and 12 bit is listed as an HDMI 2.2 capability, the 96 Gbps generation. DisplayPort agrees — VESA's FAQ states that DisplayPort 1.4a reaches 8K@60Hz at 24-bit color through 2:1 DSC, and 30-bit through 2.5:1.

So how does 8K@60Hz exist on a cheap box? Chroma subsampling. 8K@60 at 4:2:0 and 8 bit is 23.9 Gbps of payload, about 26.9 Gbps of line rate, which fits inside 48 Gbps. The label is not a lie. The reader's assumption — that it means 4:4:4 — is.

Now look at how two listings write it.

The UGREEN 8K model's second bullet begins "resolution up to 8K@60Hz, backward compatible with 8K@30Hz, 4K@240/144/120/60/30Hz" and ends, verbatim, "It also support HDMI 2.0, HDCP2.2/1.4, 3D, and HDR10+." 8K@60Hz and HDMI 2.0, in one bullet. HDMI 2.0 has no DSC and no 48 Gbps tier; it cannot even carry 4K@120 (23.9 Gbps of payload, roughly 29.9 Gbps of line rate). The same bullet lists 4K@240 as compatible — that is a DisplayPort 1.4 with DSC mode, not something HDMI can deliver.

ATLAHET's four-port unit is blunter. The title says "8K 60Hz 4K 120Hz". The comparison table on the same page says, under inputs, "Per PC: 2×HDMI2.0", and under maximum resolution, "4K 120Hz". Two descriptions of one box. Its own bullet adds that reaching 8K requires the computer, the monitor and the cable to support 8K — true, but it omits the part where an HDMI 2.0 GPU closes that door before you start.

What this channel concludes: the 8K label is real, but it is a subsampled 8K that needs three things to line up. Any listing that says HDMI 2.0 should not be trusted with 8K@60Hz or 4K@120Hz as obtainable modes. One evidence boundary: I could not find an official figure for HDMI 2.0's own bandwidth, because that generation's page is gone from HDMI.org — only 2.2 and 1.4b remain — so this article uses the published 48 Gbps as its reference and quotes no second-hand number.

Channel two: the EDID row, ticked versus crossed

EDID is the table a monitor hands the source: which modes it supports, which refresh rate is preferred, how wide its gamut is. The moment a KVM moves the signal from machine A to machine B, machine A sees a monitor that has disappeared.

The listings describe that in two completely different tones.

TESmart writes that every input port has an EDID emulator, so the computer always receives the correct display information and the display settings do not change when you switch inputs. ATLAHET puts it in user language: the built-in EDID emulation recognises and stores the monitor's parameters, so the system finds the monitor quickly after a switch and the windows on the display are not rearranged each time you switch.

UGREEN writes the opposite. The 4K60 model's comparison table has a row labelled "EDID Emulation" with a cross in the right-hand column, and a bullet that reads "EDID emulation is not supported". The 8K model puts "Not support EDID emulation" as note 4 at the bottom of the page. Same term, one tick and one cross, and in between sits the difference between a window layout that survives a switch and one that does not.

One thing has to be said clearly: the rearranging-windows consequence is the vendors' own description of what emulation buys, not something I measured. These five boxes never touched my desk, so this article does not claim "I switched and the windows jumped". What can be said from first principles is narrower: without emulation, the source loses the monitor's self-description during the switch and the GPU treats the screen as unplugged. What the operating system then does depends on the OS and the driver. Check that on the product page, or simply buy from the tier that has emulation.

Worth noting that EDID shows up elsewhere on this site. In the DisplayLink piece from October 20, the resolutions you can pick are decided jointly by the monitor's EDID and the chip's capability, not by the box. Same field, two jobs: a resolution ceiling in a dock, a window layout in a KVM.

Channel three: why four USB 3.0 ports still need a wall adapter

Four of the five listings hide an almost identical sentence.

Both UGREEN models say that to keep USB devices working you must connect the supplied power adapter. ATLAHET says to connect a 12V1A external supply while in use, and says it twice — once in the USB bullet, once in the compatibility bullet. TESmart specifies 12V 2A.

That line reads like boilerplate, but it answers a practical question: where the current for those four USB 3.0 ports comes from. The upstream USB cable carries data, 5 Gbps of it. Four downstream ports driving an SSD, a USB Ethernet adapter and a webcam draw their power from the box's own adapter. Which is why these units have a reputation for "keyboard and mouse work, the drive never shows up" when the adapter is left in the drawer — though that symptom is my inference from the power requirement, not a measurement.

There is a bandwidth layer too. UGREEN lists four ports as three USB-A plus one USB-C, all sharing 5 Gbps. TESmart gives two USB 3.0 ports plus a front port that can charge a phone. AOOCOO gives three USB 3.0, one USB-C and one PD port. Shared means shared: an external SSD and a 2.5-inch spinning drive plugged in together split one 5 Gbps uplink, they do not each get 5 Gbps.

The PD row deserves its own look. AOOCOO advertises 100W PD and then states, in the same bullet, that the laptop actually receives 73W to 85W because the KVM and the cable lose some. The vendor supplied the number, so you do not have to guess. The same pattern showed up in the multiport USB-C charger piece from November 25: the headline wattage is a ceiling, the allocation table is the promise.

FAQ

Q: Can a dual-monitor KVM put one machine on the left screen and the other on the right?

Depends on the model. TESmart explicitly supports that layout, one screen per machine. Both UGREEN listings explicitly say no — the two screens always carry the same computer. ATLAHET mentions copy and extend modes, but with four inputs belonging to four computers, both screens follow whichever one is selected. Treat this as a hard requirement before buying.

Q: The title says 8K@60Hz. What do I actually need?

The vendors agree on three conditions: an 8K@60-capable computer, an 8K@60-capable monitor, and an 8K-capable cable. Add one they do not print but the arithmetic gives you: 8K@60 at 4:4:4 and 8 bit is 47.8 Gbps of payload, about 53.8 Gbps once coded, past the 48 Gbps tier, so what runs is 4:2:0 or a compressed mode. If your GPU is HDMI 2.0 generation, the question is already closed on your side.

Q: What does EDID emulation actually fix?

It keeps answering for the monitor after the KVM switches away, so the source does not conclude the display was unplugged. The two vendors phrase the payoff differently — "display settings do not change when you switch inputs" versus "the windows are not rearranged each time you switch". Note that both are vendor descriptions, not third-party measurements. Whether a unit without emulation triggers a re-layout depends on the OS and driver, so treat "I need this" as a reason to buy from the emulation tier.

Q: Why do four USB 3.0 ports still need a separate power supply?

Because there is only one upstream USB cable. UGREEN, ATLAHET and TESmart all require a 12V adapter in the page copy, and AOOCOO ships one. The current budget for four downstream ports comes from the adapter, not from the upstream cable. The four ports also share one 5 Gbps uplink, so two external drives divide that bandwidth rather than each getting 5 Gbps.

Q: Can a single-cable USB-C KVM replace a dual-HDMI one?

Not equivalently. AOOCOO-style boxes are one PC plus one laptop, mixing USB-C over MST, DP and HDMI on the input side, with two HDMI outputs. A dual-HDMI KVM reserves two video paths per computer. The first saves cables, the second saves steps. The first also requires a video-capable C port, and macOS extension mode is excluded by the vendor.

Closing

The difference between these five comes down to one sentence: only the resolution makes it into the title, while EDID and hotkeys decide how the thing feels every day.

So run the checklist backwards. Count machines and screens, confirm the port generation, then hunt for the EDID row — a cross means do not expect your window layout to survive — and finally check whether the USB ports need their own adapter. Leave the 8K number for last: it is the loudest claim and the one most likely to fail on your own GPU.
