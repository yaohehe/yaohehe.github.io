ESD Mats: What the Three Resistance Numbers Actually Govern

ESD matANSI/ESD S20.20workbench

Search for an anti-static mat and you will almost always find one number on the listing: 10^6 to 10^9 ohms. It reads like a safe band. You lay the mat down, run the ground cord to the screw on an outlet cover, and consider the job finished.

The standard does not check that number. The worksurface clause in ANSI/ESD S20.20 judges the resistance of the path from the surface to ground (Rtg), and it sets only an upper limit. The 10^6 figure is a manufacturer recommendation, not a requirement. And 1 MΩ has nothing to do with static control at all — it is a current-limiting resistor for human safety.

I actually copied four Desco technical bulletins, the ESD Association's public certification newsletter and eight live Amazon listings side by side. The four numbers come from four different documents, and reading them as one number is how people buy the wrong mat.

TL;DR: The standard enforces exactly one limit — resistance from worksurface to ground must be below 1×10^9 ohms. The 10^6 figure is only a recommended lower bound, and the reason given is charged device model (CDM) damage from discharging too fast. Three conclusions you can use immediately: (1) if a listing says "surface resistivity 10^9–10^10 Ω", that order of magnitude already sits on the limit, and it is a resistivity figure rather than the resistance-to-ground the standard asks for — the two are not interchangeable; (2) two mats rated 9.5×10^8 in series read 1.9×10^9, which is 1.9 times the limit, which is exactly why the official bulletin says do not daisy chain; (3) the 1 MΩ resistor protects people from shock, not parts from static, and Desco's European bulletin says so in plain words: "the resistor is not for ESD control". If you want the short list: ground the person with the SCS ECWS61M-1 wrist strap (the listing states a 1 megohm resistor and S1.1 compliance), and ground the mat with the Alphastat grounding kit (1 MΩ buried in the cord, one ground point).

The standard measures the path to ground, not the material

Desco's catalog states the worksurface requirement without hedging: resistance to ground must be less than 1.0×10^9 ohms, per ANSI/ESD S20.20 Table 3. The next sentence in the same paragraph recommends a lower limit of 1.0×10^6 ohms Rtg "to reduce the possibility of a Charged Device Model (CDM) events." SCS publishes the same sentence with softer wording: a lower limit of 1.0×10^6 ohms "if CDM failures are of concern."

Those two sentences do not carry the same weight. The first is the pass/fail criterion. The second is a design recommendation. Buyers routinely read them as one rule.

The criterion also reaches further than most people expect. The ESD Association's facility certification newsletter spent a section clarifying what counts as a work surface: any surface on which an unprotected ESD-sensitive device is placed, including conveyors and production aids — and including packaging. The newsletter's example is a circuit board sitting on a static-shielding bag. In that moment the bag becomes a work surface, and resistance to ground from the bag's top surface must be below 1×10^9 ohms.

Applied to a desk, that has a direct consequence. You lay down a mat, then put an ungrounded shielding bag, a piece of foam and a plastic tray on top of it. None of those become compliant by proximity. What gets measured is each object's own path to ground.

The third row is Desco Europe's wording in bulletin TB-7505: a conductive working surface is defined as material with a surface resistance below 1×10^4 ohms; a dissipative surface is "at least 1×10^4, but less than 1×10^9 ohms." The same bulletin gives the reasoning — conductive material grounds a charge fastest, but it can also damage parts by discharging too rapidly, so conductive materials are usually floor mats, while bench tops should be dissipative.

The fourth row deserves its own section, because it is the one most often mistaken for an ESD specification.

10^9 is a limit, 10^6 is advice: what 5% headroom means

Bertech's published specification for its three-layer vinyl mat (3059 series) lists surface resistivity of 3.0×10^7 to 9.5×10^8 ohms/sq, measured at 10 V, service temperature -20 °F to +160 °F, thickness 0.094 inch.

Put that next to the limit: 9.5×10^8 ÷ 1.0×10^9 = 0.95. The top of the published range leaves 5% of headroom against the red line.

Five percent is not headroom in a real lab. The same Desco catalog expects the measurement to record temperature and relative humidity — the 19787 meter's full mode reads temperature first, then RH, then resistance — which is an admission that both ambient values move the reading. Cleaner residue, surface wear and contact resistance in the ground cord can each consume 5%.

That is also the real job of the 10^6 recommendation: it pulls the design target from "hugging 10^9" toward the middle of the band. A mat specified in the 10^7 range can drift tenfold and stay legal. One specified at 9.5×10^8 goes out of spec at a 5% drift.

The listings that say 10^9–10^10

The cheapest category of "anti-static desk mat" on Amazon publishes exactly that figure.

Bertech's general-purpose table mat roll (B00O0Q0S8O) is a single-layer homogeneous polymer, blue, 0.094 inch thick, 900 psi tensile. The spec line reads: "Surface Resistivity (10e9 - 10e10 ohms)," and the listing states plainly that wrist straps and ground cords are sold separately. Its positioning sentence is "designed to provide a static work surface."

A 2×3 ft kit (B07YJBGX2D) uses nitrile rubber, 0.12 inch thick, ships with a wrist strap and a 10 ft ground cord, and publishes the same 10e9 - 10e10 ohms. It was $49.45 at the time of writing, with 8 left in stock.

In the 10^9 to 10^10 band, the bottom of the range  the limit, and everything above it is out of spec. There is a second problem that matters more: both listings quote surface resistivity in ohms per square, while the standard judges resistance to ground in ohms. Resistivity describes the material; resistance to ground describes a system made of material, snap, cord and contact surfaces. So the number both straddles the limit and cannot substitute for the measurement the standard asks for. It fails on both counts.

The practical reading is not complicated. Single-layer general-purpose rolls are fine for covering a desk, keeping dust off and padding tools. Treating one as an EPA worksurface is a stretch — especially the version without a ground cord, where the path to ground has not been connected at all.

The "conductive layer" in a two-layer mat may not be conductive

One technical note in Desco's catalog explains how multi-layer mats work: a conductive bottom layer pulls the resistance to ground down, and Desco defines that layer as "less than 1.0×10^6 ohms."

Measure a popular rubber roll against that ruler (B0D151R2HZ): 118 × 23.7 inches, 0.08 inch thick, two layers, with the spec line reading top layer 2×10^6 to 10^8 ohms and bottom layer 8×10^6 to 10^8 ohms. It was $53.99 with 20 left in stock.

The bottom layer is specified at 8×10^6, which is eight times above Desco's threshold for a conductive layer. It lands in the dissipative band, not the conductive one. The listing calls it a black conductive bottom layer; by definition it is a dissipative rubber layer. It still does useful work — stacked under a dissipative top it can pull overall Rtg inside the limit — but it will not drag resistance to ground below 10^6 and leave margin for the layer above.

On a bench, that difference shows up as "the reading sits near the limit" versus "the reading sits mid-band." It is visible only in the parenthetical at the end of the spec block.

Two practical costs come with it. A 0.08 inch (about 2 mm) soft mat does not ship with a snap, so you install the ground point yourself; Desco Europe's procedure is to punch a 5 mm hole and press the snap through. And roll stock arrives curled — the official guidance is to let it lie flat at room temperature for about four hours before installing.

The 1 MΩ resistor protects you, not the parts

This is the correction I most want to make.

Desco Europe's bulletin says it in the selection section: whether a worksurface or floor mat ground cord carries a 1 MΩ current-limiting resistor "is the user's preference," and "the resistor is not for ESD control." Desco's US bulletin TB-2003 goes further: EOS/ESD S6.1 recommends a hard ground, without a resistor, for worksurfaces and floor mats.

So what is the 1 MΩ for? People. UL requires personnel grounding devices — wrist straps, footwear — to have at least 1 MΩ in series, placed as close to the body as practical. Desco works the arithmetic on its own Q&A page: with AC not exceeding 250 V and 1 MΩ in series, current is capped at 0.25 mA, which is the level UL treats as safe; at 120 V it is 0.12 mA.

The same 1 MΩ therefore means two different things depending on where it sits. On a wrist strap it is a safety device. On a mat cord it is optional, and adding it changes your Rtg reading from "under 1 ohm" to "1 MΩ ±20%."

Desco is inconsistent with itself in two places worth recording.

First, the acceptance reading for a hard-ground cord. Desco's US bulletin TB-2000 says "less than 1 ohm" without a resistor; Desco Europe's TB-7505 says "less than 10 ohms." Same company, same product family, tenfold difference.

Second, and more consequential: iFixit's portable anti-static mat (B01BLPBOS4) publishes unusually precise figures — surface resistivity 10^7 to 10^9 ohms, 10 mm grounding snap, 1.8 m grounding cord at 10^5 ohms, 60 × 60 cm, 1.8 m wrist strap at 50 ohms. It was $34.95, in stock.

10^5 ohms is neither the 1 ohm hard ground nor the 1 MΩ limit. It is a self-invented value between them. If that cord is the mat's whole path to ground and someone simultaneously touches a live conductor and the mat, 120 V would push 1.2 mA through that path — 4.8 times the 0.25 mA that Desco reads out of UL's criterion.

The 50 ohm wrist strap figure is blunter still. The number in that position under S1.1 is 1 MΩ; 50 ohms is off by a factor of twenty thousand. A 50 ohm loop will still bleed charge and will look fine on an ESD meter, but it is not a current limiter. The listing does not say whether a resistor sits elsewhere in the strap, so a buyer cannot tell from the spec block — which is itself the defect: a safety parameter published as if it were a conductivity parameter.

One product goes further. A carbon-infused silicone mat (B0HG4N5T6S, $22.99) advertises "No Extra ESD Wrist Strap Required" and ships an alligator-clip ground wire. That claim fails twice. Grounding the mat grounds the mat, not the person, and S20.20 still requires a wrist strap for a seated operator. And Desco's bulletins say alligator clips are not recommended; ESD handbook TR20.20 says in as many words not to clip a wrist strap to the edge of a dissipative mat. The listing also publishes no surface resistance figure at all, only that conductive carbon is blended throughout.

The test voltage changes by itself

When you measure the mat yourself, the easiest thing to get wrong is the voltage.

Desco's manual for the 19787 surface resistance meter (TB-3062) states: material at 9×10^5 ohms or less should be measured at 10 V; material at 1×10^6 ohms or greater should be tested at 100 V. In auto mode the meter tries 10 V first and switches to 100 V once the reading reaches 10^6. A measurement per ANSI/ESD S4.1 also requires 15 seconds of electrification, which is what the full mode's 15-second cycle is for.

Now compare Bertech's 3059 spec line: 3.0×10^7 to 9.5×10^8, condition labeled "at 10 V." By the rule above, anything at 10^7 and above should be tested at 100 V. A number taken at 10 V and a number taken at 100 V will not match. Nobody is lying; the two parties used different conditions, and the listing copied the value without the condition.

Electrode placement is specified in the same manual: keep the two 5 lb electrodes at least 2 inches from the edge of the surface and at least 3 inches from any groundable point; space them about 10 inches apart for point-to-point on a worksurface, and about 3 feet apart for a floor. Place them wrong and the reading moves by an order of magnitude.

This is why resistance to ground, point-to-point resistance and surface resistivity can all exist for one product and all differ. You only need to remember which one you want: for S20.20 compliance, it is resistance to ground.

Eight products, one order of decisions

Do not pick a mat first. Answer four questions.

First: will you solder on it? If yes, rule out vinyl. The three material limits differ by a factor of seven: Bertech's three-layer vinyl is rated -20 to 160 °F (about 71 °C), a rubber soldering mat 250 °F (about 121 °C), and silicone mats advertise 932 °F (500 °C). Vinyl's ceiling sits 50 °C below rubber's and well under an iron tip or molten solder.

Second: how is the person grounded? Seated work means a wrist strap, with total system resistance under 3.5×10^7 ohms (35 MΩ). Work that involves walking means a footwear-and-floor system, under 1×10^9 ohms with body voltage under 100 V. These are two parallel options, not a stack.

Third: how big is the mat? One ground point per mat; long rolls are often specified at one per 10 feet — that is Alphastat's listing wording, while Desco's bulletin only says one groundable point per working surface. The two vendors differ on long rolls.

Fourth: who measures it afterwards? Without a meter you are trusting the label. There is no substitute for this step.

With that order, six of the eight sort themselves out directly; the other two, the iFixit pad and the carbon silicone mat, were already taken apart in the 1 MΩ section.

For grounding the person, SCS ECWS61M-1 is the strap in this list that puts both "1 megohm resistor" and "S20.20 / S1.1 compliance" in its spec block, with a 4 mm snap and a 6 ft coil cord. Its defects: the 4 mm snap is the only interface it offers, so it does not mate with the common 10 mm mat ground point and needs an adapter; and there was no buy box at fetch time, so price and stock must be confirmed on the order page.

Mats sort by temperature. B0FKZYP91W takes the silicone route, advertises 932 °F (500 °C), costs $16.23, and publishes no surface resistance figure and no ground-point detail at all. B00O0Q0S8O is the single-layer general-purpose roll at 10^9 to 10^10 ohms: cheapest and thinnest, but it includes neither cord nor strap and its order of magnitude sits on the limit.

If budget is tight and you want one box, the B07YJBGX2D kit includes mat, wrist strap and 10 ft ground cord for $49.45. Its defects are the same 10^9 to 10^10 order of magnitude, plus 0.12 inch of thickness that raises the bench surface and catches tools at the edge.

For the last link in the chain, B089NZ48MQ is the least fuss: $11.99, a universal snap that fits material up to 0.375 inch, 1 MΩ buried in the cord, and listing text stating one ground point per mat or per 10 feet. Its defects: it supplies exactly one ground point, so multiple mats need multiple kits; and that 1 MΩ is a safety part that turns your Rtg reading into a 1 MΩ-scale number, so acceptance has to be judged on the resistorized criterion.

Four checks you can run after installation

One: count the ground points. One per working surface. Two is not extra safety — the two points can form a loop. Desco's grounding diagram is labeled "do not hook a second surface or device here."

Two: look for daisy chaining. TB-7505's stated reason is that many protective surfaces already carry high resistance, and wiring them in series pushes total resistance past the limit. Run the arithmetic: two mats at 9.5×10^8 in series read 1.9×10^9, already 1.9 times the limit. The chain limit is 1.05 mats.

Three: check where the wrist strap lands. Its cord must go to a groundable point or an equipotential bonding point, not clipped to the edge of a dissipative mat. ESD handbook TR20.20 section 5.3.2.7 says not to clip a wrist strap to the edge of a dissipative mat, because the strap cord's resistance and the mat's ground path add up and the total can exceed the 35 MΩ ceiling.

The precision screwdriver guide covers the tooling side of the same bench and already notes that a strap only counts once it is grounded; this article is about where it lands.

Four: read the cleaner label. Desco's bulletin names two things that quietly break a mat: silicone-based cleaners leave an insulative residue on the surface, and solvents such as 2-butoxyethanol or ethanolamine dry the material out. Either one lets a compliant mat measure non-compliant.

Do all four and the bench stops being "a mat I bought" and becomes a grounding chain you can actually measure. FAQ

Q: Is lower always better inside the 10^6 to 10^9 band?

A: No. 10^6 is the recommended lower bound, and the reason is that dumping charge too fast damages parts (CDM). 10^9 is the ceiling, above which charge will not bleed away. The middle of the band, 10^7 to 10^8, is where the margin lives; hugging either end is a bad trade.

Q: If the mat is grounded, do I still need a wrist strap?

A: Yes. The mat grounds objects; the strap grounds the person. S20.20 expects a seated operator to be connected through a wrist strap system, and neither substitutes for the other. The "no wrist strap required" line on carbon silicone mats merges two different jobs into one.

Q: There is no grounding bus at home. Can I use the outlet cover screw?

A: Yes, provided that screw is actually grounded. Desco's installation guide suggests exactly that — the center screw of a standard AC outlet — but requires testing it first, because corrosion and wear can take the ground away over time. Do not drive a separate earth rod: TB-2007 explains that a separate earth ground creates a potential difference against equipment ground, which turns into an overstress hazard when a grounded soldering iron sits on the same bench.

Q: What is the difference between a two-layer and a three-layer mat?

A: It is how Rtg gets pulled down. A multi-layer mat uses a conductive bottom layer (Desco's definition: under 1×10^6 ohms) to lower the resistance from the top surface to the ground point. A single-layer mat has only the material's own resistance. So even when both are "dissipative," the multi-layer mat usually reads lower and drifts less.

Q: How often should I test?

A: Desco's example interval is every six months, alongside its instruction to test all grounds and wrist straps frequently. Wrist straps are worn and abraded daily, so the working practice is a daily check before the shift. Mats can go quarterly, but must be retested after a move, a cleaning or a cord swap.

Scope and data notes

I have no lab here and ran no chamber-controlled comparison. Every resistance value above is a published figure from an official document or a product listing, not something I measured. The arithmetic (5% headroom, 1.9×, 8×, 1.2 mA) comes from dividing or multiplying two published numbers, and you can recompute all of it.

Desco's US and European bulletins disagree on a few details — hard-ground reading of 1 ohm versus 10 ohms, electrode weight of 5 lb versus 2.2 kg, ASTM-150 versus IEC 61340-2-3 — and I have attributed each statement to the document that made it rather than harmonizing them on the vendor's behalf.

Temperature conversions use the standard Fahrenheit-to-Celsius formula, rounded to whole degrees. A material's temperature ceiling is not a licence to park an iron on it; the official wording for vinyl is a "suggested range" for most flexible vinyl products.

If your situation is a corporate ESD protected area, the governing criterion is whatever revision your program document cites. The table and clause numbers here come from Desco's and SCS's public technical documents; the standard itself has to be purchased from the ESD Association.
