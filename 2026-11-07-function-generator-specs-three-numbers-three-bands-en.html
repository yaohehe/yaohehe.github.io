Function Generator Specs: Three Numbers, Three Bands

test equipmentfunction generatorspec sheet

The listing says 60 MHz. You put it on a scope to measure the rising edge of a 20 MHz square wave, and the edge comes out sloped, sloped enough that it is no longer a square wave.

I actually read six official manuals from RIGOL, Siglent and UNI-T line by line before this made sense: the machine was fine and my reading of the spec sheet was not. The "frequency" row on that sheet is six rows, not one.

Retail listings print a single number, usually 60 MHz or 120 MHz. In the vendor manual that number has a name: sine. The same table carries separate rows for square, pulse, ramp and arbitrary, and every one of them is lower than the sine row. You buy on the sine row and you work on the others.

This piece re-derives four in-stock models on one consistent basis, and pulls apart the three numbers that get treated as one: frequency, sample rate and amplitude.

TL;DR: three points up front.

  • Frequency applies to sine alone. The Siglent SDG2122X is sold as 120 MHz, but its square limit is 25 MHz and its ramp limit is 1 MHz. The UNI-T UTG962E is sold as 60 MHz, with a 20 MHz square and a 400 kHz ramp, a 150x gap between sine and ramp on the same box.
  • Divide the sample rate by the interpolation factor before comparing. Siglent prints "1.2 GSa/s (4X Interpolation)" and its own white paper states the DDS clock is 300 MHz. Divided by four, it is 1.5x the RIGOL 200 MSa/s, not 6x.
  • Amplitude carries two conditions. 20 Vpp holds below 20 MHz into a high-Z load, and a manual footnote reads "the specification will be divided by 2 while applied to a 50Ω load."

Number One: Frequency Only Covers the Sine

The RIGOL DG1000Z manual is the most explicit of the group. It splits frequency into six rows:

  • Sine: DG1022Z 25 MHz, DG1032Z 30 MHz, DG1062Z 60 MHz
  • Square: all three models at 25 MHz
  • Pulse: 15 MHz, 15 MHz, 25 MHz
  • Ramp: 500 kHz, 500 kHz, 1 MHz
  • Harmonic: 10 MHz, 10 MHz, 20 MHz
  • Arbitrary: 10 MHz, 10 MHz, 20 MHz

Look at the square row. All three machines share one number. Paying for 60 MHz ($529 on the vendor site against $389) buys a wider sine band and nothing at all on square.

Siglent reads the same way. In the SDG2000X manual the sine row is 40, 80 and 120 MHz, while the square row is a single "1μ–25 MHz". So the 120 MHz model also stops its square at 25 MHz, one fourth of its sine figure.

UNI-T spreads wider. Its official selection guide lists the UTG932E at 30 MHz sine, 15 MHz square and 400 kHz ramp. The UTG962E doubles sine to 60 MHz, lifts square to 20 MHz, and leaves the ramp at 400 kHz. The $30 step from $199.99 to $229.00 (prices as scraped on 2026-11-07) buys sine and square, and zero ramp.

That ramp row deserves its own look. At 200 MSa/s, 400 kHz means 500 points per cycle, more than a hundred times the 3.33 points left at 60 MHz sine. So the low ramp ceiling is not a sample rate limit. The vendors do not explain the gap, and from the numbers alone I can confirm exactly one thing: you cannot infer the ramp limit from the sample rate. Two different mechanisms drive those two rows.

Number Two: Sample Rate, Before You Divide by the Interpolation Factor

This is the row people misread most often.

The model table in the Siglent SDG2000X manual reads "Sampling rate 1.2 GSa/s (4X Interpolation)". Most readers skip the parentheses. The same manual, in the arbitrary wave section, gives the real numbers: 1 μSa/s to 75 MSa/s in TrueArb mode, 300 MSa/s in DDS mode.

The official white paper explains the mechanism in plain words: the reference clock of the DDS circuitry on the SDG2000X is 300 MHz, and the interpolation circuit raises the sampling clock of the D/A converter to 1.2 GHz, which is where the 1.2 GSa/s figure with its "4X interpolation" note comes from.

The SDG1000X Plus is written the same way: "1 GSa/s (4X Interpolation)" in the model table, and 250 MSa/s in the arbitrary wave section. One gigahertz divided by four is 250, and the two match.

So a claimed sample rate has to be divided by its interpolation factor before it means anything:

Recompute points per cycle at each vendor's own sine ceiling and the listing-page spread largely disappears:

  • UNI-T UTG932E: 200 / 30 = 6.67 points per cycle
  • UNI-T UTG962E: 200 / 60 = 3.33 points per cycle
  • RIGOL DG1062Z: 200 / 60 = 3.33 points per cycle
  • Siglent SDG1062X Plus: 250 / 60 = 4.17 points per cycle
  • Siglent SDG2042X: 300 / 40 = 7.50 points per cycle
  • Siglent SDG2122X: 300 / 120 = 2.50 points per cycle

Everything lands in single digits, from 2.5 to 8. Those are the vendors' own sine ceilings, the point where the instrument stops meeting spec, and that leftover headroom belongs to the reconstruction filter, not to your waveform.

Interpolation is not a trick. The white paper is direct about why it exists: a 300 MSa/s D/A can only reach 120 MHz because of reconstruction filter limits, while a 1.2 GSa/s D/A reaches higher, and with 4x interpolation the distortion products of a 120 MHz output sit at 960 MHz and 840 MHz, far outside the passband. What interpolation buys is a cleaner high-frequency sine, not four times the waveform detail.

The arbitrary wave section proves the point: the interpolator pushes the DAC clock to 1.2 GHz, while point-by-point TrueArb output still stops at 75 MSa/s.

Number Three: Amplitude Comes With Two Conditions

Amplitude is the messiest of the three because it carries two conditions at once.

Start with frequency. RIGOL prints the finest table in the group, three tiers into 50Ω:

  • Up to 10 MHz: 1.0 mVpp to 10 Vpp
  • Up to 30 MHz: 1.0 mVpp to 5.0 Vpp
  • Up to 60 MHz: 1.0 mVpp to 2.5 Vpp

Walk from 10 MHz to 60 MHz and the usable amplitude falls to one quarter. The 10 Vpp on the listing only exists below 10 MHz.

Siglent gives two tiers and switches the reference to a high-Z load: 2 mV to 20 Vpp up to 20 MHz, 2 mV to 10 Vpp above it. The data sheet also states that 20 Vpp is guaranteed at 20 MHz on both channels, which tells you where the line sits.

UNI-T writes "1 mVpp~10 Vpp (50Ω); 2 mVpp~20 Vpp (high Z)" with no frequency condition at all. That is not unique to UNI-T, but it means the manual cannot tell you whether 10 Vpp survives at 30 MHz. You have to measure it yourself.

Now the load. Siglent adds a footnote under the output characteristics table: "Note 1: The specification will be divided by 2 while applied to a 50Ω load." UNI-T puts the two columns side by side instead: 10 Vpp into 50Ω, 20 Vpp into high Z.

Both statements describe one thing. A 50Ω source impedance and a 50Ω load form a divider, so the load sees half the set value. A scope input is usually 1 MΩ, and there you read twice the set value. Switch to a 50Ω feed-through or a 50Ω terminator and the reading drops back by half. Same instrument, same setting, two readings, 6 dB apart.

One safety number while we are here. The RIGOL manual gives the overvoltage protection thresholds at the output: with amplitude set above 2 Vpp, or offset above 2 VDC, an external voltage beyond ±11.5 V trips protection; with amplitude at or below 2 Vpp, ±3.5 V is enough. The output connector is not an input, so do not clip it onto a live node.

Pick by What You Need to Measure: Three Branches

If you use one criterion, use the ceiling of the waveform you actually need, not the sine ceiling.

  • Edge, clock and SPI or I2C timing work: read the square row. 25 MHz is the ceiling here (SDG2042X and SDG2122X), UTG962E reaches 20 MHz, UTG932E stops at 15 MHz. Then read the edge time, because it decides whether the wave is still square. The SDG2042X specifies 9 ns typical rise and fall at 1 Vpp into 50Ω. At 25 MHz the period is 40 ns, the two edges eat 18 ns of it, and with a 20 ns half period only 11 ns of flat top remains. On a scope that is a trapezoid.
  • Sweeps, triangles and sawtooth stimulus: read the ramp row and put 400 kHz or 1 MHz first. There is no middle option in this group; it is either 400 kHz or 1 MHz.
  • Arbitrary waveforms, sensor simulation or recorded playback: read the arbitrary row and the memory depth. 8 Mpts is 40 ms at 200 MSa/s and 26.7 ms at 300 MSa/s. If you need to replay one second of a recording, 8 Mpts will not hold it, and you will be looping or segmenting.

The other side of the same question is when not to buy a bench unit. If you need sines, squares and triangles below 1 MHz, the UTG932E already covers all three rows at 200 MSa/s and 14 bit, and the extra money buys nothing on square or ramp.

Five Minutes of Checks After It Arrives

First, set the frequency to the vendor's ceiling, pick sine, and watch the scope for steps. By the arithmetic above only 2.5 to 8 points per cycle remain up there, so steps are expected; what matters is whether the amplitude holds.

Second, switch to square, set the vendor's square ceiling, and look at the rising edge. The published edge time is a typical value at 1 Vpp into 50Ω. Set 10 Vpp and the edge slows down, which the RIGOL manual states in the condition column rather than the body text.

Third, change the load from 1 MΩ to 50Ω and watch the voltage. Halving is correct. No change means your 50Ω termination is not seated.

Fourth, wait 30 minutes before judging accuracy. Both RIGOL and Siglent require the same conditions: within the calibration period, continuously powered for at least 30 minutes, at 18 to 28 degrees Celsius. A number taken at power-on does not count.

FAQ

Q: Can a 60 MHz function generator output a 60 MHz square wave?

No. Square is its own row in the manual: 20 MHz on the UTG962E, 25 MHz on the RIGOL DG1062Z, 25 MHz on the SDG2122X. The 60 MHz or 120 MHz figure applies to sine alone.

Q: Is 1.2 GSa/s six times better than 200 MSa/s?

No. Siglent prints "1.2 GSa/s (4X Interpolation)", and its white paper states the DDS clock is 300 MHz with the interpolator raising the DAC clock to 1.2 GHz. Divided by four that is 300 MSa/s against 200 MSa/s, a factor of 1.5. Interpolation moves the distortion products of a high-frequency sine further out; it does not add waveform detail.

Q: Why does 20 Vpp read as 10 Vpp on my scope?

A 50Ω source impedance and a 50Ω load form a divider. Siglent states it in Note 1: "The specification will be divided by 2 while applied to a 50Ω load."

Q: How much does 14 bit versus 16 bit matter?

Across a 20 Vpp range, 14 bit gives 16,384 levels and roughly a 1.22 mV step; 16 bit gives 65,536 levels and roughly 0.305 mV. That is a fourfold difference in step height. It shows up when you stack a small signal on a large one or test ADC linearity, and it mostly disappears when you drive digital timing.

Q: Do I need 8 Mpts of memory?

Depends on playback length. 8 Mpts is 40 ms at 200 MSa/s and 26.7 ms at 300 MSa/s. Beyond that you loop or use segmented sequences. For standard waveforms the memory depth does nothing for you.

Q: Why is the ramp ceiling so low?

The vendors do not say. What can be confirmed is the arithmetic: ramp runs from 400 kHz to 2 MHz while sine runs from 25 MHz to 120 MHz, and 400 kHz at 200 MSa/s leaves 500 points per cycle, far more than the 3.33 points at 60 MHz sine. So this is not a sample rate limit. Buy against the ramp row, and do not derive it from the sine row.
