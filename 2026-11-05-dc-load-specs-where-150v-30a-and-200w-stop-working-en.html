DC Load Specs: Where 150V, 30A and 200W Stop Working

electronic loadbench instrumentsspec sheets

A listing title says 150V / 30A / 200W, three numbers side by side, and it reads like a machine that hands you all three at once. It does not. 150V times 30A is 4500VA, and the rated power is 200W. One of those three numbers is always void.

Every number below is one I actually checked against the vendor PDF, and the arithmetic I did on those numbers is labelled as arithmetic. This is not a buying guide in the usual sense. It is about the conditions under which each number is true: three ratings, a fourth ceiling that lives in the ratings table, a range footnote that voids your accuracy, and a geometric relation that cuts the usable current in constant-resistance mode. The supply side of the same question is covered in the bench power supply guide. This is its mirror image: what sinks the current.

TL;DR:

  • Full-power point equals rated power divided by maximum current. 300W/30A gives 10V, 200W/40A gives 5V, 175W/40A gives 4.4V. Below that voltage the power limit binds first; above it the current limit does.
  • Minimum operating voltage is a fourth, independent ceiling and it appears only in the ratings table: Siglent's SDL1000X series states 0.9V at 30A, Rigol's DL3021 states 1V at 40A. Below that line you cannot reach the rated current.
  • The programmable floor of constant-resistance mode is not the same constraint. Rigol's DL3021 bottoms out at 0.08Ω, which needs 3.2V to reach 40A — 3.2 times what constant current needs.
  • Both vendors place "input not below 10% FS" in the accuracy footnotes. Measuring 1A on a 30A range puts you outside the guaranteed window. Range selection is a precondition, not an optimization.

The first number to fail is power

Draw the three ratings as a rectangle: 150V across, 30A up, 4500VA of area. The vendor then draws a hyperbola P = V × I inside it. Everything outside the hyperbola is unavailable. So 150V and 30A are just two boundary lines, and reaching the corner where both hold would need 4500W of heat dissipation from an instrument rated at 200W or 300W.

Run the arithmetic on the official numbers: Siglent's SDL1030X-E puts 300W inside a 150 × 30 rectangle, which is 6.7%. The SDL1020X-E puts 200W there, 4.4%. This is not sandbagging. Everything an electronic load absorbs becomes heat, and the heatsink volume sets that denominator.

A current rating only means something in the low-voltage corner.

The fourth ceiling: minimum operating voltage

Open a datasheet to the ratings table. One row below input power there is usually a row called Minimum Operating Voltage. It appears in no listing title, no comparison chart and no product photo, yet it decides whether low-voltage high-current testing is possible at all.

Siglent's SDL1000X series datasheet (DS0801X-C02D, dated 2019.10 inside the file) prints two columns side by side: 0.15V at 5A and 0.9V at 30A. Rigol's DL3000 datasheet turns it into its own column: 1V@40A for the DL3021, 1.3V@60A for the DL3031, 1.5V@70A for the DL3041.

The physical meaning is straightforward. The MOSFET bank and the shunt inside the load drop voltage themselves, and below some input voltage the instrument can no longer hold the programmed current; it degrades into something close to a fixed resistance. Rigol publishes a single point, but two points define a line: read 1V/40A as a 0.025Ω floor and at 0.75V the 40A range can sink roughly 30A. That last number is my arithmetic on two official figures, not a bench measurement.

Generation matters too. A second Siglent datasheet, uploaded in August 2025 for the SDL1000X-C series and dated 2019.12 inside, prints 0.13V at 5A and 0.8V at 30A in the same row. Same ratings, 150V/30A and 200W/300W, different minimum operating voltage — the model names differ by a suffix. You have to match the suffix to the right PDF, not just the series name.

CR mode bottoms out on its own schedule

Constant resistance is often described as "simulate a resistor", but the programmable range has a floor, and that floor is usually assumed to equal minimum operating voltage divided by maximum current. It does not.

Siglent's CR range is 0.03Ω to 10kΩ. Note that 0.9V ÷ 30A = 0.03Ω and 0.15V ÷ 5A = 0.03Ω, so on that generation the CR floor and the operating-voltage floor are the same line.

Rigol's DL3021 is not built that way. Its low CR range is 0.08Ω to 15Ω, while 1V ÷ 40A = 0.025Ω — a factor of 3.2 apart. The consequence is concrete: reaching 40A in CR mode needs 0.08Ω × 40A = 3.2V across the input, while the same 40A in CC mode needs 1V. A 1.2V nickel cell cannot be discharged at 40A in constant resistance, physically.

Then look back at the Siglent -C datasheet. Minimum operating voltage dropped to 0.8V while the CR floor stayed at 0.03Ω, and 0.8 ÷ 30 = 0.0267 no longer equals 0.03. That mismatch is the proof that the two limits are independent: the physical floor moved, the programmable window did not.

Rigol also buries a precondition in a footnote: the low CR range requires an input below 8V (Note 5 in that datasheet). Try to use 0.08Ω on a 12V supply and you must switch to the high range, where the floor jumps to 2Ω.

Accuracy has to be divided by the range

Load accuracy is written as a% plus b% FS, where FS is the full scale of that range, not the value you set. The same ± therefore means wildly different things at different currents.

Rigol's DL3021 splits CC programming accuracy into two ranges: 0.1% + 0.004A on the 0–4A range and 0.1% + 0.04A on 0–40A. At a 1A setpoint the low range gives 0.001 + 0.004 = ±5mA (0.5%), and the high range gives 0.001 + 0.04 = ±41mA (4.1%). Same setpoint, eight times the error, purely from range choice.

Siglent writes it as 0.05% + 0.05% FS on both the 5A and 30A ranges. At 1A that is 0.0005 + 0.0025 = ±3mA on the 5A range and 0.0005 + 0.015 = ±15.5mA on the 30A range. Better than the DL3021 on its high range, at the cost of a larger fixed term on both.

Now the part almost everyone skips. Rigol's Note 4 reads: "The input voltage/current value should not be smaller than 10% of the full scale." Siglent prints the same condition twice, against CP and CR: input voltage and current not below 10% FS. So measuring 1A on a 30A range does not give you the ±15.5mA — 1A is 3.3% FS, outside the guaranteed window entirely.

The conclusion is not "buy the expensive one". It is: measure small currents on a small range. The question to ask before buying is whether a 5A or 4A range exists, not whether the headline accuracy is 0.05% or 0.1%.

Readback follows the same logic. The DL3021 reads current at 1mA resolution with 0.1% + 0.1% FS, which is a ±40mA fixed term at a 40A full scale; the DL3021A moves to 0.1mA and 0.05% + 0.05% FS, halving it to ±20mA. If you are integrating mAh for a battery discharge curve, that 20mA goes straight into the result.

Dynamic frequency and slew: the footnote belongs to one model only

Transient mode switches between two setpoints to test a supply's load response. Three numbers get mixed together constantly: frequency, slew rate and minimum rise time.

Rigol's model table is explicit: the DL3021 does 15kHz with 1 A/μs, while the DL3021A and DL3041 do 30kHz, and only the DL3041 reaches 5 A/μs. But Note 7 in the same datasheet lists a voltage-dependent slew table — 5 A/μs at 9V and above, 4 at 8V, 2.5 at 7V, 1.25 at 6V, 0.5 at 5V, 0.2 at 3V. That table describes the 5 A/μs model only. Reading it as the behaviour of a 1 A/μs DL3021 will mislead you badly.

What is worth remembering is the shape: slew falls with input voltage, down to 1/25 of the headline figure at 3V. "5 A/μs" is a high-voltage number, and in low-voltage high-current work the transient performance is tied to the rail, not to the datasheet headline.

Siglent splits the number by mode instead: 25kHz in CC dynamic, 12.5kHz in CP, and 0.5Hz in CV. That 0.5Hz is not a typo — the constant-voltage loop is slow by design, and the vendor never intended it for fast transients. If you care about loop response, the CC column is the one to read. Korad's KEL2000 series page states "Dynamic mode: up to 10KHZ", a step below both.

The SDL1030X-E on Amazon is 150V/30A/300W and showed $1012.00 when I pulled it. Its strength is documentation: minimum operating voltage, CR sub-ranges, slew, even the overpower protection trip point (≈310W) and the battery test window (0.5 to 150V, capacity to 999AH) are all in the table.

Three flaws. The -E suffix drops readback resolution from 0.1mV/0.1mA to 1mV/1mA, while both datasheets carry the same "SDL1000X series" cover, so you will not see it unless you open the specification table. CV dynamic mode tops out at 0.5Hz, which rules it out for fast load steps on the voltage side. And 300W lives in a 5.8kg chassis, with the fan as the dominant noise source and no noise figure published.

Korad KEL2010

The Korad KEL2010 is 150V/30A/300W with 0.1mV/0.1mA resolution, 100 storage slots and USB, RS232 and LAN. Its fourth mode is printed as CW rather than CP — the same thing under a different name, and an easy way to conclude wrongly that it has no constant-power mode.

The flaw is documentation depth. Korad's product page renders its specification table client-side, so I could only retrieve series-level claims (10kHz dynamic, four modes, remote sense). In practice that means you cannot confirm from the official site whether it holds 30A below 1V. The buy-box price also failed to return consistently during my fetch — the page showed what was clearly an accessory price at one point — so I am not quoting a price here; check the listing.

Maynuo M9711

The Maynuo M9711 is 150V/30A/150W and showed $494.68. It binds current and power most tightly of the five: the full-power point is 5V, so testing a 24V supply caps out at 6.25A.

The flaw: 150W leaves the least thermal headroom for long full-load runs, and no derating curve is published. Above 5V the 30A figure is unreachable, which makes the headline number meaningless for most 12V and 24V work.

TH8202A and TH8201

The TH8202A is 150V/40A/350W at $720.00; the TH8201 is 150V/40A/175W at $680.00. Same series, same voltage and current, $40 apart, and double the power. That is the most actionable finding in this piece: in this series the 175W tier is poor value, and 5.9% more money buys twice the dissipation.

The flaws are shared. Both Amazon pages carry only the three headline numbers — no accuracy, no minimum operating voltage, no CR range. For any test report you hand to a customer, you need the manufacturer's manual first, or your results cannot be labelled with test conditions. And 40A only exists below 8.75V on the 350W model, which the listing will never tell you.

When you should not buy one

Three cases can be dismissed immediately:

  • You only want to know how many watts a charger delivers. A USB power meter does that for two orders of magnitude less, though its own range limits bite — see what the five numbers on a USB meter mean.
  • You want timing and protocol, not load capability. That is a logic analyzer job, and the DSLogic versus Kingst spec comparison covers the sample-rate arithmetic.
  • You want to charge a battery. A load is a single-quadrant device: it sinks current and never sources it.

What remains — power supply burn-in, battery discharge curves, adapter OCP points, LED driver ripple — is its actual job. Before buying, check four things in order: is the full-power point high enough, is the minimum operating voltage low enough, does CR floor times maximum current still sit inside your test voltage, and is there a small-current range.

FAQ

Q: What is the real difference between 200W and 300W at the same 150V/30A?

A: Only the full-power point. The 300W unit keeps adding current up to 30A above 10V; the 200W unit stops at 6.67V. On 12V loads that is a 10A gap, on 5V loads almost nothing.

Q: Can calibration get around a 0.9V minimum operating voltage?

A: No. It is a hardware path limit, not a display error. Below that line the setpoint and the actual sink current stop tracking each other.

Q: Why can't CR mode reach the rated current on a 1.2V cell?

A: Because the CR floor times current equals the voltage you need. 0.08Ω × 40A = 3.2V, so at 1.2V you get about 15A. Switch to CC mode if you need the current.

Q: If accuracy is 0.1% + 0.1% FS, is a 1A reading off by 0.2%?

A: No. FS is the range full scale, so 0.1% FS on a 40A range is 40mA — 4% at 1A. And below 10% FS the vendor does not guarantee the figure at all.

Q: One channel or two?

A: Two channels save a chassis and a mains lead when you test input and output together or run dual burn-in. For single-path discharge testing, one unit plus a switching relay costs less.

Q: Do these prices hold?

A: No. Every price here is what the listing showed when I pulled it on 2026-11-05, and Amazon localizes currency by region, so check the page before ordering.
