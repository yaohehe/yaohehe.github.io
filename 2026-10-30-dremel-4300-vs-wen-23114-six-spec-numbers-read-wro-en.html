Dremel 4300 vs WEN 23114: Six Spec Numbers Read Wrong

rotary toolDremel 4300dremel review

A rotary tool is the most "one tool, many jobs" item on a workbench: swap the accessory and it cuts, sands, engraves, polishes or removes rust. It also has the most misreadable spec sheet in its price bracket. The same 4300 model is documented with different field conventions on Dremel's US site and its EU site, and the most eye-catching number of all, the accessory count, is completely inverted against official MSRP in the company's own press release.

I did not run any of these tools. I do not own them, and I am not going to pretend a bench test happened. What I actually did was duller: on 2026-10-30 I pulled Dremel's official product pages, the official press release, the official manual PDF and WEN's official product page, and compared them line by line to see which numbers can be compared directly and which cannot. Of the six numbers people quote most often, four only hold with a condition attached.

TL;DR: three shortcuts. First, if you want to stop fumbling with collets, take the Dremel 4300-9/64, whose chuck, in Dremel's own words, "accepts all Dremel accessory shank sizes" — you never touch a collet to change a bit. Second, if you want speed held steady under load without paying for the flagship kit, take the Dremel 4000-2/32 with electronic feedback: 1.6 A, and the official page states "the electronic feedback consistently gives you maximum force". Third, if the budget is tight and you want accessories, the WEN 23114 is officially 1.4 A, 8,000 to 35,000 RPM, three collets plus a flex shaft — the catch is a floor that starts 3,000 RPM higher than the Dremel trio.

One: 35,000 RPM is a no-load figure, and your accessory has its own ceiling

On the three corded Dremel models, the US spec tables all have a row called Speed, and every value is "5,000 to 35,000 RPM". On the EU site, the same 4300 lists that row as No load speed 5.000 - 35.000 1/min — Dremel wrote the qualification into the field name instead of burying it in the manual.

The manual corroborates it in a more useful place. After fitting an accessory, Dremel tells you to "run the power tool at maximum no-load speed for one minute", because "Damaged accessories will normally break apart during this test time". In other words, 35,000 is a number produced in open air. How far it sags once the bit bites is not stated anywhere in the published specs.

That is where the real difference between the three models shows up. It is not the speed range: the ranges are identical. It is whether the tool compensates under load.

  • Dremel 4300: the official page says "built-in variable speed and electronic feedback circuitry"
  • Dremel 4000: the official page says "the electronic feedback consistently gives you maximum force", and the May 2024 press release gives it a separate line: "Electronic feedback control provides consistent performance in the most demanding applications"
  • Dremel 3100: neither the product page nor the press release mentions this feature at all

Pair that with the press release's "33% more power than the 3100" (1.2 A to 1.6 A) and the premium becomes legible: what you are actually paying for is speed retention under load, not a higher RPM ceiling.

There is a second rule that gets skipped, and Dremel uses the same wording in several manuals:

The rated speed of the accessories must be at least equal to the maximum speed marked on the power tool. Accessories running faster than their rated speed can break and fly apart.

Read it backwards when shopping for third-party accessory kits: the rated speed on the package must be at least as high as the top speed of your tool. The same manual adds that you should not use reinforced cut-off wheels left over from a larger tool, because "Wheels intended for a larger power tool are not suitable for the higher speed of a smaller tool and may burst".

"It is also a 1 A tool" is therefore an incomplete sentence until you name the top speed.

Three: 1.2, 1.6, 1.8 A, and what the official "33%" is counting

The May 2024 press release, worded identically on dremel.com and on PR Newswire, lays out a clean power ladder:

  • 3100: 1.2 A, officially "33 percent more power than the Dremel 200"
  • 4000: 1.6 A, officially "33% more power than the 3100"
  • Check the multiplication: 1.2 x 1.33 is about 1.6, so both "33%" claims agree with the amperage in the spec table

Apply the same ruler to the 4300's 1.8 A and it is only 0.2 A above the 4000, roughly 12.5 percent, and 0.6 A, or 50 percent, above the 3100. So if you are stuck between the 4000 and the 4300, power does not explain the price gap. Dremel differentiates the 4300 on other things: the three-jaw chuck, the Pivot Light, and the bundled 225 flex shaft.

There is also a cross-region convention problem sitting in the middle of this. The US page gives Amperage 1.8 and Voltage 120V. The EU page gives Rated power input 175 W and Voltage 220 to 240 V. Amps and watts are not the same quantity, so the power fields on the two sites cannot be converted into each other. But the EU page hands you three fields the US page omits entirely: sound pressure 74.4 dB(A), sound power 85.4 dB(A), and vibration 18 m/s2. If noise and vibration for long sessions matter to you, the EU version of the page is the only place to look.

Four: collet sizes, and the manual's own line about kits

The official 4000 manual, the OEM PDF hosted on dremel.com, gives four values in the Collet capacities row of its Functional Description and Specifications table: 1/32", 1/16", 3/32" and 1/8". Immediately beneath it, in small type:

Note: Most rotary tool kits do not include all four collets sizes.

The tool accepting four shank sizes is not the same as four collets being in the box. The manual also tells you how to identify them by counting rings at the tail of the collet: one ring for 1/32", two for 1/16", three for 3/32", none for 1/8" (annotated "Included in most tool kits on the tool").

There are three genuinely different engineering approaches here:

1. Collet plus nut (3100, 4000, WEN 23114). The 3100 page says "The innovative EZ Twist nose cap, with a built-in key, makes accessory changes quick and easy, eliminating the need for a wrench" — what it eliminates is the wrench, not the collet. Smaller shanks need extra parts: the 3100 tips page lists #481, #482 and #483 singly or #4485 as a set; the 4000 page lists the 4485 Collet Nut Kit or the 4486 keyless chuck, both explicitly sold separately. The WEN 23114 page states that the box contains "three different collet sizes (1/8", 3/32", 1/16")", with no 1/32".

2. Three-jaw chuck (4300). The headline claim is "The 4300 is the first Dremel rotary tool to allow tool-less and collet-less accessory changes with the inclusion of the keyless chuck".

3. Change the form factor instead of the collet. The official Dremel 225-02 Flex Shaft sells on "Small handpiece ... provides superior control, less fatigue, and better results", and the tips pages for both the 4000 and the 3100 suggest "Use our Detailers Grip or Flex Shaft attachments with your Dremel Rotary Tool for extra accuracy".

Two fitting disciplines get repeated across the manuals. Leave "a gap of less than 1/4 inch between the accessory and the collet nut", do not seat the bit all the way down against the nut. And the bolded warning: "Always unplug Rotary Tool before changing accessories, changing collets or servicing your Rotary Tool". Unplug, not just switch off.

The two more expensive tiers come with fewer accessories. The press release says so itself: the 3100-2/60 is "$15 less than the comparable 3000-2/28" and is an Amazon exclusive.

The second convention problem is that "pieces" includes consumables. The official 4000-2/32 contents list is heavy on sanding: #407, #408, #432, #445, #411, #412, #413 and more are all sanding bands and discs that wear out. The GOXAWEE 140-piece set is the same story, its page reading "140Pcs Rotary Tool Accessories & Attachment". An accessory count tells you how many different jobs you can try on day one. It tells you nothing about how long the kit lasts.

Two sharper contradictions:

  • The 3100-1/15 body copy reads "At just 7.5 inches long and weighing approximately 20 ounces", while the table on that same page says Length in 3.5
  • The 3100-2/60 table lists Width in as 225.0 inches, which converts to over 5.7 metres

The conclusion is blunt: the dimension fields in these tables cannot be used to judge how a tool feels in the hand. For actual size and weight, read the prose on the same page (the 3100 is approximately 20 ounces), or the standalone Weight (oz.) 25.0 row in the 4300 table; the EU site uses 0.66 kg. Neither the fields nor the values line up between regions, so do not mix numbers from the two.

Five tools, five real weaknesses

### Dremel 4300-9/64: 1.8 A / 5,000-35,000 RPM / 64 pieces

Officially "our most powerful motor", bundled with 64 accessories and the 225 flex shaft, a three-jaw chuck that accepts all Dremel accessory shank sizes, electronic feedback, and replaceable motor brushes (the spec field reads Replaceable Motor Brushes: Yes).

Real weakness: 1.8 A is only about 12.5 percent above the 4000's 1.6 A, so power does not justify the price difference; a large share of those 64 pieces is sanding consumable; and when I fetched it, the listing did not return the standard In Stock string, so confirm the page state before ordering.

### Dremel 4000-2/32: 1.6 A / 5,000-35,000 RPM / 32 pieces

This tier gets electronic feedback, called out as its own selling point in the press release, and the kit includes the A576 and 565 guide attachments.

Real weakness: the official page states that shanks below 1/8" require the 4485 Collet Nut Kit or the 4486 keyless chuck, marked sold separately; seven of the 32 pieces are sanding consumable; and at the time of fetching the listing showed a third-party seller shipping through Amazon, which is a different arrangement from the other models.

### Dremel 3100-1/15: 1.2 A / 5,000-35,000 RPM / 15 pieces

The $59.99 entry kit: 1.2 A, five speed settings, a 6-foot cord, replaceable brushes, a two-year limited warranty, and the 675 sharpening attachment included.

Real weakness: neither the product page nor the press release lists electronic feedback, so there is no speed compensation under load; the spec fields say Soft Grip No and LED Worklight No; only 15 accessories ship in the box; and anything other than 1/8" needs #481, #482 or #483, or the #4485 set.

### WEN 23114: 1.4 A / 8,000-35,000 RPM / 100+ pieces

Well equipped on paper: a 1.4 A motor, three collets, three interchangeable collars (drill guide, LED, spark guard), a flex shaft, 100+ accessories, a carrying case and a two-year warranty.

Real weakness: the floor is 8,000 RPM rather than 5,000, leaving less margin for polishing and soft materials; only three collets are included, with no 1/32"; the keyless chuck (2305-KC) is a separate purchase listed from $8.22 on WEN's own accessory page; and the same official page describes the flex shaft as 3-foot in one place and three-and-a-half-foot in another, so two lengths coexist in the copy.

### GOXAWEE 140-piece: 130 W / 8,000-30,000 RPM / 140 pieces

The listing gives a 130 W pure copper motor, five speed settings, a keyless three-jaw chuck, a 3.3 ft flex shaft and 140 accessories, the largest count in this group.

Real weakness: 30,000 RPM is the lowest ceiling here; the 8,000 RPM floor is high too; there is no manufacturer spec page to check against, so every number above comes from the listing itself; and cut-off wheels and sanding drums dominate the 140 pieces.

FAQ

Q: Is a higher RPM always better on a rotary tool?

A: No. 35,000 RPM is a no-load figure, and the EU field name literally says No load speed. The direction of the effect often reverses: polishing plastic or soft metal needs low speed, where the bottom of the range is the constraint, not the top. The three corded Dremel models start at 5,000 RPM; the WEN 23114 is officially 8,000 RPM.

Q: Can I run a third-party cut-off wheel rated 30,000 RPM in a 35,000 RPM tool?

A: Not under the official rule. The manual reads "The rated speed of the accessories must be at least equal to the maximum speed marked on the power tool. Accessories running faster than their rated speed can break and fly apart." Check the rating on the package before you buy.

Q: Does a bigger accessory count mean better value?

A: Not necessarily. In the official press release the $69.99 3100-2/60 has 60 pieces while the $89.99 4000-2/32 has 32, and counts include consumables such as sanding bands, discs and cut-off wheels. The count measures how many jobs you can try, not build quality or durability.

Q: What do I need to buy to use 1/16" or thinner shank accessories?

A: It depends on the model. The 3100 tips page lists #481, #482 and #483 individually or the #4485 set; the 4000 page lists the 4485 Collet Nut Kit or the 4486 keyless chuck, both sold separately; the 4300 ships with a three-jaw chuck that officially accepts all Dremel accessory shank sizes, so nothing extra is needed.

Q: Can I replace the motor brushes myself?

A: All three corded Dremel models list Replaceable Motor Brushes: Yes, so the design allows it. Whether a spare set is in the box is not stated on the official pages and has to be confirmed per model.
