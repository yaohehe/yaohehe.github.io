Translation and direct connection are two different paths

First, why translation docks exist at all. When you plug a monitor into a laptop, the image data either travels over the graphics chip's native output path or it does not. DisplayPort over USB-C is the former. VESA's technical page states that DP Alt Mode does 4K@60Hz 24-bit "without compression" while simultaneously running USB 3.1, and that 5K (5120×2880) also works uncompressed but drops USB to 2.0; it explicitly lists BT.2020 color, HDR, and HDCP 2.2 as supported. On that path every frame goes straight from the GPU to the monitor.

DisplayLink is the other kind. The picture gets compressed on the host, wrapped into a USB data stream, and decoded for display by a chip in the dock. DisplayLink's own support documentation is blunt about the chain: the driver reads the modes reported by the display, and only resolutions the monitor reported will be offered through the DisplayLink UI or Windows Display Properties. If a monitor reports nothing, DisplayLink offers a default list. Put differently, the resolutions you can choose are set jointly by the monitor's EDID and the chip's capability, not by the box the dock came in.

That difference produces three consequences: multi-screen works without a discrete GPU, the ceiling is locked by a specific ASIC part number, and a driver is mandatory.

Three details in that table are easy to misread. First, the HDMI and DisplayPort ceilings differ: on DL-6950, DisplayPort reaches 4096×2160p60 while HDMI is listed at 5120×1440p60. With a dock advertised as "dual 4K", which port you plug into decides whether you get full rated output. Second, DisplayLink specifically notes that on the DL-6950, if both video outputs are in use the maximum resolution falls back to 3840×2160 (UHD) or 5120×1440 (5K Ultrawide); the DL-3900 and DL-5900 support dual outputs, but once a second display is connected, resolutions above 2048×1152 become unavailable. Third, the DL-3500 family's HDMI ceiling is written as 2560×1440p50 — that is 50Hz, not 60Hz.

Two more hard limits sit at the end of the same table: no DisplayLink IC supports dual-link DVI, and a monitor with DisplayPort MST must have MST disabled on the translation path, because DisplayLink does not currently support MST.

Five shipping docks and their real boundaries

Anker Prime DL7400 — densest ports, narrowest compatibility promise

Anker's own Hubs & Docks category page names this product "Anker Prime Docking Station (14-in-1, Triple Display, DisplayLink)", matching the listing title. The product page lists 14 ports: one 140W 10Gbps USB-C upstream port, two 100W 10Gbps USB-C ports, one 100W 5Gbps USB-C port, two HDMI, one DisplayPort, two 5Gbps USB-A, one 480Mbps USB-A, a 2.5Gbps Ethernet port, a headphone/mic combo jack, and SD plus TF card slots. Dimensions are 7.67×3.62×1.89 inches, weight 834.1 g, rated 160 watts, with an active fan, ActiveShield 3.0 cooling, and a status display.

Real flaw: the page states "Compatible with Windows 10/11" and notes the latest DisplayLink driver is required — the vendor does not commit to macOS support in that spec. The front USB-C ports also do not support media display, so they cannot drive a portable monitor. The page further carries an explicit DRM note: due to DisplayLink driver limitations, DRM-protected streaming from Netflix, Prime Video, and Disney+ may show a black screen while audio still plays, and the suggested fix for browser streaming is to disable hardware acceleration. That is not an Anker oversight; it is a structural cost of the translation path.

WAVLINK dual 4K dock — most flexible output combinations

Two HDMI plus two DisplayPort outputs, supporting dual HDMI, HDMI+DP, or dual DP. The compatibility list is unusually specific: Windows 10 and later, macOS 11 and later including Apple Silicon M1–M5 and Neo, and ChromeOS 100 and later, across USB-A, USB-C, USB4, Thunderbolt 3/4/5 laptops.

Real flaw: the first sentence of the vendor description states this product is "not designed for gaming, high-frame-rate video editing, or DRM-protected streaming content" — gaming, high-frame-rate editing, and DRM streaming are explicitly excluded. The page also states "Not compatible with Linux systems", which directly contradicts the TobenONE dock that claims Ubuntu support, discussed below. Separately, this dock does not provide laptop charging, so you need your own PD charger.

Plugable dual 4K HDMI adapter — the only one naming its chip on the page

The Plugable unit is an adapter rather than a full dock, about 1×5.9×2.6 inches and 5.5 ounces. The product page opens by stating it is "Powered by the DisplayLink DL-6950 chipset, with no external GPU or additional Thunderbolt port required", and gives dual HDMI at up to 3840×2160 at 60Hz in extended or mirrored modes.

Real flaw: three claims on this page have to be read together. First, macOS is not recommended — the page says "macOS not recommended" outright and its FAQ points macOS users at the USBC-6950M instead. Second, HDCP is not supported at all, so Netflix, Disney+, Hulu, and Prime Video cannot play on a DisplayLink-connected display. Third, driver rules differ per platform: Windows needs the driver, ChromeOS 100+ does not, and the page claims Windows and ChromeOS support DisplayLink automatically on compatible systems. It also does not pass PD, so the laptop will not charge.

TobenONE 20-in-1 — most screens, hardest Mac limit

Twenty ports including 4×HDMI, 4×DP, three USB 3.2, two USB-C 3.2, one USB 2.0, plus SD/microSD, Ethernet, and audio, with a power button that shuts down all attached devices. The three costs you have to pay

Cost one: HDCP-protected content will not play

This is the most substantive loss on the translation path, and four of the five products admit it on their own pages. Anker writes that it "may show a black screen while audio still plays", Plugable writes "not supported", TobenONE writes "HDCP is not supported", and WAVLINK simply lists DRM streaming as out of scope. Against VESA's description of DP Alt Mode — which explicitly supports HDCP 2.2 — this gap is architectural, not a vendor cutting corners. DisplayLink's knowledge base has a separate article on protected video not playing on Mac while DisplayLink screens are connected, and the Android article is blunter still: the DisplayLink app is limited to one display at a maximum of 1920×1080, and protected content cannot be shown in the app because of Android's own restrictions.

Cost two: the driver is not optional

Installing DisplayLink software is a prerequisite on Windows; ChromeOS 100 and later needs nothing. Linux is harder: DisplayLink's documentation says installation requires the dkms and libdrm-dev packages and runs an installer such as sudo ./displaylink-driver-5.3.0.xx.run. The docs also warn specifically that if an Xorg session is active you must disconnect the dock before running the installer or it will halt, and that plugging the dock back in without rebooting afterwards is "highly discouraged" because it can crash or freeze the X server.

Cost three: Linux has documented boundaries

DisplayLink maintains a page titled "Known issues with DisplayLink ubuntu support" that lists the limits openly: Wayland is only supported from version 3.32 and Ubuntu 20.04; closed-source AMD/NVIDIA drivers are incompatible with the DisplayLink driver, and the official recommendation is to use open-source drivers instead; DisplayLink supports up to four monitors, and more may work but is neither tested nor supported; on X11 all DisplayLink outputs are reported as DVI outputs; the X server may crash after unplugging a DisplayLink device if that device was the only active screen; and for some docks the DisplayLink screens are unavailable after resume from suspend. Switching between mirror and extended mode leaves the resolution at the highest common resolution from mirror mode — though the docs note this is an OS-level limitation that reproduces without DisplayLink present.

Here lies a genuine contradiction: the TobenONE page claims Ubuntu compatibility while the WAVLINK page says Linux is not supported. Both are translation docks, so the difference can only come from firmware and driver packaging. If you must use Linux, DisplayLink's knowledge base article is more trustworthy than any product page, because at least it lists the real traps.

Choosing by device: how to split three routes

Start with DisplayLink's own minimum-requirements table as a filter: Windows 10 Anniversary Update 2016 or later, a 1.2GHz-or-faster processor supporting SSE2, 2GB RAM for one display (4GB or more recommended for two or more), and 30MB of free disk space. The graphics processor needs DirectX 10 or later with a WDDM driver, and Matrox or SiS graphics are not supported. The docs also note that Windows 10 Anniversary Update 2016 introduced a new USB graphics architecture, so the compatibility check only applies to earlier systems.

Route one: direct connection whenever you can. If your machine has Thunderbolt 3/4/5 or supports DP Alt Mode, and you need no more than two screens plus USB 3.x and power, buy a non-translation dock. VESA's numbers show this path is uncompressed at 4K@60, supports HDR and HDCP 2.2, needs no driver, and has no DRM problems. If color accuracy or protected content matters, this is the clearly better route. For the bandwidth and interface differences, read our Thunderbolt 4 vs USB4 cable showdown: 40Gbps and 100W.

Route two: translation, for machines with no native multi-screen path. Typical cases are thin laptops with only USB-C and no Thunderbolt, generic docks bought by enterprise procurement, or a temporary need for a third screen. Check three things in order: whether the packaging or page names the ASIC part number (among these five, only Plugable names DL-6950), then whether your target port is HDMI or DisplayPort, and finally the ceiling and refresh rate in the matching row of the chip table. For the broader dock-selection logic, compare our programmer's USB-C dock buying guide; the MST route is covered in USB-C MST hub showdown: four DP Alt Mode options.

Route three: Thunderbolt docks. When you need multiple screens plus high-bandwidth external storage plus a stable Ethernet port all at full load, Thunderbolt's independent bandwidth allocation is the only one of the three that does not contend with display for bandwidth. The costs are price and the Thunderbolt port requirement. Our Thunderbolt 4 dock showdown: CalDigit TS4 vs Plugable works as a reference.

Troubleshooting: three real faults as documented by the vendor

Symptom one: intermittent blanking or flickering after connecting the dock. DisplayLink has a dedicated troubleshooting entry and points the first checks at the host: verify that USB 3.0 host controller drivers are current, that primary graphics drivers are current, and that no conflicting third-party USB graphics software is installed. There is also a documented Windows item: Windows 10's "virtual resolution changes" mechanism scales rather than physically switching when you change resolution, which can letterbox the image while the monitor OSD still reports its native resolution; the official fixes are editing the monitor's advanced settings, logging off, or rebooting.

Symptom two: no display, and DisplayLink Manager does not detect the device. The docs suggest disconnecting and reconnecting the dock. If you run with the laptop lid closed, note the separate knowledge base entry on using DisplayLink with the lid closed. If installation stalls on Ubuntu, follow the official warning cited above: unplug the device, install dkms and libdrm-dev, reboot, and reconnect.

Symptom three: streaming video has audio but no picture. This is not a settings problem; it is the necessary result of missing HDCP. The only mitigation Anker offers on its own product page is disabling browser hardware acceleration, which helps browser streaming only and costs playback performance. If DRM content is part of your workflow, a translation dock should not be on your shortlist.

FAQ

Q: How many monitors can a DisplayLink dock drive?

DisplayLink's Ubuntu knowledge base entry states support for up to four monitors and explicitly says more may connect and work but is neither tested nor supported. Vendor numbers are usually more conservative: TobenONE advertises four screens while its own page admits M1-chip MacBooks extend to only three, and Anker's three-screen claim is qualified to Windows 10/11. Check the vendor page for your actual operating system rather than trusting the number on the box.

Q: DisplayLink or a Thunderbolt dock?

The deciding question is whether bandwidth needs to be shared. When display, data, and networking are all saturated, only Thunderbolt allocates bandwidth independently. If you just want more screens and are not running fast external storage, a translation dock is sufficient and cheaper. One case should avoid translation entirely: workflows needing protected streaming, audio production, or professional color work.

Q: Does my machine need the driver?

Yes on Windows, no on ChromeOS 100 and later. Linux has the highest bar: DisplayLink's documentation requires dkms and libdrm-dev, and states that with an active Xorg session you must unplug the device first and reboot afterwards, or the X server may crash or freeze. TobenONE's page also notes that the first macOS install prompts for screen recording permission, which the vendor explains is only used to deliver the extended display.

Q: Why does the same dock behave differently on HDMI versus DisplayPort?

Because the two interfaces have different ceilings on the same ASIC. In the official table, the DL-6950 row lists DisplayPort at 4096×2160p60 and HDMI at 5120×1440p60; conversely the DL-3500 family's HDMI ceiling is only 2560×1440p50, which is 50Hz. Switching ports switches you to a different ceiling path, so check the matching row before plugging in.
