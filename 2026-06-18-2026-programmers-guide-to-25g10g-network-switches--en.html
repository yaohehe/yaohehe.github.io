Why Programmers Are Upgrading from 1Gbps in 2026

1Gbps Ethernet is a bottleneck now. Your real scenarios:

  • **NAS transfer speed**: 1Gbps NAS → ~110MB/s real throughput. 2.5Gbps → ~280MB/s. 10Gbps → ~1GB/s
  • **Homelab VM clusters**: Running 3+ VMs (ESXi/PVE) simultaneously, 1Gbps makes you want to throw your keyboard
  • **Large git push/pull**: Big repos with years of history — 30 seconds on 1Gbps, 8 seconds on 2.5Gbps
  • **Video editing / large file transfer**: Footage moving between workstation and NAS, 1Gbps is a non-starter for editors

FAQ

Q: Can 2.5G actually hit 2.5Gbps?

A: Yes, but both ends need to support 2.5G. Common 2.5G NICs: Intel I226-V (common on modern mobos), Realtek 8125BG. Real-world throughput: ~2.4Gbps ≈ 300MB/s, roughly 2.5x faster than gigabit.

Q: Do 10G switches need fiber modules?

A: Not the TRENDnet TEG-S708 — it's pure RJ45, use standard CAT6A cables. QNAP's SFP+ ports need separate SFP+ modules or DAC cables (extra $20-100).

Q: My router already has a 2.5G port. Do I still need a switch?

A: Most routers have only ONE 2.5G LAN port (typically for NAS). All other ports are still gigabit. A dedicated 2.5G switch gives ALL your devices 2.5G, not just the one connected to that special port.

Q: I run 4 servers in my Homelab. What switch size do I need?

A: 4 servers + 1 uplink = minimum 5 ports. Go 8-port to have expansion room — the TP-Link 8-port 2.5G at $69.98 is the sweet spot here.

Bottom Line

The core upgrade logic: check your NAS and motherboard NIC speed first, then pick the switch tier. For most programmers, 2.5G ($30-70) is the highest-value upgrade path. Only go 10G if you're already on that speed tier and need backbone performance.
