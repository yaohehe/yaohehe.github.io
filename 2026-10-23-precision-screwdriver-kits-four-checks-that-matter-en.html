Why bit count is the least useful number on the box

Bit count measures objects, not coverage. iFixit's own comparison table lists Bit Count 64 for the Pro Tech Toolkit, 16 for the Essential, and 112 for the Manta. What decides whether you can turn that one screw is whether the kit contains the bit matching your fastener.

The product pages make the point for me. On the iFixit Pro Tech Toolkit page, Pentalobe appears 4 times, Gamebit 3 times and Standoff 3 times, and the A+ copy explicitly says "including the Pentalobe for iPhones and other Apple devices and the Y000 for the Nintendo Switch". On the iFixit Manta Driver Kit page — 112 pieces, 48 more than the Pro Tech — Pentalobe, Gamebit, Standoff and Y000 each appear zero times. Roughly 70% more pieces, and the Apple and Nintendo specific fasteners are not mentioned in the page copy at all.

Accessories inflate the number too. The STREBITO kit is sold as 142 pieces, and its listing spells out the split: 120 bits plus 22 repair accessories (suction cup, spudger, brush, tweezers, magnetizer and so on). "142" and "120" are two ways of counting the same box, and neither says anything about coverage.

Layer 1: which standard is your screw, actually

This is the layer where most wrong purchases happen. The cross slot was never one standard.

The evidence is two independent manufacturer sources. In the iFixit Pro Tech Toolkit spec table, the Head Style field writes Phillips and JIS as two separate entries: "Phillips, Flathead, Torx, Torx Security, Pentalobe, JIS, HEX, Tri-point, ...". JIS would not be listed separately if it were the same thing as Phillips. Wera goes further. The official description of the Kraftform Micro-Set/12 reads "Phillips (PH00, PH0 — including a size optimized for Asian PH screws and one modified to JCIS 0 standard)": beyond PH00 and PH0 it makes one extra size for Asian PH screws and a separate blade reworked to JCIS 0. Three cross-tip drivers, three standards.

The third standard is Pozidriv. The STREBITO listing lists Phillips and Pozidriv together; the iFixit kits list JIS and not Pozidriv. Neither is an oversight — they aim at different hardware populations. Japanese and Apple devices sit on the JIS side; European appliances and furniture hardware sit on Pozidriv.

The practical rule is plain: identify the standard on your screw first, then check whether the kit has that bit, and only then look at the count. A bit from the wrong family rounds out the slot (cam-out), and that has nothing to do with how much the bit cost.

Layer 2: TORX and TORX Security are two groups, not one

In iFixit's 64-bit list, TORX and TORX Security are split cleanly:

  • TORX: T2, T3, T4, T5 — and that is the whole group
  • TORX Security: TR6, TR7, TR8, TR9, TR10, TR15, TR20, TR25

Notice where the gap sits. Plain Torx stops at T5; T6 and above exist only in the Security group. T6 and T8 are among the common sizes inside laptops and consoles. So when you ask "does this kit have T8", the answer is usually not "no" — it is "yes, but over in the TR group".

There is a side effect. Amazon's spec table writes both groups into a single Head Style field, so a glance shows "Torx Security" and it is easy to conclude plain Torx is fully covered. My fix was to go back to iFixit's per-bit list instead of trusting the merged field.

One official typo worth noting: the Pro Tech spec table writes "Mangetic Pickup" instead of Magnetic. It is a copy error with no functional effect, but it shows how much a spec table can be trusted — a per-bit list beats a merged field.

Layer 3: S2, 6150 and chrome vanadium, plus what 60HRC does and does not tell you

The honest finding on steel is that one brand splits materials within its own range, which is why "steel X is simply better" does not hold up.

The iFixit Manta listing states it outright: "1/4" bits made from 6150 Steel, and 4 mm bits made from S2 Steel". Small bits are S2, large bits are 6150, in the same kit. iFixit's comparison table confirms the split across the range: Essential and Pro Tech Go are S2 Steel, Mahi is 6150 Steel, and Manta is "6150 Steel and S2 Steel". 6150 is an AISI chrome-vanadium alloy steel, so the popular claim that S2 is strictly better than chrome vanadium is contradicted by iFixit's own materials table — a larger bit carries more torque and needs toughness more than hardness.

Then look at hardness figures. STREBITO is the one kit of the five that publishes one: "The precision bits are made of 60HRC Chromium-vanadium steel". iFixit's site and listings carry no HRC number anywhere. So comparing across them needs care: one side publishes a number and the other publishes nothing, and "nothing published" does not mean "lower".

Raising hardness is not free. Better wear resistance comes with more brittleness, and the bit that snaps off inside a hole is often the hard one. I am only reporting what is checkable here: one of five publishes a hardness value and four do not, so do not read a published number as proof of superiority.

Rust belongs on this layer too. iFixit's official wiki asks you to keep the desiccant pack in the bit case and add more in humid conditions; for bits that have already rusted it suggests a vinegar or Evapo-Rust bath followed by a fresh coat of oil. Manta's Finish Type is Nickel Plating; none of the other four states a surface treatment.

Layer 4: magnetism, extensions and ESD — three places it silently stops working

Almost nobody writes about this layer, and it is where I learned the most.

Magnetism disappears through an extension. iFixit's wiki says of the 150 mm Flex Extension: "The bits are held in with friction", and "the screws will not be held to the bit due the to friction system and not magnetic". The 1/4"-to-4 mm adapter carries the same line. So a magnetic bit holder stops helping the moment you add a flexible extension or an adapter, because that section holds by friction and does not conduct magnetism. Their stated fix is to magnetise the bit itself with a separately sold magnetizer/demagnetizer.

A vendor admits magnetism carries risk. The STREBITO kit ships with that magnetizer/demagnetizer, and the listing says the tool "helps strengthen the magnetism of the screw driver tips to grab screws, or weaken it to avoid damage to your sensitive electronics". A company selling magnetic tools also tells you when to take the magnetism back off.

A third route skips magnets entirely. Wera's TORX blades are listed as "TX5, TX6 with holding function", described as gripping "the screw in the tip, so you can position it one-handed and drive it without dropping it into an open enclosure" — mechanical retention instead of magnetic.

The anti-static strap is not conductive. This is the counter-intuitive one, and iFixit's wiki is explicit: "The anti-static wrist strap is not designed to be conductive. In fact, it is designed to do the exact opposite of conducting static electricity". It bleeds charge through high resistance, and the official text stresses it only works "when used in conjunction with a ground". STREBITO's bundled strap specifies a built-in 1 MΩ resistor. Two more details from the same wiki: the nylon-tipped tweezers are marked "ESD-safe nylon tips (the metal handle is not ESD-safe)", and the metal ESD tweezers "shouldn't be used on live circuits" because they conduct tip to tip.

Decision tree: five steps to your situation

1. Write down the device, then write down its screw standard. Without this the other four steps are guesses.

2. Read the standard coverage, not the piece count: Apple and Japanese devices need Pentalobe / Y000 / JIS; European appliances and furniture need Pozidriv; if you touch both, either pick a kit covering both or plan to buy one bit separately.

3. Check the Torx gap: if your machine uses T6 or T8, confirm whether the kit has them in the TORX group or the TORX Security group.

4. Check steel and finish: prefer S2 for 4 mm bits; treat 6150 as a reasonable choice rather than a downgrade for 1/4" bits; in humid conditions look for nickel plating and a desiccant pack.

5. Check the magnetic path: if you rely on flexible extensions or adapters, accept that holding force stops at that joint and keep a magnetizer handy; if you work beside mainboards all day, consider mechanical screw retention instead.

Troubleshooting: four "bad bit" symptoms with other causes

Screws stop sticking once the extension is on. The bit did not demagnetise. The flexible extension and the 1/4" adapter hold by friction and do not conduct magnetism, per iFixit's wiki. Magnetise the bit rather than replacing it.

A cross screw rounds after two turns. Suspect a standard mismatch before a soft bit. iFixit listing Phillips and JIS side by side, and Wera building separate blades for Asian PH and JCIS 0, both say the cross slot is not one standard.

A bit snapped inside the hole. That is the brittleness side of a high hardness figure. No vendor here publishes a comparable number — only one of five states an HRC value — so do not treat a published figure as proof of strength.

Bits are rusting. iFixit's wiki asks you to keep the desiccant pack in the case, and to strip rust with vinegar or Evapo-Rust and then re-oil. That is the manufacturer's stated maintenance step, not a folk remedy.

FAQ

Q: Is a 16-bit kit enough?

A: For repeated work on one device class — screens, batteries — 16 bits usually holds up; for covering many devices it will stop at the second one. The deciding input is the screw standard from step 1, not the piece count.

Q: Can a magnetized bit damage a device?

A: Vendors disagree. STREBITO's listing says its magnetizer can "weaken it to avoid damage to your sensitive electronics", while Wera ships mechanical retention instead (TX5/TX6 with holding function). A cautious default is to use a demagnetized bit near magnetically sensitive parts.

Q: Do I need the anti-static wrist strap?

A: iFixit's wiki says the strap is "not designed to be conductive" and only works "when used in conjunction with a ground". Wearing one without a ground path does nothing.

Q: Does the lifetime guarantee cover the opening picks?

A: No. iFixit's wiki marks the Opening Tool, Opening Picks and Spudger as "Consumable - not covered under warranty", with the design intent stated as "These tools are designed to break before the device does".

Q: Which price should I trust?

A: The one on the listing you are buying from. The $79.95 and $29.95 figures here come from iFixit's own site (fetched 2026-10-23) and are not live Amazon prices; my fetch egress is not in the US, so Amazon price and stock on those pages are not evidence.
