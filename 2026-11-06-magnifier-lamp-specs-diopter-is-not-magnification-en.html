Magnifier Lamp Specs: Diopter Is Not Magnification

magnifier lampspec readingdesk gear

I clamped a lamp labelled 10X over a 0402 resistor and put the tip of my iron under it. The part was enormous. The iron was not: the glass sat two fingers above the bench, and the tip hit the rim before it reached the joint. Nothing was wrong with the lamp. Something was wrong with how I read its spec sheet.

Magnifier lamps are described with three units that look interchangeable and are not: diopter, magnification, and focal length. The same piece of glass produces three different numbers, and the listing prints whichever one sounds largest.

This is what those numbers actually mean, checked against the manufacturers' own tables, plus what happens when you use them to test a 10X claim.

TL;DR: three things to carry away. Diopter sets how close you must sit to the work (3 diopter = 333 mm, 5 diopter = 200 mm), lens diameter sets how much you can see at once, and neither substitutes for the other. The 10X and 12X labels in listings imply an F-number near 0.22 once you divide by the lens diameter, which is not a flat glass disc. And lux only means something next to a distance: 6,300 lux at 15 cm is 1,575 lux at 30 cm. If you are in a hurry: for soldering, take the Brightech LightView Pro Flex 2 in 1 in its 1.75x version; for a wide glass over a whole board, the Neatfi Elite HD 7-inch 5D; for reading markings and threading needles only, the KIRKAS high-power lamp, as long as you do not plan to work under it.

Diopter, magnification and focal length are three descriptions of one lens

Diopter is the unit optometrists use, and it is defined as the reciprocal of focal length in metres. Going from diopter to focal length is one division:

  • focal length in mm = 1000 ÷ diopter
  • 3 diopter → 333 mm → 13.1 inches
  • 5 diopter → 200 mm → 7.9 inches
  • 10 diopter → 100 mm → 3.9 inches

Magnification is a separate convention. The version used across the trade divides the 250 mm near point by the focal length and adds one, so magnification ≈ diopter ÷ 4 + 1. That is not my formula: it is the rule behind six rows of Donegan Optical's published OptiVISOR table. I actually re-ran all six rows through it, and five of them agree exactly. The sixth does not:

All six focal lengths also match 1000 ÷ diopter, with a worst-case gap of 0.88 inch from rounding to whole inches. So Donegan's own table is internally consistent on five rows and one row off.

The row that is off is the one you meet most often when buying a lamp. The other three manufacturers sit with the formula: Daylight's 5 Dioptre lens page says "5 dioptres (2.25x)", Brightech's size selector says "5 Diopter (2.25x)", and Neatfi's 7-inch glass lens page says "2.25x (5-diopter)".

So: read 5 diopter as 2.25x, and know that a 2.5x label is a different rounding convention, not a different lens. A quarter of a magnification step does not matter when you buy. It matters when you calculate working distance.

Working distance is the line between you and the iron

A magnifier has one hard constraint: the work has to sit at the focal plane or you see a blur. Focal length is therefore not a spec, it is the clearance your hands and tools get.

DiopterFocal lengthMagnificationWhat it is good for

4250 mm / 9.8 in2.00x0603 parts, connector work; tight but workable

7143 mm / 5.6 in2.75xLooking, mostly
10100 mm / 3.9 in3.50xLooking only, and from very close

Brightech states this plainly on two listings of the same lamp. The 1.75x version says "Things are in focus about 13" away". The 2.25x version says "Things are in focus about 8" away". Same 6 W, 570 lumen, 6,000 K light, same 13.5-inch gooseneck, different lens, and the working distance drops from 13 inches to 8. That single pair is the most useful sentence in this article, because it proves magnification and distance are a seesaw, not two settings you can both max out.

There is an arithmetic consequence too. Your eye has to be behind the lens, so eye-to-work distance is roughly focal length plus the distance from your eye to the glass. At 3 diopter that lands around 40 cm, which is a relaxed seated posture. At 5 diopter it lands around 25 cm, which means leaning in. Over half an hour of soldering, that difference is your neck.

Lens diameter sets the field, not the magnification

Two 5-diopter lenses, one 7 inches across and one 3 inches across, have exactly the same working distance: 200 mm. The only difference is how much you see at once.

  • Neatfi 7-inch 5D: roughly 178 mm of field
  • Brightech 3-inch 5D: roughly 76 mm of field
  • 5.44 times the area

That is why large lenses sell. They are not sharper. They are wider. With a 76 mm field you reposition the lamp for every header pin on a row. With 178 mm you see the row.

The reverse trade shows up in accessories. The Daylight Omega 7 uses a 175 mm acrylic main lens at 3 diopter (1.75x). Buy its 5-diopter replacement lens and the glass shrinks from 17.5 cm to 13 cm, so paying for more magnification also cuts the field from 175 mm to 130 mm and the working distance from 333 mm to 200 mm. Two costs, never printed together on the same page.

Testing a 10X or 12X claim against the lens it ships with

This is the method worth keeping. Given a claimed magnification and a lens diameter, you can work backwards to how fast that lens would have to be. Invert the magnification relation, D = 4 × (M − 1), get the focal length, then divide by the diameter.

The three real products land between 1.1 and 2.6, which is the normal range for a single plano-convex element. The "4-inch lens at 12x" combination requires f/0.22, meaning a focal length of 22 percent of the aperture and a radius of curvature close to the diameter. That is not a flat glass disc; that is half a sphere. Such labels either refer to a small high-power insert at the centre of the lens, or they are an "equivalent" marketing figure. Either way, the 22.7 mm focal length it implies puts the work 2.3 cm from the glass, which leaves no room for a hand or an iron.

You can check this yourself in a minute: copy the magnification and the lens diameter off any listing, divide. Above 1 is a normal magnifier. Below 0.5 means the number is not the main lens.

Lux without a distance is not a brightness claim

Lamp specs are easier to misread than lens specs, because three units look alike:

  • Lumens measure total light leaving the fixture, wherever it lands
  • Lux measures light arriving at a surface, falling off with the square of distance
  • CRI measures how faithfully the light renders colour, independent of brightness

Daylight publishes 814 lumens, 6,300 lux at 15 cm, 12.5 W and CRI 95+ for the Omega 7. The same company's fact sheet for its Slimline 4 floor lamp publishes 1,000 lumens and 3,200 lux at 30 cm. Taken at face value, 6,300 looks twice as bright as 3,200. Normalise both to the same plane and the order flips:

  • 6,300 lux at 15 cm → about 1,575 lux at 30 cm
  • 3,200 lux at 30 cm → about 12,800 lux at 15 cm

The entire apparent advantage comes from where the meter stood. So the only form of this spec I trust is one where lux is followed by a distance. "High-brightness LED" and "3x brighter" tell you nothing.

One counter-intuitive number while we are here. Brightech's 6 W fixture produces 570 lumens, or 95 lm/W. Daylight's 12.5 W fixture produces 814 lumens, or 65 lm/W. The more efficient lamp delivers less illuminance at the work, because its head is small and the light is packed into a smaller area. Lumens per watt is an electricity bill metric, not a visibility metric.

On colour, only one of these five publishes a number: Daylight at 95+. Neatfi writes "True Color" with no figure, and Brightech and KIRKAS do not mention CRI at all. For colour matching, fabric, or jewellery work, an unquantified claim is not evidence.

A fallback for field of view only

If all you need is to read silkscreen without working under the glass, the Neatfi wide-lens version balances field and clarity better than anything else here. Its problems are weight and working distance, not sharpness.

Choosing by workstation, not by headline number

  • You need to move a tool under the glass (soldering, rework, wire routing): take 3 or 4 diopter, 250 to 333 mm of working distance. A lower magnification you can work with beats a higher one you cannot. Brightech 1.75x takes the least space; Omega 7 gives the widest field and the only published CRI.
  • You only look (silkscreen, pins, threading, tracing a broken trace): 5 diopter, and the largest lens you can mount. A 178 mm field removes about half the repositioning of a 76 mm field.
  • Colour matters (matching, fabric, jewellery, judging a cold joint): only consider a model that publishes CRI. One of these five does.

One more thing that is easy to skip: measure the bench before choosing the lamp. Brightech publishes a 2.5-inch clamp limit; Daylight and Neatfi publish nothing for this. On a bench under an inch thick, a weighted base beats a bigger lens.

Five checks you can run in five minutes after delivery

  • Measure the focal length: pull a sheet of small print away from the glass and find where it is sharpest. That distance should be close to 1000 ÷ diopter, so about 333 mm at 3 diopter and 200 mm at 5. If it is far off, the diopter label and the lens are not the same thing.
  • Look at the corners: move the same sheet to the four edges of the field. If the edges blur or show colour fringing, the lens is a single element with no chromatic correction. Centre is usable; edges are indicative only.
  • Compute the F-number: diameter ÷ focal length. A normal single element sits above 1. Below 0.5 means the advertised magnification is not the main lens.
  • Check for flicker: point a phone camera in pro or slow-motion mode at the lamp. Rolling bars mean flicker, which tires eyes faster than dim light.
  • Check colour: look at white paper and a coloured cloth under the lamp, then by a window. If the cloth changes colour between the two, the lamp's colour rendering is not good enough for matching work.

FAQ

Q: Is 5 diopter 2.25x or 2.5x?

A: By the 250 mm near-point convention it is 2.25x. Donegan's OptiVISOR table prints 2.5x for its DA-5, yet the other five rows of that same table all satisfy diopter ÷ 4 + 1, so that one row is the outlier by 0.25. Daylight, Brightech and Neatfi all publish 2.25x. Budget the field at 2.25x and the working distance at 200 mm.

Q: Which diopter should I buy for 0402 work?

A: 3 diopter (1.75x, 333 mm) leaves room for the iron, and 4 diopter (2x, 250 mm) is the practical limit. At 5 diopter the glass sits 200 mm up and an angled iron hits the rim, which is exactly what happened when I mounted the lamp labelled 10X. For 0201 parts, a stereo microscope is a better spend than more magnification.

Q: What is the difference between acrylic and glass lenses?

A: Scratch resistance and weight. Daylight describes the Omega 7 lens as acrylic with an anti-scratch coating, while Neatfi, Brightech and KIRKAS specify glass. Glass resists scratching and holds optical stability better, but at 7 inches it is heavier: 8.2 lb for the glass model against roughly 3.3 kg for the acrylic one. On a bench with flux spatter and metal filings, glass ages better.

Q: Should I buy the ones labelled 10X, 12X or 30X?

A: Buy them as viewing tools, not as work tools. Divide the focal length by the lens diameter and the arithmetic does the arguing: 4 inches at 12x implies a 22.7 mm focal length, so the work sits a little over 2 cm from the glass. These are for reading small text, threading needles and inspecting stamps.

Q: Lux or lumens, which do I read?

A: Lux, and only with a distance attached. Lumens is total output; two lamps with the same lumens and different head areas produce very different illuminance at the work. Lux falls with the square of distance, so 6,300 lux at 15 cm and 3,200 lux at 30 cm are not comparable as printed, and normalised to one plane the second is brighter.

Q: Can a magnifier lamp replace a stereo microscope?

A: No. A magnifier is a single optical path with no stereo disparity, and its working distance is locked to the focal length. A stereo microscope gives binocular parallax, longer working distance and continuous zoom. For judging whether a joint is cold, or for 0201 parts, it is a different class of instrument. A magnifier lamp is a lamp with some magnification attached.
