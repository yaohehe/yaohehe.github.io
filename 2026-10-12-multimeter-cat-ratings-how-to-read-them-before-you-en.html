TL;DR: fuses first, CAT rating second, counts last

  • A CAT rating is a withstand rating, not an accuracy rating. CAT III covers upstream of fixed installation, CAT IV covers the service entrance and meter base side, where transient energy is higher. CAT IV 600V is genuinely stricter than CAT III 600V, not just a bigger number.
  • The CAT rating is ultimately cashed out by the fuses' voltage rating and breaking capacity. An independent teardown found the UNI-T UT61E+ carries a 1000V-class CAT marking on the front while its 1A and 10A fuses are rated 240V, and the PCB silkscreen says 240V too.
  • Counts only decide how small a change you can see, not whether the reading is accurate. The UT61E's 22,000 counts can display 11.983V. Its basic accuracy on that range is still ±(0.05%+5). Resolution and accuracy are separate things.
  • The four routes — Fluke 87V (industrial and motor-drive work, low-pass filter, roughly $446–485), Klein MM720 (LoZ mode, the ghost-voltage cure, roughly $95–100), Klein MM420 (around $60, best seller of the group but CAT III 600V only), UNI-T UT61E (22,000 counts plus PC data logging, out of stock and with a disputed CAT claim).
  • The Amazon Basics multimeter (DT-9561, ASIN B083V7N12H) returns 404 on amazon.com. I do not link it and do not count it in the four. It is the one product I wanted to recommend and cannot.

Why the fuse matters more than the accuracy spec

When a multimeter is measuring voltage and the circuit is miswired or hit by a transient, current has to be interrupted by a fuse. The fuse's voltage rating determines how much voltage it survives without failing; its breaking capacity determines how large a fault current it can safely interrupt. The CAT rating on the faceplate is a promise about the whole assembly protecting the operator in the stated environment, and the weakest link in that assembly is often a fuse that costs a few cents.

There is a textbook counterexample. Independent teardowner Kerry Wong documented the UNI-T UT61E+: both the 1A and 10A fuses are rated 240V at 6mm × 25mm. By contrast UNI-T's actually certified UT161E uses a 600mA 1000V fuse on the low-current range (6mm × 32mm) and an 11A 1000V fuse on the 10A range (10mm × 38mm). The same write-up notes the UT61E+ carries no UL or CE marks on the meter itself.

Two independent conclusions on the EEVblog forum are worth quoting. One is that the fuses are only 250V and lack the breaking capacity a real CAT IV 600V meter needs. The other questions the certification step directly: Intertek would not grant approvals beyond 61010 in that situation.

Put those together and the conclusion does not need hedging: when a meter's CAT marking disagrees with the voltage rating of its own fuses, believe the fuses, and treat the CAT rating as an unverified claim rather than an established fact. That is not timidity. The faceplate is marketing copy; the fuse silkscreen is a component spec.

It is worth being precise about where CAT boundaries sit. The Fluke 87V product page states that all inputs are updated to the third edition of EN 61010-1 to CAT III 1000V and CAT IV 600V, and that the meter is designed to withstand spikes above 8000V. CAT III 1000V and CAT IV 600V appearing together is not redundant labeling: the first corresponds to fixed installation at the panel, the second to the line side closer to the service entrance, and the two positions see different transient energy.

Putting accuracy back where it belongs

With safety class settled, three numbers still need to be kept apart.

Counts determine resolution. A 22,000-count meter on the 2.2000V range resolves to 0.1mV, so you can see whether a 3.3V rail reads 3.298V or 3.301V. A 4,000-count meter shows both as 3.30V. The UT61E product page's spec table spells the progression out: 4,000 counts on the UT61A/B/C, 6,000 on the UT61D, 22,000 on the UT61E and UT61E+.

Basic accuracy determines whether the reading can be trusted. It is written as ±(0.05%+5): the percentage term scales with range, and the 5 is a fixed count added at the least significant digit. On low ranges that fixed term can outweigh the flattering percentage. This is why "22,000 counts" and "high accuracy" are not synonyms.

Input impedance determines how much the meter distorts what it measures. The same teardown recorded the UT61E+ DC mV range falling from 3GΩ to 1GΩ. That is invisible on most bench work, but on sensor signals or high-impedance nodes it lands in your error budget.

Laid out side by side: 22,000 counts make a reading look fine-grained, ±(0.05%+5) determines whether you can believe it, and the fuse voltage rating determines whether it survives the mistake. None substitutes for another.

Troubleshooting: four failure modes I would actually hit

Mistake one: treating three consistent readings as accuracy. Reading the same meter three times in the same spot will agree, which proves the meter works, not that it is right. Verifying accuracy requires a known source, such as a reference voltage source of known precision or an already-calibrated meter to compare against.

Mistake two: plugging leads in before selecting the range. Seating probes in the wrong jack while the circuit is live is the most common and most avoidable way to destroy a multimeter. Both the 87V and the MM420 alert you with an LED or buzzer on misplacement (Lead Alert / Input Alert), and the MM720 carries the equivalent lead-alert design. Of everything in these four meters, that is the feature I would rank highest — worth more than an extra range.

Mistake three: letting the CAT marking stand in for a safety judgment. Back to section one: the CAT label is a promise, the fuse silkscreen is a fact. If you will be doing high-voltage work where nobody is checking over your shoulder, the only responsible approach is to select on the most conservative rating available and to open and inspect any meter of uncertain provenance.

Mistake four: selling a cheap meter's high count as precision. All four mistakes orbit one root cause: resolution, accuracy, and safety are three orthogonal dimensions, and treating one label as the whole conclusion is the most expensive mistake in multimeter shopping.

FAQ

Q1: What is the actual difference between CAT II and CAT III?

CAT III applies to fixed installation, such as a fixed branch circuit inside a panel. CAT IV applies at the service entrance, on the line side, and immediately around the meter base. The latter sits closer to the supply and sees higher transient energy, which is why the requirements are stricter at the same voltage. The short version: a CAT IV meter can do CAT III work, not the other way around.

Q2: Does a higher count always mean a better meter?

No. Counts only raise resolution. What determines trustworthiness is the percentage and fixed-count terms in the basic accuracy spec, plus whether the input impedance suits the circuit. A 40,000-count meter rated ±(1.0%+10) can be worse in practice than a 4,000-count meter rated ±(0.05%+2).

Q3: Can these meters measure temperature?

Only the Fluke 87V bundles an 80BK probe covering -200°C to 1090°C out of the box. The MM720 and MM420 both list temperature measurement and ship a thermocouple, while the UNI-T UT61E lacks it, which multiple third-party reviews list as a genuine shortcoming. If you routinely check heatsinks, motor bearings, or case intake temperature, confirm the model has a temperature channel before buying.

Q4: Is True RMS actually necessary?

Only when the signal is not a pure sine wave. Switch-mode power supplies, variable-frequency motor drives, LED dimmers, and PWM audio all produce distorted waveforms. An average-responding meter reads these low and sends your entire diagnosis in the wrong direction.

Q5: How do I confirm a ghost voltage?

Use a meter with LoZ mode such as the MM720: switch to LoZ and if the reading collapses toward zero it was capacitive coupling; if it holds steady, the voltage is real. Without LoZ you can put a resistor of known value in parallel across the point under test and watch whether the reading is pulled down. It is a crude method, but the conclusion holds.
