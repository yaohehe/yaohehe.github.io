Left-Handed Mouse: Three Numbers That Are Not Mirrored

left-handed-mouseergonomic-mouselogitech

Using a right-handed mouse with your left hand is not about grip. It is about spending eight hours a day with your wrist held in internal rotation. The real problem starts earlier, though: for most left-handed users, the model that fits does not exist as a SKU in their region.

I found this the tedious way. I went through the official spec pages, commercial data sheets and part-number tables at Logitech, Kensington, Perixx and Elecom (fetched 2026-11-05 to 2026-11-11). The traps are not in the marketing copy about comfort. They are in three columns of the spec table: part-number coverage, button count and tilt angle. All three disagree with themselves inside the manufacturers' own documents.

Why the word "mirror" misleads you

Turn a right-handed mouse over and the shape does look flipped. But a product is not a shape. A left-handed SKU needs new tooling, a new side-button PCB, new packaging and documentation, and new regional certification. Whichever of those costs more than expected is the one that gets cut first.

The Logitech Lift commercial data sheet (ergo-lift-b2b-data-sheet.pdf, fetched 2026-11-11) contains a Part Number table with 13 rows. It is the most useful table in this article, because it tells you where the left-handed version sits commercially more directly than any review can.

Trap 1: 13 part numbers, 2 left-handed, 2 regions

  • Taiwan and South Korea: 910-006499 Graphite, 910-006500 Off-White
  • China: 910-006501 Graphite, 910-006502 Off-White
  • Japan: 910-006503 Graphite

The left-handed version occupies 2 part numbers, both Graphite, both inside SAMR and EMEA. The AP, Taiwan/Korea, China and Japan tables have no left-handed column at all.

Then look at the same company's China site. The Lift commercial comparison table there states "left-handed use: available (Graphite)*" and "MX Vertical: not applicable". So the part-number table says China has no left-handed SKU, and the China comparison table says it does. The asterisk footnote was not expanded on the page I fetched.

When two official statements collide, there is only one workable rule: trust the part number you can actually get on the order page, not the comparison table. If you are buying a locally warrantied left-handed unit in China, confirm first that you can get 910-006492 or 910-006495. If you cannot, what you are buying is cross-border or third-party stock.

(An earlier piece on choosing a vertical mouse by hand size documented the other half of this: MX Vertical has no left-handed variant, so left-handed users land on Lift's separate model. The two articles read well together.)

Trap 2: one model number, six buttons or four

Logitech publishes two support spec pages, and both list the model number as MR0094:

  • Right-handed page (support.logi.com article 17743180034327): 6 buttons (left/right click, back/forward, middle button, scroll wheel with middle click), weight 125 g, sensor listed as Logitech Advanced Optical Tracking
  • Left-handed page (article 5127268456727): 4 buttons + Connectivity button, weight 124 g, sensor listed as Pluto One

Same model number, two buttons fewer, one gram lighter, and two different sensor names. The left-handed page also contradicts itself internally: the Buttons row says 4, but the durability table further down still lists "Buttons (Forward and Backward): 500K clicks". If there is no forward/back button, that row has no subject.

Two more documents, two more answers:

  • The commercial data sheet says "Four, easy to reach customizable side buttons"
  • The Amazon listing title says "4 Buttons"

Four versions: 6 buttons, 4 plus a connectivity button, four side buttons, 4 Buttons. You cannot establish from official documents which two buttons the left-handed version drops. This is the one question I could not close in this round of research, so I am not going to write "the left-handed version loses forward and back" — it may well be true, but the manufacturer did not say it clearly and I will not say it for them.

What to do instead: before ordering, find a top-down or side photo of the actual unit and count the buttons on both flanks, or buy through a channel with a return window. If your workflow depends on side-button shortcuts, do not gamble.

Trap 3: "vertical" is not one angle — 41° and 57° are 16° apart

Most people assume vertical mice are one category and differ only by brand. The four manufacturers disagree:

  • Logitech Lift left-handed: 57° (data sheet: "optimal 57° angle"; China comparison table: "natural grip, 57 degrees")
  • Kensington Pro Fit Left-Handed: 41° (official wording: "41º inclined angle", described as a "Neutral handshake grip")
  • Perixx 713L and 513L: no angle published at all, only "Slightly tilted left shape"
  • Elecom EX-G left-handed: not a vertical mouse at all — a thumb trackball, where tilt is not a meaningful axis

16° is not a rounding difference. 41° is closer to half-upright: a substantial part of your palm still carries weight on the desk. 57° is closer to a handshake, with the heel of the hand largely off the surface. The same phrase — ergonomic vertical mouse — describes two different pressure distributions on your wrist.

There is a detail worth noticing here. Kensington calls 41° a handshake grip; Logitech calls 57° a handshake position. The same words, applied by two companies to angles 16° apart. If a page says "handshake" and gives no number, you have learned nothing.

"Adjustable DPI" means three different things

  • Logitech Lift left-handed: 400–4000, continuously adjustable in 100-DPI increments
  • Kensington Pro Fit Left-Handed: 800 / 1200 / 1600, three steps
  • Perixx 713L: 800 / 1200 / 1600, three steps
  • Perixx 513L: 1000 / 1600, two steps
  • Elecom EX-G left-handed: 750 / 1500, two steps

Every listing says "adjustable DPI", but "which values can you actually land on" is a different question. If you work at 2400 on a large display, only the Lift reaches 2400; the three-step models give you 1600 or 1200, and the two-step models give you a choice of two.

There is no right or wrong here, only fit. "Does it support DPI adjustment" is a useless question. "Which numbers can it land on, and at what increment" is the useful one. The 2026 productivity mouse comparison uses the same framing.

Which means any sentence of the form "this one is 30 grams lighter" cannot be supported by official data. If weight matters to you, put it on a scale.

Discontinued, out of stock, on sale: three pages, three answers

Kensington's US product page displays three statements at once:

1. "This product is no longer available. Please click on the replacement SKU."

2. "Discontinued"

3. "Item(s) unavailable. Expected in stock 12/05/2026"

The same page says the product is discontinued and that it is expected back on 5 December. The South Africa site, same SKU, says "Expected in stock 12/03/2026" — two days apart between regional sites. And on Amazon the same mouse shows $36.99 and In Stock.

(This site hit the opposite failure in October: on Audio-Technica's pages a Discontinued string turned out to be an empty site-wide template label, which a control group of two other products disproved. This case is different — the status here comes with specific copy and a replacement SKU, alongside a restock date. Real status text fighting marketing copy, not an empty label.)

The conclusion: a manufacturer's status label cannot stand alone as evidence. To decide whether a left-handed mouse is still buyable, check three places — the manufacturer's status, the retailer's stock, and whether a replacement SKU exists. When they disagree, trust "can I order it right now, and how long until it ships".

Troubleshooting: five checks before you click buy

1. Part number. Is the left-handed version a separate PN? If only the colour differs and the PN matches, it is not a left-handed version.

2. Buttons. Do the official "number of buttons" row and the durability table agree? If not, go find a photo of the physical button layout.

3. Angle. Is there a published angle number? "Ergonomic tilt" with no number is no information.

4. Connection. Bluetooth, or 2.4GHz only? Switching between machines needs Bluetooth or a dedicated switch button.

5. Battery and hand size. One AA or two AAA, and are they included? How far is the rated hand-size band from your own palm length?

If any of these five cannot be answered, do not read it as "the manufacturer keeps specs secret". Read it as "this model is not safe to buy blind".

FAQ

Q: Can a left-handed person just use a right-handed mouse?

Short term, yes. Long term, it depends on volume. The problem is not that you cannot reach the buttons; it is sustained internal rotation of the wrist and extra load on the thumb side. If you use a mouse more than four hours a day and already feel wrist fatigue, changing the hand position helps more than changing the brand. If you use one occasionally, do not spend money on this yet.

Q: Why do left-handed versions have fewer colours, fewer part numbers, and constant stock issues?

Because the left-handed version is added as a SKU, not built as a product line. Of Lift's 13 part numbers, 2 are left-handed, both Graphite, and the AP, Taiwan/Korea, China and Japan tables have none. Kensington ships exactly one left-handed model and has already marked it Discontinued. That is not a slight aimed at anyone; it is what happens to a small SKU. Which is also why, when you find a left-handed model that fits, you should not wait for the next generation.

Q: 41° or 57° — how do I choose?

Start from where your wrist hurts. If the outer edge of your palm (the little-finger side) takes the pressure and you want the heel of your hand off the desk, go toward 57°. If you want to keep some palm support and find fully upright hard to control, 41° is steadier. Both companies call their own angle a handshake, which is why the words are useless and only the number helps. Expect your accuracy to drop for the first two weeks either way.

Q: Is the Renewed left-handed Lift on Amazon worth buying?

In the listings I pulled, the Renewed unit was $52.89 with one left in stock, and the new one was $63.99 In Stock — an $11 gap. The risk is not the mouse. It is that Graphite is the only official left-handed colour, so you need to confirm from the seller's notes and photos that the colour matches, that the battery compartment has not been swapped, and that the Logi Bolt receiver is the original. Left-handed models are hard enough to find that Renewed is a supplement, not a substitute. Order with a return window you can live with.

What this article does and does not do

It does: cross-check four manufacturers' official spec pages, commercial data sheets, part-number tables and Amazon listings, surface three places where the manufacturers contradict themselves (button count, weight, part-number coverage), and list the real shortcoming of each of the five buyable models.

It does not: I do not own these mice. Every number here is a manufacturer claim, not a measurement. Weight, grip feel and click quality are things only your hand can settle, so this article draws no conclusion about them. Prices and stock are from 2026-11-11 and will change. Ambidextrous-shell models are out of scope — those require checking each official button map to see whether both flanks carry side buttons, which is a separate article. Driver-level remapping differences across the four vendors are also not covered.

If you remember one thing: with left-handed mice, read the part number first, the angle second, and the brand last.
