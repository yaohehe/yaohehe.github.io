Dynamic vs Condenser: Swapping Mics Won't Fix Your Room

MicrophonesDynamicCondenser

An untreated room records hollow. It is the question I get asked most this year, and the reflex answer is almost always "change the microphone": the dynamic camp says a condenser pulls the whole room in, the condenser camp says a dynamic sounds muffled. I actually opened all five official spec pages and the five Amazon listings myself and copied every number that could be matched onto one sheet, and the conclusion turned out to be worth more than the swap itself: changing the microphone does not make the room quieter. It only moves the noise to a different stage of the chain, where it gets amplified.

And once the five spec sheets are laid side by side, only two sets of numbers can be subtracted at all. The rest look comparable and are not.

A new mic does not change the room, it relocates the noise

Start with the part that has nothing to do with brands. Reflections are a property of the space: walls, desk, ceiling and the distance between your mouth and the capsule. Swapping the microphone leaves every reflection exactly where it was. What changes is the magnitude of two other things.

The first is how much preamp gain you need. More gain means the preamp's own noise gets amplified with the signal; less gain means that noise contributes proportionally less. The second is how quiet a signal the microphone itself can resolve, published as self-noise or equivalent noise. On a spec sheet these sit in two different rows, and they act at opposite ends of the chain: one at the input transducer, one downstream in the preamp.

So "the room sounds hollow" is really three problems wearing one sentence: room reverberation, preamp noise, and microphone self-noise. A spec sheet answers the last two. The first one is answered by positioning and soft furnishings.

There is also a question a spec sheet never answers: between two microphones both marked Cardioid, which one rejects the room better. On all three official spec pages I checked, the polar pattern row contains the single word "Cardioid" and no polar plot data. Dynamic marketing copy says the tight pattern "helps to reject ambient noise" (RØDE's Procaster page). Condenser copy says the capsule is "extremely sensitive — picks up the subtlest sounds in any environment" (Audio-Technica's own definition block). Both statements describe two sides of the same property, and neither is quantifiable.

Shure SM7B: the manufacturer wrote down "60 dB of gain"

This is the default answer across the podcast world whenever a room is noisy. The informative part of its official page is not the tonal description but the last two sentences of the product copy: "The SM7B is a professional dynamic microphone that operates best with preamps that provide 60dB of gain. Please ensure your audio interface can provide sufficient gain or you may find our SM7dB is a better option with a builtin preamp."

Of the five reviewed here, Shure is the only brand that turns the preamp requirement into a hard number. And 60 dB is not decorative: many entry-level interfaces top out somewhere between 40 and 60 dB of analogue gain, and the exact figure is on the interface's own spec page. If yours delivers 40 dB, the missing 20 dB has to come from software gain — which amplifies everything already recorded into the chain, preamp noise included.

Real defects: no published sensitivity, self-noise or maximum SPL, so you cannot work out how far it sits from a 4 dBA condenser in a quiet room; no built-in preamp, so a gain shortfall means a new interface; no switches at all, so pad and low cut have to be solved upstream.

Shure SM7dB: $90 more buys you a 48V dependency

The SM7dB is the same microphone with a preamp inside. Shure's page states "+18dB or +28dB of extra gain", with a bypass. It addresses exactly the 60 dB problem above: when the interface cannot supply the gain, lift the level before the preamp.

Then comes the sentence most people skip: "All you need is an interface or mixer offering +48V of phantom power to operate the preamp." The +18 or +28 dB preamp only works when 48V phantom power is present. This is the most counterintuitive finding in this piece. Dynamic microphones are defined by not needing phantom power — Audio-Technica's own definition block lists "No external power requirements" as a dynamic characteristic — yet this upgraded dynamic adds a phantom power dependency in order to fix a gain problem.

If your interface has no 48V, or the switch is broken, or the rail is already committed elsewhere, the preamp is decoration and the mic reverts to passive behaviour, identical to an SM7B.

Real defects: $90.00 more than the SM7B ($459.00 versus $369.00); the preamp depends on 48V; and the line "all the extra gain you'll ever need" is marketing, not a specification — the sheet still publishes no sensitivity or self-noise, so you still cannot compare it numerically with a condenser.

RØDE Procaster: a -56 dB broadcast dynamic with the plainest sheet

The Procaster has the most complete spec page of the five, even though it too omits self-noise and maximum SPL: dynamic, cardioid, end address, 75 Hz to 18 kHz, output impedance 320Ω, sensitivity -56.0 dB re 1 Volt/Pascal (1.60 mV at 94 dB SPL), plus or minus 2 dB.

That "at 94 dB SPL" is the load-bearing detail. 94 dB SPL equals one pascal, which is the reference point behind every sensitivity figure and, in the next section, behind the self-noise arithmetic too.

Audio-Technica AT2020: 20 dB(A), the highest self-noise here

The AT2020 page contradicts itself in a way worth reading closely. The marketing block promises "reliable performance without any unwanted background noise", while the official comparison table on the same page lists its self-noise at 20 dB(A) — the highest of the five.

There is no real contradiction; "without unwanted background noise" describes what its pattern picks up, not its own floor. And 20 dB(A) is perfectly usable in a quiet studio. In your living room it is a different conversation.

The AT2020 is also useful as a ruler. The AT2035 publishes 12 dB(A) self-noise and 82 dB(A) signal-to-noise ratio; the AT2020 publishes 20 dB(A) and 74 dB(A). Both pairs satisfy the same identity: 94 minus the SNR equals the self-noise (94 - 82 = 12, 94 - 74 = 20). That means the two fields in the official table are internally consistent and can check each other. And the 94 is the same one-pascal reference from the section above.

Real defects: the highest self-noise in this comparison; the official feature list shows no pad and no low cut, so high SPL or close-mic bass buildup has to be handled upstream; it is side-address, which is less flexible to place than an end-address mic; and it needs 48V phantom power to operate.

Why those 16 dB do not cash out in a real room

Now put both sets of numbers against the same problem.

A self-noise figure of 4 dB(A) means that with no external sound at all, the microphone's output carries a noise floor equivalent to 4 dB SPL. Same for 20 dB(A). The gap is 16 dB.

The problem is that "no external sound at all" clause. Your room has an air conditioner, a fan, a window and a neighbour. Converted to the microphone position, all of that sits well above 20 dB(A). Once the room floor exceeds the microphone's self-noise, the room is what limits the signal-to-noise ratio, not the microphone. The premium you paid for 4 dBA does not cash out in that room; it only pays off in a space where the floor has been pushed far lower.

That is the arithmetic version of "a new mic will not fix the room": a quietness spec only means something when compared against the room floor; on its own it means nothing. I deliberately do not give a room-floor number here, because rooms differ too much for any single figure to be honest. Measure yours once with a phone sound level meter or a handheld meter at the desk.

The dynamic side has the opposite problem, and it is not self-noise but gain. Sensitivity 24 dB lower means the preamp must open 24 dB wider, which amplifies preamp noise by the same amount — while the reflections do not disappear. They only become relatively smaller, and that relative change appears nowhere on a spec sheet, because distance and pattern decide it, not the model number.

Work backwards from your room and your interface in three steps

Step one: measure the room. Take a sound level reading at the desk. If it sits an order of magnitude above the candidate microphones' self-noise, that column has almost no discriminating power for you and is not worth a premium.

Step two: check the interface. Find the analogue gain ceiling on its official spec page and compare it with Shure's 60 dB threshold for the SM7B. If the shortfall is large, look at a version with a built-in preamp such as the SM7dB (and confirm the interface supplies 48V) rather than patching it with software gain.

Step three: look at the switches. An untreated room benefits most from low cut and a pad. The AT2035 officially lists an 80 Hz high-pass and a 10 dB pad; the AT2020 feature list shows neither. This is the one row that can be compared directly across models, and it is more useful than sensitivity.

FAQ

Q: My room is echoey. Will a dynamic microphone help?

A: It changes the gain requirement and the microphone's own noise floor; it does not change reflections. Echo comes off the walls and ceiling, and every spec sheet here prints the word "Cardioid" with no polar plot, so you cannot tell from the sheet which of two cardioids rejects the room better. What does help: speak closer, aim the mic away from the main reflecting surface, and add soft material to the side walls.

Q: Can I hear the difference between 4 dB(A) and 20 dB(A) at home?

A: Only when the room floor is below both numbers. Once the room floor is above 20 dB(A), the room sets the signal-to-noise ratio and both microphones sound equally "noisy". Measure the room first, then decide whether low self-noise deserves a premium.

Q: Will 48V phantom power damage a dynamic microphone?

A: By Audio-Technica's own definition block, dynamics have "no external power requirements". The exception here is the SM7dB, whose built-in preamp officially needs "an interface or mixer offering +48V of phantom power". In other words, a dynamic only wants 48V when a preamp inside it needs to run.

Q: My interface only has 40 dB of gain. Can I still use the SM7B?

A: Shure recommends 60 dB and points to the SM7dB in the same paragraph. If you close the 20 dB gap in software, you amplify everything already captured, preamp noise included. Check the analogue gain ceiling first, then decide whether to change the microphone or the interface.

Closing

In an untreated room, a new microphone changes how much gain the preamp must supply and how quiet a signal the capsule can resolve. It does not change reflections. The only two columns you can subtract on a spec sheet are self-noise and sensitivity, and they act at opposite ends of the chain — mix them together and you reach the wrong conclusion.
