Open-Back Headphones 2026: Ohms, dB/mW, Amps

An open-back studio headphone listing usually gives you three lines of specification: impedance, sensitivity, frequency response. The second line is the one that decides whether your interface can drive them, and it is also the line every manufacturer writes differently. Sennheiser prints "106 dB SPL (1kHz/1Vrms)". AKG prints "104 dB SPL/V @ 1 kHz". Audio-Technica prints "99 dB" and stops. Philips prints no reference at all.

So the same question gets three different answers.

Impedance and sensitivity answer two different questions

Impedance answers "how much voltage does this power require". Sensitivity answers "how much power does this sound pressure require". Keep them apart and the numbers line up:

  • Power needed: P = 10^((110 - S) / 10) in milliwatts, where S is sensitivity converted to dB/mW.
  • Voltage needed: V = √(P / 1000 × R) in volts rms, where R is rated impedance.

The DT 990 PRO prints 250 ohms, which reads as intimidating. The K240 Studio prints 55 ohms, which reads as easy. By the official numbers the second one needs 72.4 mW at 110 dB and the first needs 25.1 mW. The 4.5x higher impedance is the easier load, because its sensitivity is 4.6 dB higher and every 3 dB is a doubling of power.

The same table holds the opposite case. The SHP9500 sits at 32 ohms with 101 dB and becomes the cheapest load of the six at 0.50 V. The HD 600 sits at 300 ohms and 91.8 dB/mW and needs 4.47 V, which is 8.9 times the SHP9500. "High impedance is hard to drive" only survives while sensitivity stays roughly equal.

About the 110 dB target: it comes from 85 dB average plus 25 dB of transient headroom. That is this article's own convention, not a manufacturer standard. Move the target to 105 or 115 dB and every figure shifts together while the ranking stays put.

The conversion between the two references depends only on impedance: dB/mW = dB SPL/V + 10 × log10(R / 1000). For the HD 599 at 50 ohms that is 106 - 13.0 = 93.0. For the HD 600 at 300 ohms it is 97 - 5.2 = 91.8. Both manufacturers print the reference in brackets, so both can be checked.

The three that print no reference are the problem. Audio-Technica's table says "Sensitivity 99 dB" and nothing else. beyerdynamic writes "Nominal sound pressure level 96 dB". Philips' official PDF says "Sensitivity: 101 dB". None of them says whether that is at 1 mW or at 1 V.

There is a consistency check available: compute both readings and see which one contradicts another number on the same sheet.

  • SHP9500: read as dB/V at 32 ohms, it becomes 86.1 dB/mW and needs 251 mW for 110 dB, while the same datasheet rates maximum input power at 200 mW. It cannot ask for more power than it is rated to survive, so the dB/mW reading is the only coherent one.
  • DT 990 PRO: read as dB/V it becomes 90.0 dB/mW and needs 100.5 mW at 110 dB, which lands exactly on the 100 mW power handling in beyerdynamic's own datasheet. No contradiction, just a perfect fit. Both readings survive, so this article marks it unresolved.
  • ATH-M50x: read as dB/V it needs 407 mW against a 1,600 mW rating. Again no contradiction, so the check cannot decide.

Four of six resolve cleanly; two can only be given as a range. Printing the range is more useful than pretending to know. To settle it before buying, ask the manufacturer or read the footnotes of the datasheet.

  • The extremes differ by 9.1x in power (72.4 / 7.9). That ratio decides whether you need a separate amp. The impedance spread is 9.4x (300 / 32) and explains less.
  • The two lowest-impedance models are the easiest loads. SHP9500 and M50x both sit under 13 mW. Impedance and difficulty move together at the ends of this table and break apart in the middle, where the K240 outranks the DT 990 PRO.

Working backwards from the device: how far an entry interface gets

The headphones' demand is only half of it. Take the Focusrite Scarlett Solo 4th Gen, because its official spec page is unusually explicit about the headphone output:

  • Maximum output level: 2.5 dBu into 33 Ω, 10 dBu into 300 Ω
  • Maximum output power: 32 mW into 33 Ω, 22 mW into 300 Ω
  • Output impedance: 50 Ω

Converted: 10 dBu = 0.775 × 10^(10/20) = 2.451 V; 2.5 dBu = 1.033 V. Then line them up:

One detail that gets skipped: the two 300-ohm figures do not reconcile. 2.451 V across 300 ohms is 20.0 mW, while the same table claims 22 mW into 300 ohms. A 10% gap means the two numbers come from different conditions, one a voltage limit and one a power limit. Quote "about 2.5 V" and do not back-calculate voltage from 22 mW.

The second detail is output impedance. At the published 50 ohms, damping factor (load divided by source) works out to 0.64 for the SHP9500, 1.1 for the K240, 0.76 for the M50x, 5.0 for the DT 990 PRO and 6.0 for the HD 600. Below 1, the amplifier has less control over the driver than the driver's own resistance, and the frequency response follows the headphone's impedance curve. That is why the same 32-ohm headphone can sound bass-heavy on one jack and thin on another, while high-impedance loads stay steady.

So "do I need an amp" is really "can my output deliver the voltage this headphone asks for". On the official numbers the whole question collapses into the 5 dB between 2.45 V and 4.47 V.

Open versus closed is a leakage decision, not a sound-quality decision

This part is not electrical, but it decides whether the purchase works. beyerdynamic's own FAQ on the DT 990 PRO page is direct: for use with a microphone they recommend closed-back headphones, because those isolate better from external noise and reduce leakage into the mic; open-back models offer a wider, more natural soundstage but may bleed into sensitive microphones and cause feedback at high volumes.

Audio-Technica describes the ATH-M50x from the other side: a closed-back design that gives "excellent sound isolation in loud environments". Put the two statements together and you get a rule you can act on:

  • Listening while your mic is open in the same room (podcast, calls, screen recording): open-back leaks, so choose closed.
  • Shared office or dorm where you need to block other people: closed.
  • Listening alone to check mix detail: the open-back soundstage argument finally applies.

Neither manufacturer publishes an isolation figure in dB, so nothing quantitative belongs here. Anyone quoting "open-back leaks -25 dB" should be asked where that number came from.

Three practical details that get skipped

  • Earpads wear out, and the three brands document it differently. beyerdynamic lists spare part EDT 990 V (order number 926.679), AKG's spec says replaceable earpads: yes, and neither Audio-Technica nor Sennheiser lists the item on the spec page. Decide now how you will replace them in two years.
  • The cable connector decides whether an off-the-shelf cable works. The DT 990 PRO is fixed and needs service if it fails; the K240 uses mini-XLR; the HD 599 uses a proprietary detachable cable; the M50x ships with three detachable cables and is the easiest to replace.
  • Maximum input power is not a requirement. The range across these six is 100 mW to 1,600 mW, a 16x spread, and it only says how much input the driver survives. Using it as a difficulty metric produces the opposite conclusion.

FAQ

Is 250 ohms always harder to drive than 32 ohms? No. On official numbers the 250 Ω DT 990 PRO needs 25.1 mW and the 55 Ω K240 Studio needs 72.4 mW, so the lower-impedance model is the heavier load. Difficulty follows converted sensitivity; impedance only converts power into voltage.

Can a laptop or phone drive the HD 600 directly? At this article's target the HD 600 needs 4.47 V, and most motherboards and laptops publish no headphone output level at all, so the claim cannot be checked. If you only have a 3.5 mm jack, the HD 599 class is the safer purchase.

Is 106 dB easier to drive than 96 dB? Depends on the reference. The HD 599's 106 dB is measured at 1 V and converts to 93.0 dB/mW, while the DT 990 PRO's 96 dB reads higher once converted to dB/mW. Subtracting the two printed numbers is meaningless.

Will open-back headphones leak into my recordings? Yes. beyerdynamic's own FAQ says open models bleed into sensitive microphones and can cause feedback at high volume, and recommends closed-back for mic work. For podcasting or calls, a closed model like the ATH-M50x fits better.

Do I need a separate headphone amp? Compute the voltage your headphone needs, then compare it with what your output publishes. A few dB short is survivable; 5 dB short, as with the HD 600 on an entry interface, means a different output stage. Buy amps by voltage, not by impedance.
