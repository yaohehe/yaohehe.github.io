Chip Quik Removal Alloy: 58°C vs 79-91°C, Two Kits

The number that gets misread in desoldering work is rarely the iron setting. It is the little block of alloy in your hand: how low is "low temperature", actually. One box, two melting points, both printed by the maker

The Chip Quik store page for SMD1 carries exactly one temperature line: "Chip Quik Alloy Melting Point: 58 Celsius (136 F)". The lead-free sibling, SMD1NL, replaces that line with "Chip Quik Lead-Free Alloy Melting Point: 79-91 Celsius (174-195 F)", and the description adds "Same as the SMD1 but with Lead-Free Chip Quik Alloy".

Same page layout, same accessory list, same SMD291 flux in the box. Only the alloy changes. And when it changes, the melting point moves from 58 to a 79-91 range: 21°C higher at the start, 33°C higher at the end.

TL;DR: ① 58°C is the melting point of the leaded removal alloy; 79-91°C is the lead-free version. Same kit, two versions, 21-33°C apart. ② 58 is a point, 79-91 is a range 12°C wide. The same vendor writes "eutectic" explicitly on its 138°C paste, so the distinction is not lost on them. ③ The 138°C SMDLTLFP is not a removal alloy. It is a solder paste, Sn42/Bi57.6/Ag0.4, 87% metal by weight, and it is meant to stay on the board. ④ The lead-free kit costs exactly $1 more ($17 vs $16). What you buy for that dollar is compliance, paid for with a higher and wider melt. ⑤ SRA's competing FAST CHIP page says only "same alloy as the leading brand, lower cost" and gives neither melting point nor composition — readable for price, useless for temperature.

58 is a point. 79-91 is a range.

Whether a melting point is written as a single number or a span is not a typesetting choice. It is the shape of the alloy. A single number means the solidus and liquidus coincide: the alloy goes from solid to liquid at that one temperature, which is what eutectic means. A span means the two lines separate, and inside those 12°C the alloy sits half-solid.

The vendor knows the difference well enough to print "Melts 138C 281F eutectic" on the paste page. On the two removal-alloy pages it gives 58 for one and 79-91 for the other, with no explanation of why one is a point and the other a span.

The practical consequence is one sentence long: with the lead-free alloy, "starts to soften" and "fully liquid" are not the same moment. If you wait the way you wait with the leaded version, at 79°C the alloy has already gone soft but still has the rest of a 12°C mushy zone to cross. Push a component inside that window and the joint tends to string out instead of releasing.

One caveat worth stating plainly: the Chip Quik pages give no recommended iron temperature or dwell time for the removal alloys. I am not inventing one. That is a gap in the evidence, and I would rather name it than write around it.

The 138°C product is a solder, not a removal alloy

SMDLTLFP has an entirely different spec block: Alloy Sn42/Bi57.6/Ag0.4, Flux Type Synthetic No-Clean, Metal Content 87% metal by weight, Particle Size T3 (25-45 microns), Melting Point 138C (281F), Size 5cc/15g syringe. The same page states the shelf life: more than 6 months refrigerated, more than 2 months unrefrigerated.

The dividing line between this and a removal alloy is not temperature. It is whether the joint is meant to survive. The vendor files it as Solder Paste, with the intended use written as soldering surface-mount components to a PCB. Removal alloy lives under SMD Removal Kit, and the kit ships with alcohol pads.

There is a pricing detail that is easy to miss. The same paste is sold as a 10cc/20g T5 version at 67% metal content for $39.50, which works out to roughly $1.98 per gram, against $1.06 per gram for the 87% version at $15.95 for 15g. Twenty percentage points of metal content is a large difference in flux volume, and neither version's page publishes a recommended reflow profile.

Chip Quik SMD1: 58°C is why this box sells

The official kit is 2.5 ft of alloy plus SMD291 tack flux plus alcohol pads, at $16.00 (at the time of writing). Melting point 58°C (136°F). Shelf life appears in two separate lines: more than 24 months refrigerated, more than 24 months unrefrigerated.

The weakness is printed on the label. This is a leaded alloy, and the page carries a California Prop 65 warning verbatim: "This product can expose you to Lead, which is known to the State of California to cause Cancer, Birth Defects, or other Reproductive Harm." For reworking a board that has to be lead-free, this is the easier alloy thermally and the harder one legally.

The listing is here, and its title spells out all three accessories: 2.5ft., flux, alcohol pads. You can tell what is in the box from the title alone.

Chip Quik SMD1NL: same box, melting point becomes 79-91°C

Official price $17.00, one dollar above the leaded kit, with three compliance lines at the top of the page: Lead-Free / RoHS 3 Compliant / REACH Compliant. Accessories, flux and packaging match SMD1.

The weakness is the range itself. It starts melting at 79°C and finishes at 91°C, and there is no second product in the family to compare that 12°C width against, because the leaded version publishes a single number. Whatever operating difference you hit on the lead-free kit cannot be validated by switching brands. And the word "lead-free" is not a synonym for "cold": a 79°C start sits 21°C above the leaded version, near the tolerance edge of many heat-sensitive parts.

Chip Quik SMDLTLFP: a 138°C paste that is allowed to stay

Official price $15.95 for a 5cc / 15g syringe, T3 particle size 25-45 microns, 87% metal content, eutectic at 138°C.

The weakness is that the handling requirements are stricter than for a removal alloy. The vendor requires refrigeration at 3-8°C (37-46°F), no freezing, and the paste must be brought to 20-25°C before use, which takes four hours. Shelf life shrinks accordingly: more than 6 months refrigerated, more than 2 months at room temperature. A 15g syringe has a much shorter usable window than a length of alloy.

The listing is here, and the title states 138C 281F directly.

This is the flux that ships inside the kits: SMD291 tack flux no-clean. The 10cc syringe lists at $15.95; the 75g jar, SMD29175G, lists at $63.95 with a stated Flux Classification of REL0 and Synthetic No-Clean.

Decision tree: three boards, three temperatures

  • Board must be lead-free / RoHS: take SMD1NL and read it as a 79-91 range, not as a 58-style number.
  • Leaded board, you just need the part off: SMD1 at 58°C is the cheapest on thermal budget, and the cost is lead in the residue.
  • You have to solder something back in place: a removal alloy and a solder paste are different products. Going back on the board means a paste such as SMDLTLFP, where 138°C is a eutectic point and there is no mushy zone to cross.

The earlier piece on solder wire specs already covered the official figures for eutectic Sn63Pb37 at 183°C and SAC305 at 217-220°C, so I will not repeat them here. What this article adds is the layer underneath: why a removal alloy can sit more than a hundred degrees below those numbers. It was never meant to become a joint.

The division of labour between wick and pump is covered in the desoldering wick article and the desoldering pump article: one absorbs, one extracts, and the alloy's job is to turn the joint liquid first.

Five checks before you order

  • Confirm the compliance requirement first. On a lead-free process, SMD1 is a residue problem, not a temperature problem.
  • Confirm whether you need to remove or to solder. Only Solder Paste leaves a joint; a Removal Kit does not.
  • Read whether the melting point is a point or a range, and give yourself margin at both ends of a range.
  • Check the accessories. A kit with flux and alcohol pads saves a second order; both SMD1 and SMD1NL include them.
  • Check price and shelf life together. Paste lasts 2 months at room temperature; alloy lasts 24 months. Will you finish it?

FAQ

Q: Can the 58°C alloy be used as solder?

No. The vendor files it under SMD Removal Kit and names it "Alloy for SMD Removal". Solder is the Solder Paste line, for example the 138°C eutectic SMDLTLFP.

Q: Does a lead-free board require a lead-free removal alloy?

For compliance, yes. For temperature, not necessarily. SMD1NL is specified at 79-91°C, 21-33°C above the leaded kit. If your components cannot take that window, the problem to solve is thermal budget, not alloy choice.

Q: For a 79-91°C range, which number do I set the iron to?

The official page gives no recommended iron temperature for removal alloys, so there is no number to copy. What can be confirmed is narrower: at 79°C the alloy is not fully liquid yet.

Q: Can the 138°C paste replace a removal alloy?

Different jobs. Paste puts a component on; removal alloy takes one off. The paste's residue is meant to stay, while the removal kit ships with alcohol pads.

Q: Why is the gap between the leaded and lead-free kits only one dollar?

The two official pages differ in one line: the alloy. Accessories, flux and packaging are identical, so the difference is basically alloy cost. Where price really separates is specification: the 67% metal, 20g version of the same paste is $39.50.
