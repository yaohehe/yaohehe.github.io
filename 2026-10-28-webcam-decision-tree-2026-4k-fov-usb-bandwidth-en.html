How to Pick a Webcam in 2026: 4K, FOV, and USB Bandwidth

webcamremote-workbuying-guide

The biggest number on a webcam product page is usually the one that matters least in a real meeting.

I actually went through the official Zoom, Microsoft Teams and Google Meet help docs, and the official spec pages for Logitech, Elgato, Razer and OBSBOT (retrieved 2026-10-28), then re-ran the maths on the three numbers that get put in headlines: 4K, a 90-degree field of view, and USB 3.0. The result is counter-intuitive: for most people the premium paid for 4K is consumed one layer up, inside the conferencing app.

Scope first. Every performance figure here is an official rating or an official bandwidth document; I did not run side-by-side image tests and I did not measure frame rates. The USB bandwidth section is my own arithmetic from official resolutions and bit depths, not a vendor claim. On price, the only figure I could trace to an official source is Razer's launch MSRP of $299.99 (Razer newsroom, CES 2023); everything else is unavailable to me in a reproducible way, so check the listing before you buy. TL;DR: where each of the six lands

  • Plain video calls, decent light, tight budget: Logitech Brio 300. Official spec: 1080p/30 fps, 2 MP, 70-degree diagonal field of view, fixed focus. The compromise is that 70 degrees is the narrowest here and you cannot focus it — see the Logitech Brio 300 listing.
  • Autofocus plus three FOV presets: Logitech Brio 500. Official spec: 1080p/30 fps and 720p/60 fps, 4 MP, dFoV 90/78/65 degrees, 4x digital zoom. Note that 1080p does not get 60 fps — see the Logitech Brio 500 listing.
  • Uncompressed 1080p60, fixed focus acceptable: Elgato Facecam. The official spec sheet lists 1080p60 as uncompressed, but the same page states the camera only supports MJPEG on USB 2.0, and the lens is fixed focus with a 30-120 cm range — see the Elgato Facecam listing.
  • 4K, bigger sensor, three FOV presets: Logitech MX Brio. Official spec: 8.5 MP Sony STARVIS sensor, dFoV 90/78/65 degrees, 4K/30 fps and 1080p/60 fps, USB-C 3.0 cable. 4K is 30 fps only — see the Logitech MX Brio listing.
  • Dim meeting rooms first: Razer Kiyo Pro Ultra. Official spec: IMX585 sensor with Sony STARVIS-2, f/1.7, FOV 82 degrees with LDC off and 72 degrees with LDC on, 4K at 30 fps. The Razer newsroom describes a 1/1.2-inch sensor with 2.9 micrometre pixels — see the Razer Kiyo Pro Ultra listing.
  • Gimbal tracking or showing objects on your desk: OBSBOT Tiny 2. Official spec: 1/1.5-inch CMOS, f/1.9, FOV(D) 85.5 degrees at 4:3, two-axis gimbal with plus/minus 140 degrees of pan. Costs: 4K is 30 fps, and beauty/background effects officially want an RTX 2060 or an Apple M1 or better — see the OBSBOT Tiny 2 listing.

All three cap out at 1080p, and none of them unconditionally. Unless your plan, client and CPU all line up, the 4K frame your camera captures is downscaled before it leaves your machine.

Check 2: virtual backgrounds and upload bandwidth cut it again

Zoom's help centre has an easy-to-miss line: with a virtual background and no green screen, your video is capped at 720p, "potentially at 360p, for some lower-end Windows computers". If you run a virtual background every day, camera resolution is close to irrelevant.

The bandwidth numbers are equally explicit. Zoom says sending 1080p requires a minimum of 3.8 Mbps upstream. Google lists up to 3.6 Mbps for 1080p and up to 1.7 Mbps for 720p for small organisations or individuals. Microsoft's recommended tier is 1.5/1.5 Mbps for one-to-one video and 2.5/4.0 Mbps for meetings. Below roughly 2 Mbps of upload, fixing the network beats buying a camera.

Note that room audio is a separate decision from the camera; this piece stays on the video side.

Check 3: what uncompressed video costs, on paper

Elgato's own spec sheet states this more clearly than any review. The Facecam MK.2 lists "Supported Resolutions (uncompressed)" with a footnote: "When connected by USB 3.0, otherwise MJPEG", and the system requirements add "Connected by USB 2.0, Facecam only supports MJPEG video". Razer splits its spec the same way: uncompressed is YUY2 and NV12, compressed is MJPEG and H.264.

Check 4: in bad light, look at the sensor, not the pixel count

If your meeting room needs the blinds down by day and has one ceiling light at night, two things matter more than resolution: sensor size and aperture.

Razer's CES 2023 newsroom post describes the Kiyo Pro Ultra as an "ultra-large Sony 1/1.2 STARVIS 2 sensor with a 2.9 micrometre pixel size", paired with an f/1.7 lens; the spec page gives a focus range of 10 cm to infinity, "Optimized for 50 cm to 200 cm". Logitech describes the MX Brio sensor as 8.5 MP Sony STARVIS and calls it the largest, back-illuminated image sensor of its webcams. Elgato's MK.2 spec sheet lists a SONY STARVIS CMOS 1/2.5-inch sensor at f/2.4.

One useful cross-check: Logitech's own support page lists "Night Vision: No" in the MX Brio 705 specification table. The marketing copy talks about low light; the spec table says no night vision. That kind of internal contradiction is worth more than any tagline.

Check 5: match field of view to desk depth, and remember narrow is cropped

Wider is not automatically better; it depends on how far the camera sits from you. More importantly, most multi-step FOV controls are digital crops:

  • Logitech Brio 300 is rated at a 70-degree dFoV and is fixed focus, which feels cramped on a shallow desk.
  • Logitech lists 90/78/65 degrees for both the Brio 500 and the MX Brio, and labels the zoom "4x digital zoom" — a crop, not an optical pull.
  • Razer writes "FOV: LDC Off - 82, LDC On - 72". LDC is lens distortion correction; turning it on gives up 10 degrees by trimming the edges.
  • Elgato's own two generations disagree: the Facecam product page says 82 degrees, the Facecam MK.2 technical specification page says 84. Dimensions and weight differ too (80x48x58 mm at 103 g versus 84x38x61 mm at 90 g), so match the model number before ordering.

Fixed focus has one more constraint people miss: Elgato rates the Facecam for 30-120 cm. Sit outside that band and it will not sharpen up. If you park the camera a metre behind the monitor, fixed focus is the wrong choice.

Check 6: do you need a gimbal?

A gimbal only pays off if you move around during calls or hold physical objects up to the lens. OBSBOT rates the Tiny 2 gimbal at plus/minus 140 degrees of pan, +30 to -70 degrees of tilt, up to 120 degrees per second, with a 10 cm minimum focus distance.

The cost is in the same spec sheet: for 4K output with beauty and background effects, OBSBOT recommends an NVIDIA RTX 2060 or better on Windows, or an Apple M1 or later on Mac. On older hardware, the mode you actually get is probably not the 4K one.

Real drawbacks, one per model

  • Logitech Brio 300: 1080p/30 fps and 720p/30 fps — 30 fps in both modes; a 70-degree dFoV, the narrowest of the six; fixed focus; and the official spec says a mono microphone, not stereo.
  • Logitech Brio 500: 60 fps exists only at 720p; 1080p stays at 30 fps. The 4x zoom is explicitly digital. The compatibility block still carries "Windows 8.1 or higher required for resolutions greater than 1080p" even though the camera tops out at 1080p — template text, ignore it.
  • Elgato Facecam: fixed focus, 30-120 cm, so it cannot compensate if you shift position. The official setup steps say connect directly to a USB 3.0 port and "do not use a USB hub"; a dock pushes you back to MJPEG, and USB 2.0 is MJPEG only.
  • Logitech MX Brio: 4K is 30 fps. The official figures conflict — the Logitech EU page says "up to 3840 x 2160 pixels" while Logitech's China store lists 4096 x 2160 for the same model, which is not the same frame. The support page also lists "Night Vision: No".
  • Razer Kiyo Pro Ultra: 4K is still 30 fps; enabling LDC drops FOV from 82 to 72 degrees; the launch newsroom post itself says raw 4K 30 fps becomes uncompressed 4K at 24 fps. It is also much larger than the rest at 348 g without the lens cover.
  • OBSBOT Tiny 2: in the uncompressed YUV table, 1080p stops at 30 fps; 1080p60 exists only under MJPEG and H.264, and the YUV 4K row has no frame rate filled in. 4K with beauty or background effects wants an RTX 2060 or M1. One more caveat: across two retrievals of the same ASIN I saw the listing title switch between Tiny 2 (1/1.5-inch) and Tiny 2 Lite (1/2-inch, USB 2.0), so verify the model and sensor size in the title before ordering.

FAQ

Q: I bought a 4K webcam. Why does nobody look sharper?

A: Check the send side first. Zoom restricts 1080p to Business and above and requires Zoom Support to enable it. Google restricts sending 1080p to specific plans. Zoom also caps you at 720p with a green-screen-free virtual background. Any one of those means your 4K frame never leaves the machine as 4K.

Q: My laptop only has USB 2.0. Can I still get 1080p60?

A: By the arithmetic above, 1080p60 in YUY2 needs about 1.99 Gbps, far past what USB 2.0 carries, so what happens in practice is a fallback to a compressed format. Elgato states it outright: on USB 2.0 the Facecam supports MJPEG only. You get 1080p, but compressed 1080p.

Q: Fixed focus or autofocus?

A: It depends on whether your seating position is stable. Elgato rates the Facecam for 30-120 cm, so a fixed posture suits fixed focus fine. Brio 500, MX Brio and Kiyo Pro Ultra are all listed as autofocus, which suits anyone who shifts forward and back.

Q: Is a wider field of view always better?

A: No. FOV has to match desk depth: sit close and you need wide, or the frame is only a face; sit far with 90 degrees and half the room joins the call. Also note that multi-step FOV is usually a crop — Razer documents 82 degrees dropping to 72 with LDC on.

Q: On a small budget, what should I not skimp on?

A: Follow the order in this piece. Confirm whether your plan and bandwidth can carry 720p or 1080p, then pick the sensor for your lighting, and only then look at resolution. In a dim room, sensor size and aperture are usually more visible to your colleagues than the jump from 1080p to 4K.

Wrapping up

Inverting the usual order saves real money: check what the meeting app accepts, then your upload bandwidth, then the sensor for your light, and only then resolution and field of view.
