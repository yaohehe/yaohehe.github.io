Why a 0.001 g Reading Is Not 1 mg Accuracy

precision scalelab balancebuying guide

Most people who buy a precision balance are pulled in by one number: 0.001 g. Marketplace listings print it at the top, and the first line of every review says "reads to a thousandth of a gram." But a reading and accuracy are two different things. A balance that displays 0.001 g only proves its screen shows three decimals. It does not prove those decimals belong in your lab notebook.

I copied the official specifications of four balances that get compared a lot, ran the numbers, and fetched each Amazon product page three times to check stock. The results were less flattering than the listings suggest: for three of the four, the last digit is essentially a display digit, not a trustworthy one.

Three words you must separate: division, repeatability, linearity

The U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), in its weighing-and-scales FAQ, draws a clear line between two easily confused quantities. The scale division d is the smallest change you can read on the display. The verification scale division e is the value used to decide whether the balance is within tolerance. When a balance needs legal-for-trade use, what decides compliance is e, not d. The OIML R 76 recommendation treats "reading" and "verification" as separate registers as well.

Once those two are separated, the advertised 0.001 g drops to a display parameter. What actually decides whether you can trust the number is three things:

  • Repeatability: how much a single object scatters when you weigh it ten times in a row. It sets your minimum trustworthy change.
  • Linearity: whether the error stays consistent across different points of the range. It decides whether you can trust the number after switching weights.
  • Temperature drift: how far the reading moves when the room changes by one degree. It decides how long the balance holds up outside a climate-controlled room.

A balance's effective resolution should be its capacity divided by its repeatability, not its capacity divided by its division. That is the baseline for every judgment below.

TL;DR: On a $40-ish budget for samples under 20 g, pick the AWS Gemini-20 and verify it yourself with weights, because AWS publishes no repeatability or linearity. For routine weighing to 200 g that settles in one second, pick the Ohaus SPX222, but note Ohaus itself marks it Not Applicable for legal trade. For a 1 mg reading with a draft break, pick the A&D EJ-303 and budget for its ±20 ppm/°C drift. For 0.1 mg with internal calibration, pick the Ohaus PX224, at the cost of a 4.5 kg body and a higher price.

The bill nobody adds up: temperature drift outranks the reading

The A&D EJ manual lists sensitivity drift at ±20 ppm/°C. That sounds tiny, but multiply by capacity and it is not: 310 g × 20 ppm ≈ 6.2 mg/°C. In other words, a 1 °C room change can move the reading by 6.2 mg, while the display resolution is 1 mg.

Push it further. If a bench sees a 5 °C swing across a day—ordinary in any room without active temperature control—the drift is 31 mg. That is 31 display steps, and ten times the balance's own repeatability of 0.003 g.

This does not make EJ-303 bad. It means its 0.001 g only holds under a stable temperature. The manual is explicit: operating range 10 °C to 30 °C, below 85% RH, non-condensing. Put it by a north window or under an AC vent and the 1 mg reading stops meaning anything.

I ran the same calculation on the other three. Only PX224's official page omits a drift coefficient, so this metric cannot be quantified for it—which is not an advantage, it is missing information.

Decision table: work backward from use, not from the reading

  • Under 20 g powders, gold, gems, tight budget: Gemini-20, and run your own ten-weigh repeatability test first.
  • Routine weighing to 200 g, one-second settle, no verification needed: SPX222.
  • 1 mg reading, stable bench temperature, OK with manual calibration: EJ-303, and get a draft shield.
  • 0.1 mg reading, internal calibration and standard shield, fixed location: PX224.

If your use involves issuing weight records to customers or needs a verification certificate, none of these four is the answer—their official pages say Not Applicable or "No." Look at models that explicitly carry the matching certification class and ask for a calibration certificate.

FAQ

Does a 0.001 g reading mean I can weigh to 1 mg?

No. 0.001 g is only the division d, the smallest step the display shows. NIST's weighing FAQ defines d and the verification division e separately, and compliance is judged on e. In daily use the more useful yardstick is repeatability: a balance with 0.003 g repeatability is trustworthy to 3 mg, not 1 mg.

My balance sits near an AC vent and the digits jump. Is it broken?

Usually not; it is airflow. Balances at the 1 mg level are extremely sensitive to moving air. EJ-303's manual lists 10–30 °C and below 85% RH non-condensing, but says nothing about wind resistance. Add a draft shield, move away from the vent, put it on a solid bench, and let it warm up before weighing.

I need to weigh outgoing goods and issue weight records. What do I pick?

Read the "Legal for Trade" or "Certification" field first. In this article, SPX222 says Not Applicable and PX224 says "No," so neither fits a verified scenario. Choose a model that explicitly carries the matching certification class and request a calibration certificate.

Do I have to buy calibration weights?

Yes, and buy them for the capacity you use. EJ-303's factory weight is 300 g, PX224 uses internal calibration, SPX222 ships a calibration mass for capacities under 620 g. Only weights let you answer "is this balance still accurate today," and at the 1 mg level that question needs asking every day.

Why do prices for the same balance differ so much across pages?

Currency and seller both vary. In this article SPX222's buy box was in CNY (¥2,776.35) while Gemini-20 was in USD ($37.99); that is localization from where the network sits. The same ASIN can also show several third-party offers side by side. Treat every price here as a magnitude reference and read the live page at checkout.
