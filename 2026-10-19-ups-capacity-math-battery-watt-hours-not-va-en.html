UPS Capacity Math: Real Runtime Across 5 Models from 850VA to 1500VA

What actually makes an outage painful is not that the lights go out. It is that your NAS is still flushing to disk, the database is mid-transaction, and all you can do is watch it get hard-cut before spending a weekend repairing data. The most common buying mistake here is to simply match the "1500VA" on the box. What you end up with is a machine that cannot actually deliver 1500 watts, and — worse — one whose runtime has almost nothing to do with that number.

A smaller 850VA unit from the same maker also manages 3 minutes at full load. In other words, a bigger VA number does not mean longer runtime.

Three Numbers That Are Not Interchangeable

The VA, the watt, and the battery watt-hour get mixed up constantly. They have different units and different jobs, and treating them as one number produces a completely wrong answer.

VA (volt-amperes) is the output-side capacity ceiling — the largest apparent power the unit can safely pass through. Think of it as what a wall circuit can handle. The CyberPower CP1500AVRLCD3 is rated 1500 VA, meaning its inverter output path is built to that apparent-power limit.

W (watts) is the real continuous power it can deliver, and it equals VA multiplied by the power factor. These two 1500VA units have different power factors, which is why their watt ratings differ. The CP1500AVRLCD3 official spec table gives an Output Power Factor of 0.6, so 1500 × 0.6 = 900 W. The sine-wave CP1500PFCLCD is listed at 0.7, so 1500 × 0.7 = 1000 W. That is the entire explanation for "same 1500VA, one is 900W and one is 1000W" — it is the power factor, not marketing spin.

Battery watt-hours (Wh) is the number that actually decides runtime. A cell marked "12V / 9 Ah" stores 12 × 9 = 108 Wh. Two cells in series, the usual UPS arrangement, store 216 Wh. This is the real ceiling on available energy.

Once those are separated, the logic is straightforward. Conservation of energy requires battery Wh = output watts × time × conversion efficiency. Battery watt-hours is fixed, and efficiency sits in the neighborhood of 20% (I verify this below against official data), so lower output power buys more time and doubling the power roughly halves the time. That matches the intuition that a bigger battery-backed unit can still have a short runtime.

The key point: VA does not enter this calculation at all. The 1500VA figure only matters when the power draw of what you plug in approaches that limit.

Why Your Runtime Comes In Below the Published Number

The math above uses full load. In practice you rarely run at full load, so the half-load column matters too. The half-load figures back-solve just as cleanly: the CP1500AVRLCD3 at half load (450 W) for 12 minutes gives 41.7%, and the CP850AVRLCD at half load (255 W) for 11 minutes gives 43.3%. Efficiency is higher at half load, which matches lead-acid discharge behavior — higher current draw means more internal losses and less extractable energy.

Three real-world factors shrink your usable time further:

First, published runtime is measured with new, fully charged batteries at normal temperature.CyberPower states this directly next to the runtime chart: runtimes are based on testing fully charged, new batteries at normal operating conditions, the curve is approximate, and results vary with battery age, level of charge at test, environment, and other variables. That sentence is the vendor's, not a disclaimer I added.

Second, lead-acid batteries age.The CP1500AVRLCD3 manual lists Typical Battery Life as "3 to 6 years, depending on number of discharge/recharge cycles." Note the range rather than a single number — on a machine several years old, internal resistance has risen noticeably and full-load runtime will be shorter than a new unit's.

Third, lead-acid has a defined cutoff voltage.The manual's low-battery warning sounds every half second to tell you the battery is nearly spent and you should save files and shut down, or let software do it. So your truly usable time is whatever you calculated minus the shutdown sequence itself. If your NAS takes 30 seconds to shut down cleanly, those 30 seconds come out of your budget.

Worth noting: in the section titled DETERMINE THE POWER REQUIREMENTS OF YOUR EQUIPMENT, CyberPower's manual recommends keeping the load on the battery outlets below 80% of the unit's capacity. The advice is sound — headroom keeps the inverter in a gentler operating region and reduces how often AVR has to intervene.

Troubleshooting: Three Things That Will Actually Happen

Your calculated runtime is twice what you observe

Check whether you overestimated the load first. The LCD on these units shows a load percentage directly, so read that instead of estimating. A common error is treating the maximum power on a PSU nameplate as its consumption — nameplates list peak figures, and real sustained draw is often only a third to a half of that.

The other easy-to-miss cause: you did the math against new-battery runtime. The manual states a three-to-six-year battery life, so a two-year-old unit will naturally come up short.

The battery meter hits 20% and the unit shuts down immediately

CyberPower's manual explains that the battery capacity bar displays in 20% increments, and the low-battery alarm sounds every half second when the battery is nearly spent. If your equipment draws a lot, far less energy remains at the low-battery warning than you planned for, and the unit cuts out shortly after the alarm.

Two ways out: reduce the load, moving monitors and desk lamps to ordinary outlets, or move to a unit with more battery watt-hours. Note that means more watt-hours, not more VA. In the four-model table at the top, the 850VA unit carries a 108 Wh cell while the two-cell units carry 216 Wh, and that gap, not the VA number, is what separates a two-minute ride from a twelve-minute one.

Fans spin erratically or the device reboots itself when connected

This is almost always simulated sine meeting an active PFC supply. CyberPower positions its sine wave models as being for equipment requiring active PFC power source compatibility; read that backwards.

The fix is a sine wave model such as the CP1500PFCLCD or the APC BR1500MS2. Before spending money, verify: plug the suspect device into a UPS you already know works with it, and confirm that is the cause first.

A Decision Tree

Working backward from your equipment list is far more accurate than picking a VA number:

Start by computing your real draw.Read the nameplate of each device you plan to connect and take sustained consumption, not peak. A NAS, a router, and a 24-inch monitor usually lands between 150 and 300 W.

If total draw is under 200 W and any device uses an active PFC supply, take the sine wave route: the CP1500PFCLCD or the APC BR1500MS2. The trade in this tier is runtime for compatibility — the sine wave units actually show shorter runtime figures than the same-capacity simulated sine models.

If total draw is 200 to 350 W and the devices are all conventional, the CP1500AVRLCD3 is the best overall pick here: two cells give 216 Wh and the longest published runtime in this group at 12 minutes half load, with the most complete interfaces and a $500,000 guarantee.

If you want the cheapest unit and your draw stays under 300 W, the CP850AVRLCD is the value pick. Its 510 W ceiling is ample for that combination, $169.95 is the lowest MSRP here, and its runtime still approaches the 1500VA unit.

Treat the BRG1000AVRLCD with care.The five-year warranty and USB charging are genuine advantages, but a one-minute full-load runtime is anomalous in this class. If your draw approaches 600 W, such as a workstation with a discrete GPU, it will trip protection on the startup transient.

Whichever unit you pick, confirm one thing first: that you will not need an external battery pack.None of these five lists an expansion battery interface in the official manual or spec sheet. If you might need longer runtime later, none of them is the right choice and you should look at product lines with an expansion battery bay — that is a more important filter than the VA number.

FAQ

Q: Can a 1500VA unit actually handle my equipment?

A: Look at watts, not VA. Compare the manufacturer's watt rating, which equals VA times the power factor, against your total draw and leave 20% headroom. For the CP1500AVRLCD3, 1500 VA corresponds to 900 W of actual output, so it is only safe for loads under 900 W. CyberPower's manual also advises keeping loads below 80% of rated capacity.

Q: Why do two 1500VA units rate 900 W and 1000 W?

A: Different power factors. The official spec tables list Output Power Factor as 0.6 for the CP1500AVRLCD3 and 0.7 for the CP1500PFCLCD, so 1500 × 0.6 = 900 and 1500 × 0.7 = 1000. A higher power factor means more useful power from the same VA, which is one of the advantages of sine wave models like the CP1500PFCLCD.

Q: Does simulated sine versus sine wave matter for ordinary office gear?

A: For the vast majority of setups, no. The difference shows up mainly with active PFC power supplies, which includes most modern PC power supplies plus some NAS and network devices, and symptoms can include odd fan speeds, buzzing, or repeated restarts. If you are unsure which category your gear falls into, check the nameplate for PFC, or simply pick a sine wave model to be safe.

Q: Can I replace the battery myself, and how long does it last?

A: Yes, and the units here are all marked User Replaceable: Yes. The CP1500AVRLCD3 manual gives a typical battery life of three to six years depending on discharge and recharge cycles, while APC documents three to five years. Always disconnect mains power and unplug the unit before replacing; the manual has the full procedure.

Q: Should the UPS stay plugged in all the time?

A: Yes. In its care section, CyberPower's manual explicitly states that for optimal battery charge you should leave the UPS plugged into an AC outlet at all times, and that a unit stored for an extended period should be recharged every three months. Leaving lead-acid in a discharged state accelerates its decline, which matters far more than any standby power savings.

Wrapping Up

The most commonly wasted money in this category comes from deciding on the "1500VA" number alone. Once the official data for these five is laid out together, the pattern is clear: runtime is set by battery watt-hours, VA only governs peak headroom, sine wave costs you compatibility rather than endurance, and none of these units lets you add a battery later, which is a question worth answering during the selection stage rather than after.

The approach that saved me the most time across the setups I have configured is to invert the order: measure the real sustained draw of your devices first, then decide how many battery cells you need, rather than picking a VA figure and hunting for a matching box. Your equipment list is the only reliable input. The number on the packaging is a reference point.
