Solder Fume Absorbers: Reading Hakko and Weller Specs

solderingfume-extractionworkbench

TL;DR: The part of solder smoke worth worrying about is the rosin. Kester's own safety data sheet lists Rosin as the hazard-determining component with statement H334, "May cause allergy or asthma symptoms or breathing difficulties if inhaled." A benchtop smoke absorber does not remove smoke from your room — it pulls it through a small carbon filter and blows the air back at you. The official spec pages hide three numbers most buyers never read: the same Hakko FA-400 moves 2.2x more air standing up than laying down; one filter holds only 7 g of activated carbon with an official 2 g absorption cap; and Hakko's own FA-430 page mislabels one of its cfm figures. Every number below comes from vendor first-party pages and manuals. I tested each figure against a second official source before using it, and I ran the unit conversions myself.

Absorber versus extraction system: not a size difference

Hakko's own product line splits this in two: the FA-400 is called a smoke absorber, the FA-430 a fume extraction system. These are two different devices, not two sizes of one device.

The FA-430's official page says it extracts "the contaminated air directly at the source": a 110W brushless motor unit with two formable ducts (55 mm diameter, 1.2 m long), nozzle hoods positioned over the iron tip, dual duct ports for two operators, and filter replacement indicator lights. Hakko's US site lists FA430-16 (without duct kit) at a list price of $686.97, and the unit weighs 7.5 kg. The spec table says 1500 Pa of vacuum, 4.1 m³/min (145 cfm) high-mode airflow with a single duct, 4.7 m³/min with two.

The FA-400 is a 19W (120V) desk fan with one 130×130×10 mm activated-carbon filter, weighing 910 g. Hakko describes it as "ESD-safe by design," housed in non-sloughing static dissipative material. It attaches to no duct and removes nothing from the room; it pulls air near the iron through the filter and blows it back.

So answer two questions before buying: how long you solder at a time, and how many workstations. For occasional repairs an absorber is enough. For daily work or two operators, Hakko built the ducted FA-430 class for exactly that scenario — its Amazon listing is this page.

Same unit, two positions, 2.2x the airflow

The most overlooked line on the FA-400 spec sheet: Hakko publishes airflow for both mounting positions. The US site lists the vertical position at 1.1 m³/min (38.8 CFM) and the low-profile horizontal position at 0.5 m³/min (17.7 CFM). Same machine, different orientation, 2.2x the airflow. The UK site's numbers agree, but the footnotes do not: the US page marks its figures "w/o filter" while the UK page states "Maximum suction capacity is measured with a filter present." Hakko's own regional sites disagree on whether the filter was installed during testing.

The air velocity columns are more interesting. The UK site lists 1.0–1.1 m/s vertically but 2.6–2.9 m/s horizontally: less total airflow, yet faster air at the filter face. Section 4.2 of the manual puts it plainly — laid flat, "the absorption power is much stronger in this installation than in the conventional installation," but it is "not suitable for absorption of widespread smoke." Translated into buying advice: horizontal is a close-range spot grab (aim the intake 10–20 cm from the tip), vertical is wide-area coverage. Your desk size decides which you need.

Official noise figures: 48 dB (50 Hz) / 51 dB (60 Hz) at 1 meter, per the UK site. At desk distance that is not a burden.

Two real flaws to know upfront. First, when I pulled the Hakko USA page for this article it showed the FA-400 as Backordered — availability at purchase time rules. Second, the optional 999-198 high-efficiency filter (officially "removes up to 90% of airborne particles") must be used in the vertical position or mounted on the C1568 arm stand; Hakko states it fails laid flat.

The filter is the main character: 7 g of carbon, a 2 g cap

The core consumable is that small carbon pad. Hakko's spec page spells out the A1001 filter completely: 130×130×10 mm, 7 g of activated carbon, and "Max. absorbing weight of noxious components per filter: 2 g." Weller's official page gives the same kind of number for the WSA350: 2.5 g per filter. Filter life is not measured in months; it is measured in how much the pad has absorbed.

The manual gives the replacement criterion: swap the filter when most of its surface turns white, or when dust sticks to it, and keep it dry. One more easy-to-miss prohibition — the manual's own words, "Do not operate the absorber without the filter"; running it bare can cause malfunction. And switch it off when you leave the bench.

The FA-430's filters are a different system: a pre-filter for coarse particles and a main filter with built-in activated carbon. Hakko says to replace them "after about 160 hours of adsorbing solder smoke," with indicator lights on the front panel. But the same page carries a limitation worth underlining: the filters "are designed for removing particulate and by products from the soldering process ONLY."

Consumables belong in your budget: the Weller WSA350F 3-pack is the designated filter for the WSA350, and estimating replacements from the 2.5 g cap beats any "lasts for months" claim.

What 80%, 90% and 99.97% actually measure

Product pages will show you all three numbers. They are not marks on the same ruler.

80% comes from Hakko's US page for the standard carbon filter: "absorbs up to 80 percent of the odors." The target is odor — the gas fraction.

90% comes from the 999-198 high-efficiency filter: "removes up to 90% of airborne particles." The target is particles, vertical position only.

99.97% comes from the FA-430 spec sheet: 0.3µm filtration efficiency at 99.97% in low/medium mode (99.96% in high). The footnote, though, says this is the "initial value of collection efficiency based on counting method for airborne dust collected by a single plate of filter (unfolded)." It is not a whole-machine figure, and it is certainly not a measurement of your room.

One number measures odor, one measures particles, one is a lab initial value for a single filter sheet. Any ranking that puts them side by side is meaningless — and the product pages will not tell you that.

What budget spec sheets leave out

The YIHUA 948DQ-I is the classic budget pick, and its official parameter table is refreshingly modest: rated power 5W, input AC 110–240V, dual filtration (front and rear 9 mm-thick carbon filters), a 5-blade fan, a 30° tiltable stand, and side fenders. The most honest line sits in the official feature list: "Recommended effective distance: 10cm from the fume extractor." That is the capture distance the maker itself claims — 10 cm, measured straight from the front.

Now look at its Amazon listing: the spec block says "Maximum power 200W." Cross-check the official family table and the problem is obvious — only the iron-equipped 948DQ-II and -III carry 125W/200W maxima; the I-model's rating is 5W. The listing also says it is "designed to operate on 110-127V," conflicting with the official 110–240V range. Same model, two official stories; the listing rules at checkout, but do not walk away thinking this is a 200W device.

The WSA350 has a naming trap of its own. Weller catalog data (as published by Grainger) gives 48 CFM maximum airflow; some distributor spec sheets add an "Air Consumption 87-107 CFM" column next to "Adsorption Capacity 1.25–1.37 m³/min (50/60 Hz)." One cubic meter per minute is about 35.3 CFM, so 1.37 m³/min converts to roughly 48.4 CFM — matching the 48 CFM figure. Read the "adsorption capacity" line; the 87-107 column measures something else. The WSA350 unit makes sense once you stick to that line.

Budget units share a pattern: no published absorption cap (YIHUA's site omits it) and no filtered-airflow figure. What the vendor is willing to guarantee is itself part of the spec sheet.

Troubleshooting: it still smells like flux

Work through four checks, all grounded in official documents:

Check the filter first. A mostly-white surface means it is spent — recall the 2 g / 2.5 g caps. Replace with the matching part: A1001 for the FA-400, WSA350F for the Weller. A damp filter fails too; the manual insists on keeping it dry.

Check position and distance next. Horizontal grabs close range, vertical covers wide areas — do not ask the low-airflow position to clear a whole desk. Budget units live or die by distance: YIHUA's own number is 10 cm, and smoke beyond that was never in the working envelope.

Then check the airflow environment. Air conditioning draft and open-window crossflow scatter smoke before it reaches the intake or hood. Grainger's catalog copy states the boundary in plain words: benchtop absorbers "filter some soldering-fume contaminants from the air while directing the remaining contaminants away from the workspace." They are not a substitute for a ventilated enclosure; long sessions in a small room still need ventilation.

Finally, calibrate expectations. The absorber targets particles and odor from flux smoke; Hakko states its filters are designed for soldering-process byproducts only. Lead exposure control is a separate engineering problem — ventilation, process control, housekeeping. One carbon pad does not solve it.

How to pick: a decision order

  • Occasional soldering, tight desk: the FA-400 or WSA350. Both filters measure 130×130×10 mm, but carbon content and caps differ (7 g / 2 g for the A1001 versus a 2.5 g cap for the WSA350F); consumables are not interchangeable.
  • Daily soldering, two operators, teaching labs: the FA-430. Ducted capture at the source, 110W brushless motor, an official 160-hour filter interval, dual ports. Weigh the 7.5 kg and the $686.97 list price too — the Amazon listing title reads only "Soldering Fume Extractor, 120V, ESD Safe," so confirm the FA-430 model inside the page before ordering.
  • Budget first, 10 cm capture distance accepted: the YIHUA 948DQ-I, which ships with 8 filters and 2 fenders.

FAQ

Q: How often should I replace the carbon filter?

A: The official criterion is visual, not calendar-based: replace it when most of the surface turns white. Hard numbers exist too — Hakko's A1001 caps at 2 g of absorbed noxious components per filter, Weller's at 2.5 g, and Hakko's FA-430 FAQ says about 160 hours of adsorbed solder smoke. Solder more, burn more rosin-cored wire, and the pad saturates faster.

Q: Does a smoke absorber protect me from lead?

A: Not the way people assume. Hakko states its filters are designed for soldering-process particulate and byproducts only, and in Kester's SDS the sensitizing component is rosin, not the tin-lead alloy itself. Lead exposure control is occupational-hygiene engineering — exhaust ventilation, process control, cleaning routines. The absorber is not on that line of defense.

Q: Should the FA-400 stand up or lie flat?

A: Follow the smoke. Iron parked in a stand with a concentrated plume — lay it flat for the high-velocity spot grab (2.6–2.9 m/s). Reworking a whole board with smoke from everywhere — stand it up for the wide-area 38.8 CFM. The manual says both things in one section: flat is "much stronger" but "not suitable for absorption of widespread smoke."

Q: Do I still need an ESD mat and wrist strap?

A: The absorber itself is covered: Hakko marks the FA-400 "ESD-safe by design" in static dissipative material, and the FA-430 is listed ESD Safe. Mind the fine print, though — the FA-430's ducts are ESD-safe but its duct dust nets are not. Your mat and strap are a separate grounding system; keep using them.
