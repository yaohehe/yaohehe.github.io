Three Numbers: Jaw Width, Jaw Opening, Throat Depth

bench visespec readingmaker tools

Search for a bench vise and almost every title leads with one number: 5-inch, 6-inch, 8-inch. That number is jaw width. It tells you how wide a workpiece can sit flat against the jaws. It does not tell you how thick a workpiece can be, and it does not tell you how deep a workpiece can stand. Those are jaw opening and throat depth, and on most product titles they appear zero times.

I actually went through the official product pages from Yost, WEN, Wilton, PONY and Forward, plus the listings those brands fill in on Amazon, and lined the numbers up side by side. The spread is wider than expected: among vises that all say 5-inch, jaw opening runs from 4 inches to 6 inches, published clamping force runs from 2,950 lb to 5,500 lb, and the iron grade climbs from 30,000 PSI gray iron to 130,000 PSI austempered ductile iron.

TL;DR: on a bench vise, "5-inch" means jaw width, not capacity. Jaw opening can exceed jaw width — the Forward CR80A pairs an 8-inch jaw with a 9-inch opening, the WEN BV455 pairs 5 inches with 6, and Wilton's 10-inch model pairs 10 with 12 — and it can fall short of it, as the Yost LV-4 does at 4.5 inches of jaw against 3 inches of opening, and the PONY 5-inch at 5 against 4. There is no conversion factor between the two. Throat depth is the number most often left out entirely: Yost's own page for the model 205 says it has an "extra-large jaw opening and throat depth" and prints neither figure. Clamping force has no shared test method: none of the five Yost pages I checked publishes one at all, WEN's own two 5-inch models differ by 1.86x, and the Forward CR80A states 12,000 lb and 55 kN in the same sentence when 12,000 lb converts to 53.4 kN. Buy in this order: jaw width by the shape you clamp, throat depth by whether you work standing up, iron grade last. The Yost LV-4 covers light bench work, the Yost 650-C and the Wilton 745 cover the 5-inch tier, the Forward CR80A covers 8-inch work, and the PONY 5-inch sits in between.

Three numbers, three separate jobs

Pin the definitions down first; every figure after this depends on them.

Jaw width is the left-to-right length of the jaw faces. It decides how much of a workpiece can sit fully on the jaws. Clamp a 6-inch board in a 5-inch vise and only the middle 5 inches are gripped; the rest hangs.

Jaw opening is how far the jaws can separate — how thick a workpiece can go in. This is the item most often misread: a title says 5-inch, a buyer hears "holds 5 inches," and the opening is actually 3.

Throat depth is the vertical distance from the top of the jaws down to the inner channel of the body. It decides how deep a workpiece can stand. When you saw a board standing on end, or drill it vertically, throat depth is the room the blade or bit has to travel.

Nothing in the casting forces these three to track each other. A jaw can be shorter than the opening (thick but narrow work) or longer than it (wide but thin work), and throat depth follows the shape of the body casting on its own.

The habit is not specific to vises. The caliper piece explains why one ±0.02 mm cell on a spec table splits into two rows, the magnifier lamp piece explains why diopter and magnification are not the same quantity, and the function generator piece shows that frequency is six rows in a manual, not one. A vise works the same way: the number in the title maps to one row of the table, and the rest you have to go find.

Throat depth: the line that gets deleted

Yost's page for the model 205 machinist vise reads, verbatim: "The Yost 205 5" machinist vise with swivel base has an extra-large jaw opening and throat depth." Both features are named. Neither is numbered. The same page does give overall dimensions as "12 x 9 x 20.5 inches (H x W x D)", gives the mounting hole diameter as 0.73 inch, and gives the body material as 65,000 PSI tensile ductile iron. Opening and throat depth stay as adjectives.

It is not an isolated case. WEN's BV455 page goes the other way and prints opening (6 inches) without throat depth, while the WEN MPV502 prints both (5-inch opening, 2.75-inch throat).

Where the numbers do appear, throat depth divided by jaw width lands between 0.51 and 0.75: Forward CR80A at 4.1 ÷ 8 = 0.51, Yost LV-4 at 2.6 ÷ 4.5 = 0.58, WEN MPV502 at 2.75 ÷ 5 = 0.55, PONY at 3.5 ÷ 5 = 0.70, and both the Yost 650-C and Wilton 745 at 3.75 ÷ 5 = 0.75.

Wider vises have proportionally shallower throats. The 8-inch CR80A has 4.1 inches of throat; the 5-inch 650-C has 3.75. If your job is hand-sawing a 20 cm board standing upright, the smaller vise is the better tool even though it looks like a downgrade.

Clamping force: a number with no test method

This is where the pages disagree most.

I checked five Yost product pages — LV-4, 455, 650-C, 205 and ADI-5 — and not one of them publishes a clamping force figure. They publish iron grade, opening, throat depth, pipe capacity, swivel range and warranty.

WEN publishes it on both models, and both are 5-inch jaws: the BV455 says "over 2950 pounds of clamping force", the MPV502 says "up to 5500 pounds". Same brand, same jaw width, 1.86x apart.

PONY's listing says "Reach up to 3968 lbs. of clamping force" alongside a 5-inch jaw, 4-inch opening and 3.5-inch throat.

Forward's CR80A states the most complete version: "Clamping Force: 12000 lbs (55kN)". One pound-force is 4.4482 newtons, so 12,000 lbf = 53,379 N = 53.4 kN, not 55 kN. Working backwards, 55 kN corresponds to about 12,364 lbf. The 3.0% gap does not affect use, but it does show the figure is estimated between pages rather than measured and certified.

The real problem is that nobody says how the number is obtained. Where on the jaws the load is applied, how tall the workpiece is, how long the handle lever is, and whether the figure is a peak or a sustained value — all four are unstated. The same phrase spans 2,950 lb to 12,000 lb, a 4.07x range, so comparing it across brands is meaningless. Comparing it inside one brand at one size is meaningful, and there the difference is structural: the BV455 swivels 120 degrees, the MPV502 swivels 360 and adds a second lockdown.

The same manufacturer is saying its austempered ductile iron beats its own forged steel line by more than 45%, which kills the blanket claim that forged is always stronger than cast.

Two official passages do not hold together, and both are worth recording.

First, Yost's Amazon bullets for the LV-4 read: "Made from heavy duty 30,000 PSI Gray Iron castings, 3x stronger than cast iron vises." Gray iron is cast iron, so read literally the sentence says cast iron is three times stronger than cast iron. The intent is presumably "three times stronger than ordinary cast iron vises," but the page never names the baseline grade or its tensile value, so the 3x cannot be checked.

Second, the Wilton 745 uses two material statements on one page: bullet one says "Durable ductile iron swivel base rotates 360°", bullet five says "Heavy-duty 30,000 PSI gray cast iron built for rugged use". The two are not contradictory — the first describes the swivel base, the second the body — but the page never puts "which part is which metal" into a single sentence, so it is easy to walk away believing the whole vise is ductile iron.

Yost also cites two revisions of the same federal specification: the LV-4, 455 and 650-C pages say "Federal Specification GGG-V-410A", while the 205 page says "GGG-V-410B Type VI Class 2". The standard has revisions; that only proves the brand is citing a standard. It is not a basis for comparing strength across models.

Pick by what you clamp: three branches

Branch one, flat plate or angle iron: jaw width first. Five inches covers most bench woodworking and small metal work; if 6-inch-plus stock is routine, go to the 8-inch tier.

Branch two, sawing or drilling upright: throat depth first, jaw width second. Among 5-inch bodies, the Yost 650-C and Wilton 745 give 3.75 inches against the LV-4's 2.6 — 1.15 inches of extra travel that becomes very concrete the moment a saw binds halfway through.

Branch three, pipe and tube: read the pipe capacity line, not the jaw width. The LV-4 is 0.6–1.85 inches, the 650-C is 0.25–2.5, the CR80A is 0.5–3.5. Jaw position matters too: the 650-C sets its pipe jaws back, which shortens effective travel on thin tube.

If none of the three fits, fall back on bench size and budget: a desk under 60 cm doing light work is served by the LV-4 tier; a solid bench doing metal work year-round is served by a 5-inch body with a 360-degree base, either the 650-C or the 745.

Four checks on arrival

1. Bolt size and hole spacing. The Yost LV-4 states outright that mounting hardware is not included. Measure bench thickness, then match the hole diameter the manufacturer prints — Yost's 205 page specifies four 0.73-inch holes. On a thin top, spread the load under the bolt heads, or one hard clamp will wallow the holes.

2. Swivel lockdowns. Whether the base turns 240, 210 or 360 degrees was decided before you paid; on arrival you can only confirm the locks hold. Two lockdowns beat one.

3. The handle. None of the five pages I read says a handle extension is allowed, and none publishes the relationship between handle leverage and clamping force. If it will not close, check whether the work is seated square and the jaws are clean before reaching for a pipe.

4. Casting burrs and coating. Gray iron castings often carry burrs at the jaw edges and in the base slide. Run a file over them before the first workpiece, or that workpiece gets scored.

FAQ

Can a 5-inch bench vise hold something 5 inches thick?

Not by default. Five inches is jaw width; thickness depends on jaw opening. At the same 5-inch jaw width, the WEN BV455 opens to 6 inches and the PONY opens to 4 — a 2-inch gap. Find the opening line before ordering.

Is it a misprint when the opening is larger than the jaw width?

No. The Forward CR80A pairs 8 inches of jaw with 9 inches of opening, Wilton's 10-inch model pairs 10 with 12, and the WEN BV455 pairs 5 with 6, all in the manufacturers' own copy. Jaw length and screw travel are separate structural dimensions and can be designed independently.

Does a 12,000 lb 8-inch vise beat a 2,950 lb 5-inch vise by four times?

That comparison does not hold. No manufacturer publishes the test conditions — load position on the jaws, workpiece height, handle length, peak versus sustained reading. WEN's own two models at one jaw width differ by 1.86x, so cross-brand and cross-size comparisons produce nothing usable.

How much does gray iron versus ductile iron matter day to day?

It matters at the grade level: in this group gray iron sits at 30,000–36,000 PSI, ductile iron at 60,000–65,000 PSI, and austempered ductile iron at 130,000 PSI. Ordinary clamping, hand sawing and hand tapping never approach the material limit. Hammering the anvil, extending the handle for leverage, and letting the body bend under load are what expose the difference.

Why do some official pages omit clamping force entirely?

All five Yost pages omit it, publishing grade, opening, throat depth, pipe capacity, swivel range and warranty instead. It reads less like an oversight than a choice: publish the structural numbers that can be verified, drop the marketing number that has no shared method. When you do see a force figure, look for the conditions attached to it first.
