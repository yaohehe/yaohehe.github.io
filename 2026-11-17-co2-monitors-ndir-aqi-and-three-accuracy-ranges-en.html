CO2 Monitors: NDIR, AQI, and Three Accuracy Ranges

CO2 monitorsspec sheet readingdesk gear

I went through the official spec sheets, datasheets and user manuals of five desktop air monitors line by line (fetched 2026-11-17 from Aranet's datasheet and product page, Airthings' official specifications page, Temtop's spec blocks and the M10+ user manual, Sensirion's sensor pages, and the US EPA's official AQI breakpoint table). The thing that struck me afterwards is that these five machines' "accuracy" numbers cannot be compared with each other, and not because anyone is lying. It is because three different things get squeezed into one word: the range, the formula, and the window in which the formula is promised to hold.

TL;DR: ① Accuracy is not a number, it is a band: ±(a + b × reading). Aranet4's ±(30 ppm + 3%) is really ±42 ppm at 400 ppm and ±90 ppm at 2000 ppm. ② The window matters more than the formula: Airthings Wave Enhance's ±50 ppm ±5% only holds between 500 and 2000 ppm, and Temtop's ±(40 ppm + 5%) only covers 400 to 2500 ppm. A range that reaches 5000 ppm is not an accuracy that reaches 5000 ppm. ③ The sampling interval decides what you never see: Wave Enhance is fixed at five minutes, Temtop offers one minute or ten, Aranet4 can be set to 1, 2, 5 or 10 minutes. ④ The AQI has no carbon dioxide in it. EPA's index covers five pollutants, so the AQI on a screen is computed from PM2.5 using particulate breakpoints; Temtop's manual collapses it into three bands and puts the good-air ceiling at 12.0 µg/m³, while EPA's current table says 9.0. ⑤ All three auto-calibration schemes wait for the same thing: the room returning to fresh air. In a room that never ventilates, the baseline creeps up and the readings run low.

Qingping's listing is generous with features and states the CO2 range as 400–9999 ppm, but neither the official page nor the listing gives a CO2 accuracy figure or a sampling interval. The interesting part of the Aranet4 row is not the 30; it is "0–5000 ppm" and "15–35 °C". Its datasheet adds that above 5000 ppm the accuracy becomes 10% of reading but is not guaranteed, because the figure is extrapolated outside the calibrated window. That footnote belongs on your shopping list: the display can show 9999 ppm, but everything past 5000 is advisory.

Put ±30 ppm into 400 ppm and you get ±42

The constant in that formula is the limit when the reading is zero, and indoor air never reaches zero. Aranet's own FAQ says outdoor air already sits at 400–420 ppm. So the number you can never obtain in a real room is ±30 ppm; what you get is the width of the band at the reading you are actually looking at.

Aranet's datasheet has a sentence that gets quoted less often: the figure is the typical limit for 95% of sensors in equilibrium at the time of sale, and a total error budget also has to account for long-term drift, which that same table lists as "not available". In other words, ±(30+3%) is a 95% statistical claim, not a per-unit guarantee. Splitting an accuracy figure into two columns like that is routine on measuring instruments; I pulled apart the same trap on digital calipers (E_MPE versus S_MPE), because instrument accuracy is almost never a single number.

A five-minute interval cannot see a two-minute peak

Carbon dioxide is a slow variable indoors. Aranet's datasheet gives a time constant of 100 seconds, meaning the reading reaches 63% of a step change after 100 seconds. But ventilation is a step event: a window open for three minutes, six people walking into a meeting room, a range hood starting up. Those events can be shorter than the sampling interval.

Wave Enhance's official specification fixes sampling at five minutes with no way to change it. Temtop's C1+ gives ten minutes in eco mode and one minute in performance mode, and the cost shows up in the battery: 70 days versus 30. Aranet4 lets you pick 1, 2, 5 or 10 minutes, and that cost is on the battery too, quoted per reporting interval: about 0.6 years at one minute, about 3.9 years at ten.

So "four-year battery" and "one-minute sampling" are two ends of the same slider on this class of device, not two features that both hold at once. The "up to 4 years" on a listing is the most frugal setting, and what it hides is that the unit wakes up once every ten minutes.

The AQI has no carbon dioxide in it

This is the one I want people to remember. EPA's AirNow page states that the AQI covers five pollutants: ground-level ozone, particle pollution (PM2.5 and PM10), carbon monoxide, sulfur dioxide, and nitrogen dioxide. Carbon dioxide is not among them. EPA's official breakpoint table confirms it: that table contains seven parameters, all of them the pollutants above under different averaging periods, and not a single row for CO2.

So what is the "AQI" on the screen? Temtop's M10+ manual answers directly: it computes an AQI for PM2.5 following EPA particulate standards, and splits it into three levels of its own — good 0–12, fair 12.1–55.4, poor 55.5–999.9 µg/m³.

The breakpoints are where it gets interesting. In that same EPA table, the 24-hour PM2.5 cutoffs are: good 0.0–9.0 µg/m³, moderate 9.1–35.4, unhealthy for sensitive groups 35.5–55.4, unhealthy 55.5–125.4. The 12.0 in the manual does not match the current 9.0, and EPA's six categories have been compressed into three, merging the 35.4 and 55.4 cutoffs.

This is not fraud; it is a proprietary three-band index borrowing the phrase "EPA particulate standards". The practical consequence for you is that any AQI needs two questions asked of it: which pollutant, and which revision of the table. If you cannot answer both, treat the number as a trend and not as a conclusion. Thermal cameras' NETD is another instance of the same confusion, a limit value marketed as accuracy whose definition is something else entirely (the NETD piece).

All three auto-calibration schemes wait for the same thing

All three units use automatic baseline calibration, and the mechanism is nearly identical: assume that at some point the sensor sees "the cleanest air available", take that as the low reference, and derive everything else upward from it.

Airthings is the most explicit: the sensor self-calibrates using an automatic baseline algorithm that "validates continuously based on the cleanest air the sensor is exposed to", updating weekly. Aranet describes an ABC algorithm and states that manual calibration requires putting the device in fresh air.

That is also the soft spot. If your bedroom or office has not genuinely ventilated for three months, the sensor has never seen a clean baseline, so it treats dirty as normal and the readings run low. You get a curve that looks better every week while the air has not improved at all. Aranet's FAQ recommends periodically taking the device outside. That is not a courtesy note; it is a precondition of the algorithm.

The temperature and humidity envelope is a precondition too. Aranet promises its accuracy only between 15 and 35 °C and 0–80% RH; Wave Enhance between 10 and 35 °C and 0–85% RH. An unheated room in winter, or a damp season that holds above 85% RH, is already outside the line. Temtop states a wider operating environment (0–50 °C, 0–90% RH), but its accuracy window is the narrow 400–2500 ppm one.

Aranet4: the one that writes accuracy as a formula

This is the spec sheet that reads most like an instrument's: accuracy as a formula rather than a single number, plus a time constant, a long-term drift entry, and a pressure-compensation altitude limit (manual calibration above 4000 m). It measures four things — CO2, temperature, humidity, barometric pressure — on an e-ink display, powered by two AA cells.

The product page is Aranet4 HOME.

FAQ

Q: Is 900 ppm in my room a problem?

None of the official documents use the word "problem"; they give bands. Aranet's reference is 400–800 ppm well ventilated, 800–1200 moderately elevated, above 1200 possibly insufficient. Read the error band alongside it: 900 ppm is roughly ±57 ppm on an Aranet4, ±85 ppm on a Temtop, ±95 ppm on a Wave Enhance. The difference between 900 and 1000 is not resolvable on any of these units.

Q: Why do the readings look off for the first few days?

Because auto-baseline calibration has to learn. Airthings updates its baseline weekly; Aranet uses the ABC algorithm; both depend on the device seeing fresh air. If you unbox it into a sealed room, it learns that room as the reference and every later reading inherits the offset.

Q: Can one unit measure CO2 and PM2.5 equally well?

It can measure both, but check whether the two accuracy figures are stated separately. The M10+ gives CO2 at ±(40 ppm + 5%) over 400–2500 ppm and PM2.5 at ±10 µg/m³ (0–100) and ±10% (100–500); those are two independent sets of conditions and neither substitutes for the other. Qingping publishes ranges without accuracy at all, so ask before buying.

Q: Can these double as a carbon monoxide alarm?

No. Aranet's FAQ states plainly that it measures carbon dioxide, not carbon monoxide, and that a dedicated CO alarm is required. They are also not medical devices and must not be used for any diagnostic purpose.
