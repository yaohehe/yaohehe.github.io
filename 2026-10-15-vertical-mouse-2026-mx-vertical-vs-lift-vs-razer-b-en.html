What a vertical mouse actually changes

With a flat mouse your forearm sits in a rotated position — anatomically called pronation. Your radius and ulna cross in that posture, and your wrist carries the load of the whole forearm. A vertical mouse lifts the hand so the forearm returns to something closer to a handshake position. Logitech's official angle for its two models is 57 degrees.

The typical vendor way of quantifying the benefit is Logitech's: the product page claims 10% less muscular activity, and the business data sheet supplies the comparison baseline — a traditional non-vertical mouse. Read that as a manufacturer self-reported figure, not a medical finding. Logitech does not claim on its own pages that the mouse prevents or treats anything, and I am not going to claim it on their behalf.

What you need to judge yourself is whether your ache comes from forearm pronation or from repeated clicking at the base of the thumb. Those two problems have different solutions. If it is the latter, a vertical mouse helps less, and what you probably want is a mouse with fewer thumb buttons — the low-button-count options in our MX Master 4 roundup cover that case.

Discrepancy 1: how heavy is the MX Vertical

This is the section worth reading first, because it can save you a return.

I checked four sources. Logitech's official business data sheet (mx-vertical-advanced-ergo-mouse-data-sheet.pdf) lists 135 g. Logitech Support's tech specs page (article 360023303394) lists 135 g. Logitech Support Hong Kong (article 4422563539095) lists 135 g. Three official pages, all identical.

Different numbers circulate in reviews. One review article states plainly that "Support lists 125 g while the product listing and business data sheet list 135 g," framing it as a Logitech inconsistency. RTINGS shows 127.5 g. Another review site rounds it to 8 ounces, about 227 g.

The verdict is straightforward. With three independent official pages agreeing, 135 g is correct and the review figures are measurement or unit-conversion errors. If you judge "is this light enough" against 125 g, you will be off by 10 grams. That is not much, but it is exactly why secondhand numbers should not be trusted.

Other official numbers from the same data sheet that are easy to miss: dimensions 78.5 mm (H) × 79 mm (W) × 120 mm (L), 300 g with packaging, a 240 mAh rechargeable Li-Po battery rated for up to 4 months per charge, a 10-million-click lifespan, and a 90 cm drop-resistant rating.

Discrepancy 2: not all of them are 57 degrees

This one matters more than the weight, because tilt changes how the mouse feels under your fingers.

Logitech lists 57 degrees for both the MX Vertical and the Lift Vertical. That part is consistent.

Razer's page for the Pro Click V2 Vertical carries a quote from its own product manager: "The 71.7° tilt gives it a handshake-style grip that feels super natural." That is Razer's own site. Meanwhile several distributor pages list 57 degrees, and PC Gamer's review spec table simply leaves the angle row blank.

So if you have read that "all three are 57 degrees, no difference in tilt," that claim is merging two different products' numbers. 71.7 degrees stands the mouse up more steeply than 57, which raises the body and lifts the forearm higher — worth extra caution if you have small hands.

Other official and measured figures for the Razer: 150 g, Focus Pro optical sensor up to 30000 DPI, 550 IPS tracking speed, 40 G acceleration, up to 1000 Hz polling, 6 buttons, a 60-million-click lifespan, up to 6 months of battery with lighting off, USB-C charging, and three connection modes (2.4 GHz HyperSpeed, Bluetooth, USB wired). MSRP is $119.99.

The decision tree: match the mouse to your hand

Step one: measure your hand.

From the wrist crease to the tip of your middle finger. Above 17.5 cm, go to route A. Below 17 cm, go to route B. Between 14 and 17 cm, route B is still the safer pick. That boundary is not mine — it comes from reviewers testing hand size against thumb-button reach, and Logitech's own framing is that the MX Vertical suits large-to-medium hands while the Lift suits small-to-medium hands.

Step two: confirm which hand you use.

Plenty of people skip this and buy wrong.

The MX Vertical's official data sheet lists Handedness: Right. It is right-handed only, with no left-handed variant. The Razer Pro Click V2 Vertical is also right-handed only.

If you are left-handed, the Lift's separate left-handed SKU is the option. That fact only appears in Logitech's business data sheet part number list (910-006492 EMEA graphite left, 910-006495 EMEA graphite left). Consumer retail pages basically never show it.

Route A: large hands, right hand, want it done once

Take the MX Vertical. The case for it is the combination: 4000 DPI adjustable range, 90 cm drop resistance, four months per charge, and Logi Options+ with Flow for sharing cursor and clipboard between two machines.

The costs are real. It is the only one of the three that needs charging (USB-C, 240 mAh) — if you are used to swapping batteries on the go, that adds friction. It has no tilt wheel and no free-spin, so scrolling long documents and wide spreadsheets takes noticeably more effort than on the MX Master series. Warranty is also regional: 1 year in the US and Asia-Pacific, 2 years in EMEA and Japan.

Route B: small to medium hands, or left-handed

Take the Lift Vertical. At 125 g it is 10 g lighter than the MX Vertical, and at 71 mm tall it is 7.5 mm shorter, which helps small hands rest the heel of the palm. It is the only one of the three with an official left-handed model. You also get SmartWheel quiet scrolling and what Logitech calls whisper-quiet clicks, plus a six-button layout versus the MX Vertical's four customizable buttons.

What you give up: click lifespan drops to 5 million, half the MX Vertical's 10 million. The wheel is SmartWheel, not the Master series' MagSpeed, so fast-scrolling feels different. It runs on one AA battery rather than a rechargeable cell — the upside is no charging anxiety and instant swap when it dies, the downside is carrying spares on long trips. It also has no Easy-Switch hardware button; multi-device switching runs through Logitech Flow in software.

Route C: you care about sensor headroom

Take the Razer Pro Click V2 Vertical. Its 30000 DPI ceiling is the only one of the three above 4000, and the 1000 Hz polling rate is the only one explicitly stated. If you run a 4K or multi-monitor setup and feel the mouse has to sweep too far, or you genuinely game and accept that this is not a competitive mouse, this is the only defensible pick. Silent clicks and the 18-zone underglow are also unique to it.

The warnings here are the sharpest. PC Gamer's testing noted two things: the sensor occasionally fails to keep up with fast movement above 3200 DPI, and at 150 g this is heavy for a vertical mouse — more than double the Keychron M5 they tested alongside it. There is also a shape-wide caveat: vertical mice fire their buttons sideways, so frantic mashing nudges the mouse slightly sideways unless you brace the thumb shelf. Razer's weight makes it steadier than most, but heavy also means it is a pain to move around.

Troubleshooting: two things that surprise people

Issue 1: the MX Vertical ships with a Unifying receiver, not Bolt.

Unifying is Logitech's older receiver. If you already have one plugged in, a Logitech Unifying receiver usually carries up to six devices. The flip side is that you cannot pair it into an existing Bolt setup. That is not a defect, but it does mean you do not get Bolt's encryption level (Bolt is Security Mode 1, Security Level 4; Unifying is not). The Lift uses Bolt.

Issue 2: the adaptation period is not "you might get used to it," it is near-certain.

Review consensus clusters at two to five days of feeling awkward, with normalcy by the second week; some reviews say three to seven days. If pain gets worse rather than better after two weeks of correct posture, the angle or the size is probably wrong for your hand, and you should return it instead of pushing through. Wrist damage costs more than one return shipping fee.

My own advice: do not book a long meeting with it in week one. Start with two hours of light work.

FAQ

Will a vertical mouse leave my fingers unable to reach the buttons?

Yes, if your hands are small. The MX Vertical is both the tallest (78.5 mm) and heaviest (135 g) of the three, and the common small-hand complaint is that the ring and little fingers cannot hold the palm heel, so reaching the thumb buttons means shifting position. Reviewers generally recommend the Lift below 17.5 cm. If your hands are borderline and weight matters most, third-party 52-degree models exist, though their spec pages are rarely as complete as Logitech's.

Can I game with a vertical mouse?

Depends on what you play. RTINGS' assessment of the MX Vertical states its click latency is too high for competitive gaming and suits casual play; they also note it lacks the gesture controls and quieter clicks of the MX Master 3S. The Razer Pro Click V2 is the only one of the three with sensor headroom for competitive use (30000 DPI, 1000 Hz polling), but the vertical shape itself is inherently awkward for fast flicks. For actual competitive play, use a flat mouse and keep the vertical one for long coding and document sessions.

How different are the scroll wheels on the MX Vertical and the Lift?

More than most people expect, and it is a quiet downgrade. The MX Vertical's data sheet lists its scroll wheel as precision — no free-spin, no tilt. The Lift has SmartWheel. Neither is the Master series' MagSpeed magnetic wheel. If you rely on the Master series' flick-and-fly acceleration for long pages, expect a noticeable drop when you move to a vertical mouse. RTINGS also lists "no tilt scroll wheel" among the MX Vertical's regressions relative to the Master 3S.

Can I treat the published dimensions as a final verdict?

They work as a hard filter, but they cannot predict your personal comfort. "Suits large to medium hands" is a population-level statement, not a personal one. The reliable approach: if the seller allows no-questions returns, use it for two full weeks at the correct desk height before deciding. Fix your chair height first — several reviews stress the order, because a correct mouse on a wrong chair still fails.

Final word

Whether a vertical mouse suits you comes down to two things you can measure: hand length, and whether you are left-handed. The MX Vertical is the large-handed right-hander's done-once option, the Lift is close to the only realistic choice for small-to-medium hands and left-handers, and the Razer is the trade for sensor headroom plus silent clicks.

Before you buy, do not fixate on the 10% marketing number. Also remember the real official one: 135 grams. The 125 g circulating in reviews will make you misjudge a weight difference that is genuinely tiny.
