Logic Analyzer Specs: The 16-Channel Sample Rate Cliff

Search for a logic analyzer and the product title will lead with a big sample rate: 400 MS/s, 500 MS/s, 1 GHz. Buy it, clip sixteen wires to your board, and the number in the software quietly drops — to 100 MHz, or to 20 MHz.

That is not false advertising. The vendor put the full derating table in the datasheet; the listing copied one cell of it. Those are four different physical limits — USB 2.0 bandwidth, memory bandwidth inside a USB 2.0 unit, USB 3.0 bandwidth, memory bandwidth inside a USB 3.0 unit.

Where the 60% comes from

USB 2.0 Hi-Speed signalling runs at 480 Mbit/s. Each channel spends one bit per sample, so 300 M samples/s is 300 Mbit/s on the wire — 62.5% of 480. The 16-channel tier at 320 is 66.7%.

The vendor says so out loud. The DSLogic product page states that real-time transfer is limited by USB bandwidth and that "Only 3 channels support under 100M sample rate", then notes that U3Pro units add a super stream mode over USB 3.0 with "bandwidth up to 5Gbps" — "10 times than normal stream mode".

That claim is checkable. 3000 ÷ 300 = 10.0, exactly ten. The tiers were not chosen by feel; the interface was fixed first, then the per-channel rate was solved backwards from the channel count.

Push the same logic further. A USB 2.0 host delivers roughly sixty percent of the signalling rate in practice, and it does not go higher. So when a USB 2.0 analyzer advertises "16 channels at 100 MS/s real time", do the multiplication — 16 × 100 = 1600 Mbit/s, more than three times 480. It cannot physically stream that. Something shrank: the rate drops to 20 MHz at 16 channels, or only three channels are active, or the samples go into onboard memory.

Buffered mode: what 0.16 seconds buys you

If real-time streaming cannot keep up, store samples on the board first and transfer afterwards. That is buffered mode, and the headline sample rate on every listing refers to it.

The question is how much memory. DSLogic Plus has 256 Mbit, the U3Pro16 has 2 Gbit, Kingst's LA2016 has 1 Gbit, the LA5016 has 2 Gbit. The datasheet line that matters: "Buffer mode (without RLE compression): 256M / num. of channels" — memory is divided among active channels.

160 to 313 milliseconds, all under a third of a second. That is the real capacity of buffered mode: it is not for watching a bus, it is for catching the waveform around a trigger. To watch a bus for minutes you have to fall back to streaming, and streaming is capped at that 300 M samples/s.

Compression ratios are numbers nobody guarantees

Both datasheets mention RLE compression raising depth: DSLogic Plus from 256 Mbit to 16 Gbit (62.5×), U3Pro16 from 2 Gbit to 16 Gbit (8×).

The catch is that the ratio depends on the signal. An idle bus with a clock that rarely toggles compresses brutally well; once data lines start switching every sample, the ratio collapses. Kingst names that row honestly in its own table: "Max Compression Depth (can't be ensured)" — the vendor will not stand behind the compressed figure.

So 16G is a best-case ceiling, not your working capacity. Budget against the uncompressed 256 Mbit and 2 Gbit, which are the parts that can be promised.

Sample rate, fastest signal, minimum pulse width

Kingst puts three easily-confused numbers on one row. LA1010: max sample rate 100M@3CH, fastest digital signal 20 MHz, minimum pulse width 20 ns.

Sample rate divided by fastest signal is 5×. DreamSourceLab's product page advises that "sample rate should be 10x – 100x than frequency of under test signal". Two vendors, and the multiplier differs by a factor of two to twenty.

Neither is wrong; they answer different questions. Five times means "you can see that this frequency exists". Ten times and up means "you can trust the duty cycle and edge position". For I²C at 400 kHz or UART at 115200, anything works. For a 50 MHz SPI clock, the 10× rule wants 500 MS/s while Kingst's 5× rule is satisfied at 250 MS/s — and that gap decides whether you spend twice as much.

My own approach is to work backwards from the shortest pulse. A 50 MHz SPI clock has a 10 ns high phase, so the sampling interval has to be well under 10 ns: 100 MS/s at the floor, 200 MS/s to be comfortable. That calculation needs no vendor convention at all.

FAQ

Q: The listing says 400 MS/s, so why does the software show 100 MHz?

A: 400 MS/s is the four-channel figure. The datasheet derating table gives 200 MHz at eight channels and 100 MHz at sixteen. The software reports the ceiling for your current channel configuration, not the headline cell.

Q: Should I use streaming or buffered mode day to day?

A: Streaming for watching a bus over time, at the cost of a rate capped by USB bandwidth. Buffered for fast transients, at the cost of 0.16–0.31 seconds of capture. You cannot have both at once.

Q: Is more channels always better?

A: No. Memory is divided by channel, so running sixteen channels gives each one a sixteenth of the storage, and the rate table drops too. If you only decode I²C and UART, an 8-channel unit can actually run faster.

Q: Can a cheap 24 MHz unit decode SPI?

A: Under the 10× rule, 24 MHz sampling covers signals to about 2.4 MHz. SPI clocks usually sit between a few MHz and tens of MHz, so slow modes are marginal and edge positions will not be trustworthy. Timing measurements need 100 MS/s and up.

Q: Why is the vendor's "fastest signal" so much lower than the sample rate?

A: Sample rate is the converter's speed. Fastest signal is what the whole chain — probe, wiring, shielding, threshold — can deliver without distortion. Kingst states a 5× relationship, DreamSourceLab recommends 10× to 100×. The conservative number is the one your measurements will actually match.

Q: Does a logic analyzer need calibration?

A: It only decides high or low, it does not measure absolute voltage, so it does not need the amplitude calibration an oscilloscope does. Threshold setting does change the answer, though, and 1.8 V logic needs an adjustable threshold. For bandwidth conventions on the analog side, see this 100 MHz oscilloscope guide; the error sources are different. If what you need is supply-side current and protocol data, a USB power meter is the direct route.
