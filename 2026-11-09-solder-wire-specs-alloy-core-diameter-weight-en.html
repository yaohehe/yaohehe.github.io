Solder wire specs: alloy, core, diameter, weight

solder wiredesolderingamazon

Unscrew a fresh spool of solder wire and you will find four things printed on it: an alloy designation, a two- or three-digit core number, a diameter in two units, and a weight in ounces or pounds. They look like four ways of writing the same thing. They are not. Each one answers a different question, and there is no formula that converts one into another.

I actually went through Kester's official twelve-entry core-to-flux table, their fifteen-row alloy melting chart, and the hundreds of rows in their product catalogue that cross alloy with flux, diameter and core. Then I opened six live listings and checked what the title and the bullet points state, and what they leave out. The conclusion runs against intuition: the number most people read as "the spec" — the core number — is the one number that cannot be derived, only looked up.

A spool carries four numbers, not one

Take the four apart and each answers a separate question. None substitutes for another.

The alloy answers when it turns liquid, and whether that is a point or a range. This decides whether you can judge a joint by feel, and what temperature the iron needs.

The core number answers how much flux is inside the wire. It is a catalogue code, not a percentage scale — the official table below shows why.

The diameter answers how much heat and how much metal you deliver per touch. At a fixed weight it also decides how long the spool is.

The weight answers how much metal is on the spool. Between weight and length sit density and cross-section, so the two are not one-to-one.

What you are holding is defined by all four at once, not by any single one. The thing retail pages do most often is compress all four into one title line and then drop the core number.

Alloy: whether melting is a point or a range

A manufacturer's alloy chart writes each melting point as a single number or as a range. That difference is not a typesetting habit; it is physical.

The column worth staring at is "width". The figure 183°C appears five times, but for Sn63Pb37 it is the melting point itself, while for Sn35Pb65 it is only where melting starts — that alloy does not become fully liquid until 247°C, and everything between is a 64°C pasty range in which a joint will crack if disturbed. The same manufacturer's FAQ says it plainly: Sn60Pb40 has a plastic range, Sn63Pb37 is eutectic and has none.

That explains a common confusion. For the same 60/40 alloy you can find 183, 188 and 190. The 183 is the solidus, the 190 is the liquidus, and 188 is a single nominal figure that secondary sources keep copying. None of the three is wrong, but only the range tells you the metal is mushy between 183 and 190.

There is a second layer: 183°C is not a working temperature. The same FAQ gives 700–800°F for lead-free hand soldering, which is 371–427°C, roughly twice 183°C. Melting is only the threshold where flow begins; the heat still has to reach the pad, the lead and the surrounding copper.

Core number: a lookup table with no ordering

This is the counter-intuitive part. The manufacturer's FAQ publishes a core-to-flux table with twelve entries:

Read that as a scale and it breaks immediately.

#22 and #58 are both 2.20%. Codes 36 apart, one value.

#60 is 6.00%, #66 is 3.30%. The higher number carries 45% less flux. If you buy on the instinct that bigger numbers are better, you buy this one backwards.

#40 is 0.60%, less than half of #50 at 1.10%. Yet #40 looks like a middle rung.

The spread is 12x. The leanest, #05, is 0.50%; the richest, #60, is 6.00%.

So the core number has to be looked up. That matters most on a product page, because some titles bury the core inside the part number: in 24-6337-8800 the 8800 encodes "245 no-clean flux plus core 50", which is 1.1%. The 3.3% version in the same family is 24-6337-0027. Identical alloy, identical diameter, identical weight, three times the flux — and neither title prints a percentage.

I would rather read the bullet points. The 24-6337-0010 listing states "Standard core 66 (3.3 percent flux)"; the 24-6337-8800 listing states "Flux %: 1.1 %". Those two sentences are worth more than the title.

The percentage is a ratio, not a quantity

3.3% is a fraction by weight, not "a third of this wire is flux". The manufacturer's FAQ says "use a flux content of 2-3% by weight" when discussing lead-free hand soldering, and listings say "Standard flux core percentage at 2.2 percent". Both are by weight.

Convert to milligrams of flux per metre using the official densities (about 8.4 g/cc for Sn63Pb37, about 7.3 g/cc for lead-free) and the difference between ratio and quantity shows up:

Read it this way: 1.50 mm at 2.2% carries more flux than 0.80 mm at 3.3% — 2.3 times more. The lower percentage wins because the thicker wire puts more metal, and therefore more flux, into every metre.

The reverse also holds. The thinnest wire, 0.30 mm, is labelled 3.5% — higher than 3.3% — yet carries only 18 mg per metre, under one seventh of the 0.031 inch wire. Thin wire is for small pads; it is not the tool that rescues an oxidised joint.

One more detail that gets missed: at the same diameter and the same percentage, lead-free wire carries about 13% less flux by mass, simply because the density is lower and the same volume weighs less. Switching from leaded to lead-free without changing the numbers on the spool does not keep the flux quantity constant.

Diameter: the two units are not a conversion

Listings normally give two figures: 0.031 inch and 0.80 mm. They look like the same fact written twice; the second one is nominal.

The worst deviation is ±5.8%, which becomes −10.7% to +12.0% once squared into cross-section. The manufacturer is not making an error; the mm column is rounded to the standard integer dies. But if you use the mm figure to work out how much thicker one wire is than another, you will be systematically wrong. The 0.093 / 2.50 pair is the clearest case: by the official mm value the cross-section is 12% larger than an exact inch conversion gives.

The question that actually matters is how big the pad is and how wide the tip is. 0.020 inch suits fine pitch and rework wires, 0.031 is the default for through-hole and ordinary SMD, and 0.062 and up is for ground planes, shield cans and heavy leads where the thermal mass is large.

It also explains a manufacturer claim. MG's lead-free listing says "About 14% longer by weight than leaded solder wires". The cause is the density difference: 8.4 against 7.3 gives about 15% more length for the same mass. The manufacturer says 14%, I get 15.1% from the two densities, and the two numbers agree closely enough to trust.

A ten-minute check before you order

Four questions, in this order, and you will rarely buy wrong.

First, fix the alloy. Leaded or lead-free, decide before anything else. Once the alloy is fixed, the melting range is fixed and most of the iron temperature is fixed with it.

Second, look up the melting range in the official chart. Do not stop at a single number in a title. If you see one number, ask whether the alloy is eutectic; if it is not, that number is probably just the solidus.

Third, translate the core number into a percentage. Then convert the percentage into an absolute amount per metre using the diameter — a higher percentage does not mean more flux.

Fourth, divide weight by diameter to get length. Whether a pound is 350 feet or 100 feet is a function of diameter, and the gap is 8.7x.

FAQ

Q: The spool says 183°C. Is that the temperature I should set my iron to?

A: No. 183°C is where Sn63Pb37 melts, meaning where it starts to become liquid. The manufacturer's FAQ gives 700–800°F (371–427°C) at the tip for lead-free hand soldering, roughly twice the melting point. The heat has to serve the pad, the lead and the surrounding copper, and it has to do so within a few seconds. Set 183°C and you get a blob of unmelted solder.

Q: Does a bigger core number mean more flux?

A: No. The official twelve-entry table is not monotonic. #60 is 6.00% while #66 is 3.30%; #22 and #58 are both 2.20%; #40 is 0.60%, less than #50 at 1.10%. The core number is a catalogue code and has to be looked up.

Q: If it says No Clean, do I really not have to clean the board?

A: That depends on the flux chemistry, not on those two words. The manufacturer describes 245 as producing "visually acceptable assemblies without cleaning" — a statement about appearance. The water-soluble 331 on the same site is described as leaving residue that can be removed with a heated water rinse. Those point in different directions. If you plan conformal coating, or the board will live in high humidity, follow the manufacturer's cleaning guidance rather than the label.

Q: Do I have to replace my iron to go lead-free?

A: Not necessarily the machine, but temperature, dwell time and tip condition all change. SAC305 is officially 217–220°C, 34°C above 63/37. The FAQ notes that lead-free solder has slightly higher surface tension and wets more slowly, and recommends 2–3% flux content by weight plus a higher tip temperature. If your iron has no temperature readout and only low/medium/high, then yes, replace it.

Q: How long will a one-pound spool actually last?

A: It depends on diameter. Using the official 8.4 g/cc density, one pound of 0.020 inch is about 874 feet, 0.031 inch about 352 feet, and 0.062 inch about 100 feet. For board repair, a pound of 0.031 inch lasts a long while. For small batches, a one-ounce tube is the better shape of the problem.

What to remember

Solder wire is the consumable that looks like it has nothing to choose, yet it compresses four independent variables into one package: alloy, core number, diameter, weight. Get any one wrong and what arrives is not the wire you thought you ordered.

The line worth keeping is this: the core number is not a scale, it is a lookup. Across the twelve official entries the number and the percentage are not even in the same order, and the ends differ by 12x. The second thing to keep is that whether melting is a point or a range depends on whether the alloy is eutectic, and 183°C shows up for five different alloys because it is only the solidus.

Every figure here can be re-checked; the official pages are named in the text. If the spool in your hand has no core number printed on it, look the part number up in the manufacturer's catalogue once — that is worth more than any number of "works great" reviews.
