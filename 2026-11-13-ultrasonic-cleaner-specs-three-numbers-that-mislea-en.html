Ultrasonic cleaner specs: three numbers that mislead

desk gearmaker toolsultrasonic cleaner

An ultrasonic cleaner is one of those products where the spec sheet looks simple and the purchase decision still goes wrong. A listing gives you three numbers: 40 kHz, 60 W, 2 L. All three are real. None of them answers the question that matters, which is whether the machine will lift flux off the board you are holding.

I did not buy six cleaners and dunk parts in them. What I actually did was copy two manufacturer technical tables line by line, add the decision rules from Branson's official Ultrasonic Cleaning Primer, convert everything to one comparable unit, and then check six live listings against it. TL;DR: cut the shortlist with W/L first

TL;DR: the word "power" on a listing has at least three meanings, and the stated capacity is only 77-93% of the geometric tank volume. What actually separates two machines at the same price is power density, meaning effective ultrasonic watts divided by rated litres. Small parts and boards want a small tank with high density; greasy parts need heat; large parts need a large tank, but a large tank is not a harder cleaning bath.

  • Glasses, jewellery, small boards: the Elma Elmasonic EP10 rates 30 W effective into 0.2 gal (0.76 L), about 39.6 W/L, among the densest of the six. The listing is Elmasonic EP10.
  • Light use on a budget: VEVOR's 1.3 L unit claims 60 W into 1.3 L, about 46 W/L, and unusually it also publishes the tank size at 155 x 140 x 65 mm. See VEVOR 1.3L.
  • Greasy hardware that needs heat: the CREWORKS 2 L lists 60 W of ultrasonics plus a 100 W heater, at CREWORKS 2L.
  • Fitting a whole board: the CREWORKS 6 L spreads 180 W across 1.6 gal for 29.7 W/L; you are buying volume, not intensity. Listing: CREWORKS 6L.

The "60 W" on the listing is three lines in the official table

Elma publishes a per-model product sheet for the Elmasonic EASY range, and it puts three power figures side by side for the same machine. On the EASY 30 H: 280 W total power consumption, 80 W effective ultrasonic power, 320 W maximum ultrasonic peak performance, with 200 W of heat listed separately.

Copy three heated models out of that sheet and two identities appear:

First identity: total equals effective plus heat, on all three rows (280=80+200, 340=140+200, 550=150+400). Second: peak is exactly four times effective, again on all three. The footnote in the same PDF explains why peak is not a capability number: it is a multiplier applied to the waveform, "Due to the form of signal the maximum peak ultrasonic power calculates with factor 4 or 8".

So a lone three-digit wattage on a listing can be total draw, effective ultrasonic power, or peak. Those three differ by a factor of four, and only the middle one describes the cleaning field. The CREWORKS 2 L listing is comparatively honest here: it separates 60 W from a 100 W heater, so you can add up 160 W yourself. Most listings leave a single number and no way to tell which.

Only a 2-3x power window is useful; past that you get surface cavitation

Branson's official primer contains a quantified statement that almost nobody quotes. Cavitation starts only when local pressure drops below the liquid's vapour pressure, so there is a threshold. Push power higher and at some point the liquid can no longer absorb it: violent cavitation forms right at the transducer face, the liquid layer turns elastic, and energy stops reaching the bath. The document calls this surface cavitation.

Then comes the sentence that matters: "The sonic range over which cavitation can exist between threshold and maximum is usually separated by no more than a ratio of 2 or 3 to 1." A tank's useful power window is typically 2x to 3x wide. Below the threshold there is no cavitation; above the ceiling you are heating electricity for nothing.

The purchasing consequence: if a machine already runs at the top of its window, going from 100 W to 300 W will not triple the cleaning. Real gains come from frequency, temperature, chemistry and loading. Conversely, a tank that sits at 10 W/L cannot be rescued by buying the next size up.

Frequency is a force setting, not a tier

Listings present frequency as a hierarchy: 40 kHz above 28 kHz, 48 kHz above 40 kHz. The manufacturers describe it as a trade. Branson again: "As the frequency increases, the number of these cavities also increases but the energy released by each cavity decreases making higher frequencies ideal for small particle removal without substrate damage." More bubbles, each one weaker.

A manufacturer technical page puts numbers on the bands: roughly 25-40 kHz gives larger bubbles that collapse with greater force but distribute less evenly, suited to heavy soils and robust metal; 80-170 kHz gives smaller, gentler bubbles spread more evenly, for delicate parts and electronics.

So frequency chooses force plus uniformity, not quality. Among the six units here, Elma runs 37 kHz, Crest offers the same model at 45 kHz or 132 kHz, and the CREWORKS and VEVOR units run 40 kHz, with one dual-frequency machine in the mix. A single Crest chassis offered at two frequencies is itself the proof that frequency is a configuration, not a grade.

Rated capacity is 77-93% of the geometric tank

"2 L" does not mean you can pour in 2 L. Official tables usually give both the rated volume and the internal dimensions, and multiplying the dimensions settles it. Across Crest's seven models, P500's 5.4 L is only 76.5% of its geometric tank, P1200 reaches 90.3%, and the rest sit at 83-87%. Across Elma's nine, EP10 is 77.8% and EP20H is 93.0%.

That is a 76.5%-93.0% band. The reason is mundane: rounded corners, a fill line, and clearance for parts and basket mean the rated figure is usable volume, not geometric volume. The problem is that many listings give only the capacity, so you cannot tell which one you are being quoted.

Two examples, one good and one thin. The VEVOR 1.3 L publishes 155 x 140 x 65 mm, which works out to 1.41 L geometric against a 1.3 L rating, or 92.2%, and the two agree. The CREWORKS 6 L states 1.6 gallon and no internal dimensions at all, so I can confirm the capacity basis but cannot recompute it. That is not a defect; it is a gap you have to close yourself.

The practical rule: measure the largest part first, then look for internal dimensions, not capacity. A 100 x 100 mm board will not enter a tank that is 118 mm wide, no matter what the litre figure says.

Loading: 70% of the cross-section, 50 mesh, one inch off the bottom

Branson's primer gives three numbers you can act on immediately, and they beat any review:

First, the sum of the parts' cross-sectional areas should not exceed 70% of the tank's cross-sectional area, or the parts block the sound. Second, anything woven tighter than 50 mesh behaves as a solid under ultrasound; openings larger than a quarter inch are what counts as open. Third, neither parts nor basket may rest on the tank bottom, because they reflect energy back into the transducer; a Branson-authored technical article puts the clearance at one inch.

One more that gets ignored: elastomers and non-rigid plastics absorb ultrasonic energy, so be careful with fixturing. Clamping a part in a silicone pad is roughly the same as putting acoustic foam next to it.

Troubleshooting: when the bath stops biting

These five checks come straight from manufacturer documents, ordered by likelihood and cost.

First, degas a freshly filled bath. Branson's technical article is specific: run the unit five to ten minutes to drive dissolved gas out, and only repeat it after the bath is newly filled.

Second, check whether parts or basket are touching the tank bottom. Both must clear it, by about an inch.

Third, do the loading arithmetic. Past 70% of the tank cross-section, the parts block their own sound field.

Fourth, inspect the basket. Anything tighter than 50 mesh acts as a solid wall.

Fifth, consider whether the chemistry is spent. Manufacturers warn that solutions wear out and that suspended soil settling on the tank floor suppresses activity; changing the bath usually beats adding time.

Two manufacturer prohibitions: IPA and mineral acids

The reflex for cleaning a circuit board is to pour in isopropyl alcohol. Branson is explicit: "Never use solvents in a small benchtop cleaner. It is neither safe nor environmentally responsible." The reasoning is that solvents vaporise fast and can collect underneath the unit, where electrical components can ignite them. The same passage names mineral acids and bleach as damaging to the machine.

Crest devotes a paragraph on its own benchtop product page to the same question, noting frequent enquiries about low-flashpoint liquids such as acetone, IPA and cyclohexane, and stating that under NFPA 79 compliance its tabletop units and tanks are not designed for Class 1 Division 1 flammable liquids or hazardous locations. The wording: "Crest does not recommend nor will it be held liable for any customer that uses flammable liquids in our ultrasonic table tops".

Two independent manufacturers, two official documents, one conclusion: clean flux with an aqueous or purpose-made cleaning solution, and keep flammable solvents out of the tank.

FAQ

Q: Is 48 kHz worth paying more for than 40 kHz?

Per the manufacturers, frequency sets bubble size and energy distribution, not a grade of cleanliness. A 48 kHz bath makes smaller, denser, gentler bubbles suited to glasses, jewellery and precision parts; 40 kHz hits harder and suits greasy hardware. For boards and delicate parts, the 40-versus-48 gap is smaller than the effect of degassing properly or staying under the 70% loading rule.

Q: Can I put plastic or aluminium parts in?

The official constraints are narrow: elastomers and non-rigid plastics absorb ultrasonic energy, so fixturing needs care, and lower frequencies collapse "with greater force", which raises risk on soft or polished surfaces. No manufacturer publishes an allowable power ceiling for aluminium, so I will not invent one. Use a higher frequency, shorten the cycle, and test on a scrap part first.

Q: Do I need a heated model?

It depends on the soil. The EP10 and the VEVOR 1.3 L have no heater, with heating power listed as 0, and they handle dust, polishing compound, salt and loose dirt; congealed grease, wax and polishing paste need temperature to soften. Branson's authored article notes that many solutions work in the 120-150 F band (about 49 to 66 C), while the thermostats on these six units generally stop at 80 C.

Q: Is a bigger tank always better?

No. What matters is part size, not total cleaning demand. When volume grows faster than power, density falls: across the two manufacturer tables here, the smallest-to-largest drop is 2.49x to 3.7x. Size the tank from your largest part first, then check that the resulting W/L is in the 25-plus range.

Q: Should I rinse after cleaning?

Yes. Ultrasonics lift soil off the surface and hold it in suspension; residue dries back onto the part as the liquid evaporates. Manufacturer procedures call for a rinse and then drying, otherwise you are plating the soil back on.

Bottom line

If you keep one sentence: a listing gives you isolated numbers, and the decision lives in the ratios between them. Divide effective ultrasonic watts by rated litres to get W/L, compare it against the 25 mark, confirm the internal dimensions clear your part, then confirm whether there is heat. Those three steps remove most of the bad matches before you order.
