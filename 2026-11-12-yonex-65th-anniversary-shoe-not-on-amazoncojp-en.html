Three keyword sets, zero results

What "limited" means in that sentence

Japanese product copy uses 限定 for at least three different things: a limited quantity, a limited window, and a limited channel. They demand completely different responses. Quantity means hurry. Window means watch the calendar. Channel means change where you shop.

This is the third kind, and it's the hardest of the three to work around, because it removes the option of waiting. A quantity-limited run at least existed on the shelf for a while. A channel-limited run never reaches the shelf.

The tell is grammatical, not lexical. 「〜での限定で販売いたします」 limits a place (で), not a count and not a date. If you're ever unsure which kind of 限定 you're looking at, read the particle. It's more reliable than any reseller's summary.

What "not an approved product" actually gates

The press release carries one footnote, and it does more work than the entire product description: 本製品は日本バドミントン協会審査合格品ではありませんので、同協会公認の大会では着用はできません。

That splits buyers in two, and the split is by destination, not by budget. Club nights, gym sessions, casual play, photos, collecting — the footnote is irrelevant to you. But if you're entering a tournament sanctioned by the Japan Badminton Association, this shoe can't be worn, and finding out at equipment check means finding another pair on the spot.

The second implication matters more: approval is granted per model, not per series. All four retail 65Z4 variants carry the 日本バドミントン協会審査合格品 line on their pages. The anniversary model doesn't carry it, and goes further by declaring the opposite. The test is that line on the product page — never "it's a high-end YONEX, so it must be fine".

There's corroboration beyond the shoe. Prefectural entry documents write approval into the conditions directly: one 2026 spring middle-school tournament form states 競技ウェアは、本年度（公財）日本バドミントン協会審査合格品とする. That clause governs apparel, while shoes are judged by the footnote above — but both point at the same system, and it is enforced at entry, not just on paper.

Worth separating this from other certification labels you may have seen on this site. The ¥10,000 line and its 2028 expiry

This deserves spelling out, because "under ¥10,000 is duty free" gets repeated as if it were universal.

Japan Customs exempts personal imports with a dutiable value of ¥10,000 or less from duty and consumption tax — but leather shoes and footwear with leather soles are named as exclusions. So the real question becomes whether the model's material row contains leather.

The anniversary release lists upper: synthetic fibre; midsole: synthetic resin; outsole: rubber. No leather in any of the three. The 65Z4 Wide retail page lists a synthetic-leather upper, synthetic resin midsole, rubber outsole. In other words, within footwear you have to read the material row per model; "they're sports shoes, so they're not leather shoes" is not a safe shortcut. Actual treatment is decided on declaration, so this is a reading rule, not a verdict.

There's also an expiry date on the rule itself. A Ministry of Finance amendment abolishes the personal-import small-parcel exemption from 2028-04-01. The ¥10,000 line is not permanent, and anyone buying expensive shoes late should re-run the arithmetic.
