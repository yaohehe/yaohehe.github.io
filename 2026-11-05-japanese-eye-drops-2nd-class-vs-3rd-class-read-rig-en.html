2nd class and 3rd class are risk tiers, not strength tiers

Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare defines the three OTC tiers in its policy paper on the 2009 revision of the OTC sales system. The wording is worth reading literally:

  • 第一類医薬品: contains ingredients requiring particular caution for safety, such as those with little history of OTC use
  • 第二類医薬品: contains ingredients that could rarely cause health damage serious enough to require hospitalisation
  • 第三類医薬品: contains ingredients that could cause bodily discomfort, though not to the point of disrupting daily life

Look at the phrasing in the second line: "rarely, hospitalisation-level". The third line is "not enough to disrupt daily life, but discomfort is possible". The whole definition talks about risk. Not one word about how well the product works.

There is a second, more practical difference attached to it — the duty to explain at the point of sale. Class 1 must be explained by a pharmacist using written material. Class 2 requires a pharmacist or registered seller to make an effort to provide information. Class 3 requires no explanation at sale at all; the seller only has to answer if the customer asks.

That single rule explains something concrete. Pick up a class-3 eye drop in a Japanese drugstore and nobody is obliged to tell you anything about it. The thinnest, most contradictory coverage of FX コンタクト in Chinese-language guides is a direct consequence: politically it falls into the "no explanation required" bucket, so the information never gets generated in the first place.

To know if you can dose with lenses in, read the indications, not the icon

Santen's brand page prints an icon per bottle: コンタクトレンズ対応 ハード◯ / ソフト×. The icon is easy, but it is second-hand information. The primary source is the 効能・効果 (indications) block on each product page, which states the legally approved scope:

  • サンテFX Vプラス: …discomfort while wearing hard contact lenses
  • サンテFX ネオ: …discomfort while wearing hard contact lenses
  • サンテFX コンタクト: …discomfort while wearing soft or hard contact lenses
  • サンテFX AL: no contact-lens item anywhere in its indications

The pattern is exact. A ◯ on the icon corresponds to the presence of the line "discomfort while wearing contact lenses" in the indications. Vプラス and ネオ say hard only, so soft is ×. コンタクト says soft or hard, so it is the only double ◯ in the lineup. AL omits the line entirely, so both are ×.

This reading rule outlives any specific model. When Santen ships something new, you find that line on the detail page and decide for yourself instead of waiting for a guide to be updated.

One prohibition is easy to miss. The コンタクト product page carries a footnote after the product name — "カラーコンタクトレンズは除く" — and the brand overview page states it more bluntly: do not use while wearing colour contact lenses. So for anyone wearing circle lenses or coloured contacts, all four FX bottles are out. Almost none of the Chinese-language summaries I read mention this, yet it is printed by the manufacturer.

Count the actives: ネオ has 6, Vプラス has 7, AL has 5, コンタクト has 4.

Two of those names deserve their own paragraph. Tetrahydrozoline hydrochloride is a vasoconstrictor; it is what removes the red from the white of the eye. The redness goes away, but only temporarily, and this is the single most abused ingredient class in combination eye drops. Neostigmine methylsulfate is an anticholinesterase acting on ciliary muscle accommodation; the official wording is that it improves eye strain through a focus-adjustment effect.

Now see where those two land: present in Vプラス and ネオ, absent from AL, absent from コンタクト.

Which is why the class-3 bottle makes sense once you read its list. コンタクト is the only one in the lineup that drops the vasoconstrictor, the antihistamine and the anticholinesterase all at once, keeping just two metabolic amino acids (taurine, potassium L-aspartate), one vitamin (panthenol) and one anti-inflammatory (epsilon-aminocaproic acid).

So "3rd class" and "4 ingredients" are two independent clues pointing the same way: the tier is low because the formula is conservative, not because the effect was discounted. AL shows the same logic from the other side — as the allergy bottle it swaps in sodium cromoglicate 1.0% and drops the vasoconstrictor route too.

One observation, offered as an observation because Santen does not explain it: the additive lists for Vプラス and AL both contain benzalkonium chloride, while コンタクト uses chlorhexidine gluconate instead. That difference lines up exactly with the "soft lenses can only use コンタクト" conclusion, but the manufacturer never states causation. I will not invent it for them. The "5+" chill rating is an in-house scale

The chill ratings are ネオ 5, Vプラス 5+, AL 5+, コンタクト 5. The caption under the scale reads: the larger the number, the stronger the refreshing sensation; the smaller, the gentler on the eye.

The plus sign is the point. The 5+ on Vプラス and AL carries a footnote reading 当社製品内で最も高い清涼感レベル — the highest chill level within our own products, not the highest on the market.

That footnote sinks claims like "FX is the chilliest eye drop there is". It tops Santen's own range and nothing else. There is no cross-brand scale, and the sensation is as subjective as the cooling in a shampoo. Buy the 5+ expecting the industry maximum and you have bought Santen's internal first place.

The more informative detail is that コンタクト still rates 5. The soft-lens bottle was not dialled down to something bland; it stays at the upper-middle of the same series. That contradicts the common assumption that anything lens-safe must be gentle to the point of doing nothing.

FAQ

Is 3rd class weaker? No. The definition is about how much harm the ingredients could plausibly do. コンタクト sits in 3rd class because it drops the vasoconstrictor, the antihistamine and the anticholinesterase, making it the most conservative formula of the four — not because its effect was reduced.

Can I use FX with soft contact lenses? Only コンタクト lists soft lens wear in its indications; the other three cover hard lenses only. Anyone wearing colour contacts cannot use any of the four, and the manufacturer says so explicitly.

How many times a day is too many? By each bottle's own dosing column: 5–6 times for Vプラス, 4–6 for AL, 5–6 for コンタクト, counted per dose. Beyond that, or a week of use with no improvement, means an ophthalmologist rather than another drop.

Which brings it back to the opening point. The hard part of this labelling system is not the Japanese; it is that risk and strength were split into two unrelated fields. Chill is a subjective in-house ranking. The risk tier is a legal classification that decides who may sell the bottle, whether anyone has to explain it, and whether you can dose with lenses in. Remember to look for that one line about discomfort while wearing contact lenses and the choice between the four bottles takes thirty seconds. What actually takes time is the arithmetic — on Amazon.co.jp the price spread on one identical bottle can be wider than the difference between the products themselves.
