Laser engravers: what 40W and Class 1 really mean

I cleared an old inkjet off my desk last month and started shopping for a desktop laser engraver to fill the space. Two weeks of spec sheets later, one pattern held across every brand: the biggest number on the listing is rarely what it appears to be.

The same machine will advertise 40W while its own module page says eight 5.5W lasers are combined into 40W. A brand's 20W model can offer a larger work area than its 40W model. Two 40W machines sit on opposite ends of the safety scale, one Class 1 and one Class 4. All three point at the same question: under what conditions does that number hold.

That 40W is several diodes summed together

xTool states it plainly on the S1 40W module page: "eight 5.5W lasers are combined into 40W". Eight diodes, optically compressed onto one spot, usually with FAC-type beam shaping. That is how diode machines reach the 40W class at all.

What this means in practice: the 40W you are buying is the sum of eight emitters, not one emitter. A single 5.5W diode and a single 40W diode are different processes with different yields and different costs. When you see a 40W badge, look for the "×5.5W" or "×10W" breakdown on the module page. If the brand does not publish it, that is itself information.

The second misread is electrical. Rated power is optical output; the wall socket sees something else.

Two results run against intuition.

First: the AtomStack A20 Pro V2 at 20W and the Creality Falcon2 at 12W land on exactly the same 2,500 W/mm². Power rose 67 percent, spot area rose 67 percent, and intensity did not move. The 20W machine covers more ground per hour because it delivers more total energy, but the ability to burn through a given layer still follows density.

Second: going from xTool's 20W module to its 40W module doubles power but lifts density by 20 percent (4,167 to 5,000), because one axis of the spot grows from 0.06 mm to 0.10 mm.

Three caveats, so this does not get treated as gospel. A rectangular spot is a geometric stand-in for a real energy distribution that is neither flat nor perfectly Gaussian. Manufacturer numbers are taken under favourable conditions, and a 0.5 mm focus error moves them. Brands also disagree on how to measure a spot at all, including whether the 1/e² edge counts. Within one brand's lineup the ratios hold up; across brands, treat them as a rough guide. It is the same lesson as our piece on diopter versus magnification in magnifier lamps: the loudest number on the listing is rarely the deciding one.

Why 17 mm and 10 mm describe the same 20W

AtomStack's spec table for the A20 Pro V2 lists 17 mm as the maximum wood thickness. xTool's official comparison page gives its 20W S1 module 10 mm of cherry in a single pass. Both are the manufacturers' own numbers, and they differ by 70 percent.

Nobody is lying. The test conditions differ:

  • Different material. Cherry and basswood are not the same density, so the same power cuts different depths.
  • Different speed and pass count. "One pass" is paired with a speed that usually does not appear in the headline. Slow the head down and the same machine goes deeper.
  • Different definition of cut. Falling apart when you push it is not the same result as a clean edge with no char.

AtomStack's own wiki is explicit for the Swift: 4 mm basswood at 7W, 7 mm at 12W, both at 100 percent power. The number holds only at full power, on a named material, in one pass.

Compare like with like, or remember that maximum cut depth is a demonstration, not a production rate. Cutting 3 mm plywood for jigs leaves all four machines with margin. Cutting 15 mm solid stock is out of reach for the machine rated at 7 mm, no matter how you tune it.

Class 1 and Class 4 differ in the enclosure

Creality's spec table for the Falcon2 40W reads "Safety Class: Class 4 (FDA)". xTool lists "Class 1 Safety" for the S1 alongside five-direction flame detection, an emergency stop, a lid-open stop and a key lock. Ortur lists "Class 1 Laser" for the H20 with a lid safety lock and nine protection layers.

Two of those are 40W-class machines on opposite sides of the classification. The laser is not what changed. The enclosure and the interlocks are. Class 1 means that under normal use a person cannot be exposed to radiation above the limit, which forces a closed cabinet, a lid that kills the beam, and no stray optical path. Class 4 is an open beam, and the machine does nothing about it; containment becomes your job.

The A20 Pro V2 is a third case. Its official spec table gives no laser class at all, just a certification list: IEC 60825, CE, FCC, RoHS, FDA, PSE, UKCA. An open frame, a magnetic filter shield and blue-blocking goggles are the containment strategy. This is not a defect: an IEC 60825 certification of the laser product and a Class 1 rating of the assembled machine under defined conditions are different claims. What it does mean is that an open-frame buyer funds the enclosure, the goggles and the floor space, and those costs are routinely left out of the comparison.

AtomStack's wiki says the same thing about the Swift in its own words: an open-frame machine, so wear laser safety goggles and use an enclosure for smoke control.

Three costs that come with any of them

Extraction. All four need it, enclosed or not. A window is the cheap route. Without one you add a purifier, and that box is frequently as bulky as the machine. Air handling around a bench is its own subject; our piece on ultrasonic cleaner power density and load ratio works the same "rated versus usable window" problem.

Test stock. Settings are not looked up, they are cut. The same basswood at a different moisture content or from a different batch wants different power and speed. Twenty identical test blanks will do more for you than a second module.

Software. LightBurn is paid; vendor apps are free and narrower. Most open-frame machines talk to LightBurn directly, while some enclosed models need the vendor app before the camera and autofocus work. Confirm your software is on the compatibility list before you buy.

Frequently asked questions

Q: Can a 20W machine cut 10 mm basswood?

It depends on speed and passes. The 10 mm in a single-pass claim is paired with a slow speed; slow it further or add a second pass and a 20W head will go deeper. What usually stops you is char and edge squareness, not power.

Q: Does an enclosure mean I can ignore the smoke?

No. Ortur's own H20 FAQ still recommends ventilation. The enclosure controls beam exposure and lid interlocks; smoke still needs a duct or a filter.

Q: Why do two 20W machines claim such different cut depths?

Material, speed, pass count and the definition of a cut are all unstandardised. AtomStack's 17 mm and xTool's 10 mm are both official, and subtracting one from the other is meaningless.

Q: Is Class 4 unusable at home?

It is usable; the containment is simply yours to build. Open-frame work means goggles, an enclosure, extraction and a household rule about who walks in while the beam is on. With small children or pets, a Class 1 cabinet is buying that rule enforcement in hardware.

The short version

Back to the three numbers at the top. 40W is eight emitters summed, and the socket sees 240–275W. What decides penetration is power over spot area, where AtomStack's 20W matches Creality's 12W. Class 1 versus Class 4 is the cabinet and the interlocks, not the wattage.

If you keep one rule: within your budget tier, find the smallest spot first, then look at the wattage.
