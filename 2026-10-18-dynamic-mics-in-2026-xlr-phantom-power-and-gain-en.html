Dynamic Mics in 2026: XLR vs USB and Phantom Power

dynamic microphoneXLRphantom power

Most people shopping for a dynamic microphone read sound comparisons first, then discover the awkward part after delivery: no signal into the laptop, a signal so quiet that cranking gain brings up the noise floor with it, or a USB microphone that turns out to need a mixer before it makes any sense. Almost none of that traces back to the microphone being mediocre. It traces back to interface, power, and gain not lining up.

Dynamic microphones follow a different logic from condensers. Condensers are sensitive and wide-ranging, but they pick up room noise and they require phantom power. Dynamics need no external power at all and barely pick up anything from across the room, at the cost of a low output level that needs more gain from the front end. Those two facts decide everything downstream: the same "dynamic or condenser" question produces completely different answers at the interface, the power, and the gain stage.

First, the thing that matters most: dynamics need no phantom power

This section drives every recommendation that follows.

Shure's own explanation of phantom power is unambiguous: a dynamic microphone such as their SM58 does not require phantom power because it does not have active electronics inside. Shure also documents that the worldwide standard for phantom power is 11 to 52 volts of DC, with 48 V being typical in studios.

More usefully, Shure's dedicated article on the topic gives the practical advice: although modern dynamic microphones are designed to accept phantom power without issues, they still advise turning phantom power off while plugging and unplugging microphones, to prevent a power surge and the pops and loud noises that could damage your speakers or headphones over time. That advice still holds. On many mixers the phantom power switch is global, so you cannot disable it for channel 2 alone.

There is also a distinction that gets missed. Phantom power is 11 to 48 V delivered equally to pins 2 and 3 of an XLR connector. Bias voltage is a separate thing: 1.5 to 9 V on a single conductor. Shure published a dedicated tech tip stating that phantom and bias are not interchangeable, and warning that a dynamic microphone should not be connected to an input that supplies bias voltage (a wireless bodypack transmitter is the example given), because the frequency response may be altered or the audio distorted. Worth remembering if you plan to feed a mic straight from a wireless transmitter.

So the order of reasoning is: check what your equipment provides, then check what the microphone needs. On the dynamic side the answer is always "nothing," which means all of the risk lives on the equipment side.

Three things need explaining, or this table will be misread.

First, the SM58's output impedance appears as two different numbers across Shure documents. The current user guide on pubs.shure.com states 300 Ω, while a 2017 SM58 datasheet states a rated impedance of 150 Ω with 300 Ω actual. These do not actually contradict each other: the 150 Ω rating is the recommended matching figure for low-impedance inputs, and 300 Ω is the measured value. But if you hit both pages while researching, you will think you misread something. Use the current guide's 300 Ω, and know why the 150 Ω figure exists.

Second, the MV7+ lists two sensitivities because it has two output paths: -55 dBV/Pa via XLR and -33 dBV/Pa via USB-C. That roughly 22 dB gap comes from the internal preamp and A/D conversion, and Shure separates the two into distinct lines in the spec table. It also explains the MV7+'s stated gain range of 0 to +36 dB: the USB path has already consumed part of the available headroom inside the preamp.

Third, the PodMic USB's 460 Ω output impedance sits apart from the more conventional low-impedance figures of the SM58 (300 Ω) and MV7+ (350 Ω). All three drive normal entry-level interfaces without trouble, but if you are feeding an older mixer, check its minimum input impedance requirement first.

Three purchasing traps that catch people

Trap one: treating "up to 192 kHz" as a quality advantage

Audio-Technica's site describes the ATR2100x-USB as having a converter with 24-bit resolution at up to 192 kHz, and the spec table lists four rates: 44.1, 48, 96 and 192 kHz.

The problem is that human hearing tops out near 20 kHz, and the bulk of speech energy sits between roughly 300 Hz and 3.4 kHz. Recording speech at 192 kHz inflates file size without changing what anyone hears. Meanwhile the PodMic USB caps at 48 kHz and the MV7+ is also 48 kHz (its 16-bit 44.1 kHz mode is marked iOS-only). Both are entirely sufficient for speech.

Sample rate should not factor into your decision at all. The only column in that table with real meaning for speech is bit depth, because the 16-bit versus 24-bit difference is an audible headroom difference. The 48 kHz versus 192 kHz difference is not.

Trap two: watch for the word "up to" in the bit depth column

Samson's Q2U spec table states a bit depth of up to 16-bit. This is the only 16-bit device in the group; the other four are 24-bit (the ATR2100x-USB says up to 24 bit).

The theoretical dynamic range difference between 16-bit and 24-bit is 8 bits, roughly 48 dB. In practice this shows up as: raise the level and the noise floor comes up with it. And raising level is precisely what dynamic microphone users do, because dynamics output a low signal and need gain. If you intend to podcast and then compress and EQ, that matters.

One related detail: on Samson's own page, the comparison table lists the Q2U's frequency response as 50 Hz – 20 kHz, while the formal specification table on the same page says 50Hz–15kHz. The page contradicts itself. I used the specification table above. If you read only the comparison table, you will get an impression of a frequency range twice as wide as the real one.

Trap three: official and regional pages disagree, and it is not you misreading

This one cost me real time while cross-checking sources, so I am writing it up to save you the same detour.

The Audio-Technica US page for the ATR2100x-USB includes two occurrences of the string Discontinued in the HTML, which looks like a discontinued model. That reading is wrong. I pulled the AT2020USB-X and AT2035 pages from the same site as controls: both contain the same two occurrences of a product__label--discontinued tag. It is an empty tag reserved by the site-wide template, and whether the word renders depends on a data field that is not set for this model. You cannot infer discontinued status from that string. Without the control check, this article would have shipped a fabricated "discontinued" claim.

The real cross-site discrepancy is the connector. Audio-Technica's Taiwan site lists the output connector as USB Type-B plus 3-pin XLRF, while the US site states USB-C/XLR. Both are official sites describing different physical interfaces. This means the revision you receive may differ by market, so read the product page rather than assuming from the model name. Samson has the same pattern: Samson's China site lists the Q2U digital output as USB Mini-B (spelled that way on their page), while the US site lists USB-C.

Work backward from your gear to pick a model

Keep this order. Confirm the equipment first, then choose the microphone.

Step one: do you have an XLR input? If you have only a laptop, the SM58 is out immediately, since it is pure XLR. The other four connect over USB.

Step two: do you need headroom for post-production? If you plan compression, EQ or denoise, the 16-bit Q2U becomes the weakest link in the chain, so stay in the 24-bit group.

Step three: do you want onboard DSP? Both the MV7+ and the PodMic USB ship with DSP, but the MV7+'s is explicitly USB-path plus MOTIV app only, and RØDE's APHEX processing is likewise described on the digital side. If you plan to live on XLR into a mixer, neither one's DSP is reachable, and you should be weighing frequency response and maximum SPL instead.

If all three are unclear: buy the model with USB-C and XLR outputs, 24-bit resolution, and a bundled tripod plus cables. Of these five, only the ATR2100x-USB lists the tripod and all three cables in its official accessories.

Selection decision tree

  • Laptop only, no interface: choose among the ATR2100x-USB, MV7+, PodMic USB and Q2U. Rule out the Q2U for 16-bit first, then pick the MV7+ or PodMic USB depending on whether you want DSP.
  • Already own an interface, want longevity: the SM58, remembering to budget for a cable and a stand.
  • Need simultaneous stage and computer capture: any model supporting both USB and XLR output works. The PodMic USB, MV7+, Q2U and ATR2100x-USB all qualify, and Samson explicitly states that both connections can be used at once.
  • Recording loud sources (instruments, drums): prioritize maximum SPL. The PodMic USB and Q2U both state 148 dB SPL. The SM58 and ATR2100x-USB do not state it officially, so research separately.

Troubleshooting: three problems you will actually hit

The three below come from mechanisms documented in official documentation, not invented "typical errors."

Problem one: the SM58 is extremely quiet into an interface, with audible noise

Symptom: the recorded waveform occupies a tiny fraction of the scale even near full scale, and pushing gain to the maximum brings the noise floor up with it.

Cause: the SM58's official sensitivity is -56.0 dBV/Pa, which is typical for a dynamic, and its output impedance is 300 Ω. If your preamp cannot provide enough gain, the mic is being asked to deliver a level it cannot. The noise is the preamp's own noise becoming audible at a low signal level.

Fix: first confirm the interface's input gain range reaches past +40 dB. Next, verify XLR pin wiring, since the guide includes the SM58 wiring diagram with pin 2 positive and pin 3 negative. If that is correct, consider a higher-sensitivity model or switch to a USB path with an internal preamp.

Problem two: the PodMic USB is much quieter on XLR than on USB

Symptom: same microphone, noticeably lower level after switching to XLR.

Cause: RØDE's split equivalent-noise figures (26 dBA analogue, 19 dBA digital) are themselves evidence that the two paths have different analogue gain. The USB path includes the Revolution Preamp; the XLR path is essentially a direct output.

Fix: this is by design, not a fault. Re-calibrate input gain in your recording software after switching, and do not carry the old setting over.

Problem three: the MV7+'s DSP disappears in XLR mode

Symptom: denoise, compression and reverb are all present over USB-C, then all disappear when you switch to XLR into a mixer.

Cause: Shure's text conditions DSP availability on "the USB-C output with the MOTIV Mix desktop app or MOTIV mobile apps." The XLR path retains only the hardware gain range of 0 to +36 dB.

Fix: if you need the DSP, stay on USB-C. If you need the mixer, work without DSP and use the mixer's own effects. On the MV7+ these are mutually exclusive, so decide before you buy.

FAQ

Q: Do dynamic microphones need phantom power?

A: No. Shure states that a dynamic has no active electronics inside and therefore does not require phantom power, while also noting that modern dynamics are designed to accept phantom power without damage. Shure still advises switching phantom power off while plugging and unplugging to avoid pops that could damage headphones or speakers. Also keep phantom power (11 to 48 V) distinct from bias voltage (1.5 to 9 V on a single conductor): Shure explicitly warns that a dynamic should not be connected to an input supplying only bias, such as a wireless bodypack transmitter.

Q: Do the ATR2100x-USB and the MV7+ need phantom power?

A: Neither does, because both are dynamic. Their power requirements serve the USB side: Audio-Technica states "Power Requirements: USB Power (5V DC)" and Shure states "USB Audio Class 2 (UAC2), powered through USB-C bus power (USB 2.0 or higher)." That USB power feeds the internal preamp, A/D conversion and headphone output, not the capsule.

Q: What does 16-bit versus 24-bit actually change, and is it worth paying more for?

A: The theoretical dynamic range difference is 8 bits, about 48 dB. It matters especially for dynamics, because a low output level forces you to raise gain, and whether the noise floor becomes audible when you do is the difference you will hear between the two. For straight streaming with no post-production, 16-bit suffices. For podcast post-production, 24-bit is steadier. Only the Samson Q2U is 16-bit among these five.

Q: Why does the PodMic USB state 20 Hz – 20 kHz while the others start at 50 Hz?

A: That is a difference in the official figures, not a typo. RØDE states 20 Hz – 20 kHz for the PodMic USB; Shure states 50 Hz – 15,000 Hz for the SM58 and 50 – 15,000 Hz for the ATR2100x-USB; the MV7+ states 50 Hz – 16,000 Hz. The difference in low-end extension affects chest resonance on male vocals, but the band that matters for speech is roughly 300 Hz to 3.4 kHz, and all five cover it. Conclusion

Choosing a dynamic microphone is simpler than choosing a condenser. No power supply needed, a defined polar pattern, and mostly indifferent to the room. What actually determines whether it works for you is what your equipment offers: which connectors, how much gain, and whether you accept design trade-offs such as DSP being available only on the USB path. Settle those three questions first and picking the microphone becomes straightforward.
