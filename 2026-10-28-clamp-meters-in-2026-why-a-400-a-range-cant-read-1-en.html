A 0.1 A Resolution Is Why a 400 A Range Can't Read 1 A

clamp metermultimeterbuying-guide

A clamp meter sells one promise: you measure current without breaking the circuit. That promise is so convenient that most people quietly assume the number on the display is the same thing a multimeter would read if you cut the wire and inserted it in series. It is not. One is the error band the manufacturer allows under stated conditions; the other is the current actually flowing.

Nothing here depends on my bench or on a seller's copy, and you can redo every calculation from the same documents.

Three words first: range, resolution, accuracy

The biggest number on a clamp meter listing is the range — 400 A, 600 A, 1000 A. That tells you the largest thing you can clamp, not the smallest thing you can trust. Three other entries decide that:

  • Range: the ceiling of the range. Fluke's spec sheet for the 376 FC says "AC Current via Jaw ... Range 999.9 A".
  • Resolution: the size of the last displayed step. The same spec sheet says "Resolution 0.1 A", meaning the smallest increment the meter can show on that range is 0.1 A.
  • Accuracy: written in the two-part form "2% ±5 digits". The first part scales with the reading; the second scales with the displayed last digit.

The second part is the one that bites. One digit is one count of the last display position, so its value in amps depends entirely on which range you are on: on the 999.9 A range, 1 digit = 0.1 A and ±5 digits = ±0.5 A; on the UNI-T UT210E's 2.000 A range, resolution is 1 mA, so the same handful of digits is worth a few milliamps. Identical notation, two orders of magnitude apart in practice. No product page will volunteer that.

The arithmetic the manufacturer already put in the fine print

These numbers need no instruments, only the published spec. Take the Fluke 376 FC jaw range (official: Range 999.9 A, Resolution 0.1 A, Accuracy 2% ±5 digits):

  • At 400 A: 2% x 400 = 8 A, plus ±0.5 A, so roughly ±8.5 A allowed — 2.1% of the reading.
  • At 10 A: 2% x 10 = 0.2 A, plus ±0.5 A, so roughly ±0.7 A allowed — 7% of the reading.
  • At 1.0 A: 2% x 1 = 0.02 A, plus ±0.5 A, so roughly ±0.52 A allowed — about 52% of the reading.

Same meter, same range; the measured current drops from 400 A to 1 A and the allowed error goes from 2.1% to 52%. The meter is not broken — the ±5 digits simply became the dominant term. Run the same arithmetic on the UNI-T UT210E's 2.000 A range (official manual: resolution 1 mA, DC ±(2%+8)): at 1.000 A the allowed error is about 0.02 + 0.008 = ±0.028 A, or 2.8%. Same 1 A, roughly 18x tighter. That gap is not brand premium, it is range resolution.

The 376 FC spec sheet also carries two footnotes that most buyers skip, and they define the conditions under which the accuracy figure is even valid:

  • Crest factor: "Crest Factor (50 Hz/60 Hz) 3 @ 500 A, 2.5 @ 600 A" with "Add 2% for C.F. > 2". If the waveform is not a sine wave, add another 2%.
  • Temperature coefficient: "Add 0.1 x specified accuracy for each degree C above 28 °C or below 18 °C". In a 38 °C switchroom — 10 °C over — that adds 1.0 times the specified accuracy, doubling the first term.

Its iFlex flexible probe goes further and publishes a position-error table: ±0.5%, ±1.0% and ±2.0% for three grades of displacement from the optimum position, with the footnote "Measurement uncertainty assumes centralized primary conductor at optimum position, no external electrical or magnetic field, and within operating temperature range". Conductor centered, no external field, inside the operating temperature — lose any one of those and the accuracy numbers above do not apply. That is the whole point of this article: a spec sheet states a conditional value, not a promise.

AC clamps, DC clamps, flexible clamps: the physics sets the ceiling

Fluke groups clamp meters into three types: current transformer (AC) clamps, Hall Effect (AC and DC) clamps, and flexible (AC) clamps. This is not marketing segmentation; it decides what you can measure at all.

A current transformer clamp has rigid ferrite jaws individually wrapped with copper coils — a transformer, with the conductor under test as the primary. Fluke's wording is blunt: "Current transformer clamp meters only respond to ac waveforms." Steady DC flux induces nothing in the secondary, so a CT clamp cannot measure DC, full stop. The Fluke 323 is this type.

A Hall Effect clamp has no copper winding; a Hall sensor sits in a gap in the core and measures flux directly, and it covers DC as well as AC up to the kilohertz range. The cost is stated on the same official page: "Because of the dc magnetic field of the earth and the possibility of other magnetic fields near the measurement site, these clamps require the reading to be 'zeroed' before taking a measurement to eliminate offsets." The UNI-T UT210E manual gives the same instruction and adds that you should keep the meter oriented the same way during measurement as it was when you zeroed it. Two independent official sources giving the same discipline means it is not one brand's quirk.

A flexible clamp (the iFlex on the 376 FC) wraps a flexible coil around the conductor and reaches 2500 A AC, at the cost of 3% ±5 digits accuracy and coarser resolution (0.1 A up to 600 A, 1 A up to 2500 A). It solves "I cannot get the jaw in there", not "I need this to be accurate".

Decide from the current you need to read

  • Just confirming that current is present (branch circuits, is the motor running): the Fluke 323 is enough. Do not pay for range you will never use.
  • Hunting standby draw, parasitic drain, tens of milliamps to a few amps: the UT210E's 2.000 A range is the only one here in the right order of magnitude, provided you stay out of distribution equipment.
  • Both AC and DC, up to 600 A: the CL800, accepting that it is discontinued, or move to a current Klein model in the same family.
  • HVAC, compressors, motor starting current: the CL445, since inrush and microamps are what it is for — but find another meter for DC current.
  • Variable frequency drives, electronic ballasts and other non-linear loads, or remote logging: the 376 FC, whose low-pass filter and inrush recording target exactly that, and which is the only one of the five to state how to read a non-sinusoidal waveform on the spec sheet.

Fix the order of your checks: AC or DC first (CT versus Hall), then whether your current falls inside the 5 to 100% band of some range (how large the digit term is), then whether the jaw fits and the CAT rating covers where you are working (whether it is safe at all). Reverse that order and you end up with a meter that has plenty of range and a reading you cannot use.

FAQ

Q: Can a clamp meter measure milliamps?

A: Most cannot, and the ones that can do it through a dedicated small range rather than a big one. Look at resolution: 0.1 A resolution on a 999.9 A range means ±5 digits is ±0.5 A, and a milliamp signal is buried in that. For milliamps you need a dedicated low range (the UT210E resolves 1 mA on 2.000 A), or you break the loop and use test leads (that is how the CL445's 600 µA DC microamp mode works), or you buy a dedicated milliamp process clamp. Do not expect a 1000 A tool to moonlight at this.

Q: Why does the DC current range need zeroing before every measurement?

A: Because a Hall Effect jaw senses DC flux, and the earth's magnetic field plus whatever else is nearby is itself a DC offset. Fluke's wording is that these clamps require the reading to be zeroed to eliminate offsets; the UNI-T manual tells you to press ZERO until the display reads zero and to keep the meter oriented the same way as when you zeroed. It is not optional — skip it and part of your first digit is the planet.

Q: Why does the reading disagree with my multimeter on a VFD or switch-mode load?

A: Check whether the meter is true-RMS first; an average-responding meter reads noticeably low on a non-sinusoidal waveform. Even with true-RMS, manufacturers publish a crest-factor penalty: the Fluke 376 FC says "Add 2% for C.F. > 2", while the UNI-T UT210E manual adds 3% as soon as the crest factor is between 1 and 2. The two official documents disagree, so take the more conservative one. Models with a VFD low-pass filter (the 376 FC, or VFC mode on the UT210E) exist for this case.

Q: Jaw size or CAT rating — which do I check first?

A: CAT, because it is a safety boundary rather than a performance figure. A jaw that will not fit is an inconvenience you can work around with a flexible probe; a CAT rating that is too low means do not touch it. The UT210E's CAT II 600 V / CAT III 300 V is the lowest here and suits outlets and equipment; the 323 sits at CAT III 600 V / CAT IV 300 V; the 376 FC's CAT III 1000 V / CAT IV 600 V covers the most.

Q: Do I need a 1000 A range for household 220 V work?

A: Usually not, and a bigger range is actively worse for household troubleshooting. Whole-house current sits well under 400 A, and a larger range brings coarser resolution, which enlarges the digit term when you measure a branch drawing a few amps. What actually matters is whether you need DC, whether you need small currents, and whether the CAT rating covers the point you are about to touch.
